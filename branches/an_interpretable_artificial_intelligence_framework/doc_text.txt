A B S T R A C T
Full-scale membrane bioreactors treating semiconductor wastewater operate under conditions laboratory studies 
rarely reproduce: fluctuating organic and ionic loads tied to production schedules, specialty solvents and 
chelating agents from photoresist stripping and CMP, and extended sludge ages far outside the short-SRT regimes 
on which most fouling theory is based. Operators must simultaneously control transmembrane pressure (TMP), 
permeate throughput, and hydraulic balance, yet no data-driven framework has identified which operating 
combinations reliably achieve this. We developed an interpretable machine-learning framework using 4593 
hourly SCADA records from a 1125 m3/h industrial MBR to define its operational basin. Sixteen algorithms 
across six families were benchmarked for TMP, permeate flow, and membrane-tank level. Extra Trees achieved 
the highest accuracy for all targets (R2 = 0.988, 0.933, 0.908) and retained first rank under blocked, chrono­
logical, and cross-train validation, including an independent parallel train. When refit daily, it held R2 = 0.871 
for TMP over a four-month out-of-time period. SHAP analysis identified sludge retention time as the dominant 
driver, with fouling worsening monotonically across the plant's extended-aeration range. Feasibility mapping 
using local-covariance resampling constrained to the plant's attainable operating manifold identified 207,238 
feasible states out of 500,000 sampled. Model-derived feasibility tracked measured attainment across 70 bins (r 
= 0.99), and operation inside recommended bands met all three targets 61–82% of the time versus 37% baseline. 
A window derived from the first seven months raised target attainment to 61.9% in the final three months (odds 
ratio 3.89). The framework yields an actionable control protocol, SRT 43.2–72.2 days, HRT 5.9–6.4 h, MLSS 
5580–6138 mg/L, F/M 0.023–0.025 day−1, aeration 4868–5892 m3/h, and is transferable to other industrial 
MBRs with site-specific retraining.
1. Introduction
The semiconductor manufacturing industry is one of the most water- 
intensive sectors globally [1–6], consuming between 1400 and 4200 L of 
ultrapure water per square centimeter of fabricated wafer [7–10]. As 
device geometries shrink and production volumes rise, securing supply 
while managing contaminated effluent has become critical, and the 
effluent itself demands multi-barrier treatment [11–13]: organic com­
pounds from photoresist processes, ionic contaminants from etching and 
cleaning, and colloidal particles from polishing must all be removed, 
with each unit process operating reliably to safeguard the ones down­
stream [9,14]. Membrane bioreactors (MBRs) have become the 
preferred solution because they combine biological degradation with 
high-efficiency membrane separation in one compact process. In a 
submerged configuration, ultrafiltration (UF) membranes sit directly in 
the activated sludge bioreactor in place of a secondary clarifier, pro­
ducing permeate largely free of suspended solids, bacteria and high- 
molecular-weight organics. The configuration gives effluent quality

independent of sludge settleability, a smaller footprint, operation at 
elevated mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS), and decoupling of solids 
retention time (SRT) from hydraulic retention time (HRT), which en­
riches slow-growing nitrifiers and enhances nutrient removal [15–19].
In semiconductor wastewater reclamation, MBRs act as the pre­
treatment barrier upstream of reverse osmosis (RO) polishing [20,21], 
and the quality of the UF permeate sets the fouling trajectory of the 
downstream RO membranes [22,23]. Fouling remains the dominant 
operational constraint, and it is more complex in submerged MBRs than 
in pressure-driven standalone UF because the membrane sits in mixed 
liquor carrying biomass, extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), sol­
uble microbial products (SMP) and colloidal organic matter [24–26]. Its 
rate is set by an interacting group of factors: MLSS and sludge rheology 
govern cake formation, the food-to-microorganism (F/M) ratio and SRT 
control EPS and SMP production, aeration supplies the shear that miti­
gates cake build-up, and HRT modulates organic loading [27–29]. 
Semiconductor wastewater imposes a fouling signature unlike munic­
ipal streams in three respects. The organic load is dominated not by 
readily biodegradable substrate but by photoresist strippers, developers, 
solvents and chelating agents, a recalcitrant fraction of which adsorbs 
preferentially onto PVDF to form a poorly reversible layer [30–34]. 
Chemical–mechanical polishing contributes silica and sub-micron 
abrasive colloids that seed a dense, low-porosity cake [35–37]. And 
loading follows the fab's batch production calendar rather than diurnal 
human activity, so organic and nitrogen pulses arrive on a schedule that 
is steep and only weakly predictable from the wastewater side. Because 
carbon is often limited for denitrification in these nitrogen-rich streams, 
glucose dosing becomes a routine control lever that couples the carbon- 
to‑nitrogen balance directly to fouling [38,39]. Together these features 
place the plant in an extended-aeration, high-SRT regime in which 
conventional short-SRT heuristics do not transfer, and they create a non- 
linear process landscape that defies simple correlation and rule-based 
optimisation.
Ubiquitous SCADA sensing and advances in machine learning (ML) 
have opened data-driven prediction and optimisation of MBR operation 
[40–42]. Artificial neural networks have predicted TMP from MLSS, flux 
and aeration rate [43]; random forest and gradient boosting have pre­
dicted fouling in pilot-scale MBRs with R2 above 0.90 [44]; long short- 
term memory networks have captured the temporal dynamics of TMP 
[45]; support vector regression and random forest have been applied to 
permeate quality [46]; and XGBoost has related operating conditions to 
fouling rates in municipal MBRs with reported R2 of 0.94–0.97 [47]. 
Three limitations hinder translation into industrial practice. Most 
studies use laboratory- or pilot-scale systems fed synthetic or municipal 
wastewater [48,49], which cannot reproduce full-scale complexity, 
where feedwater composition follows manufacturing schedules, clean­
ing residuals perturb the biology and equipment degradation introduces 
non-stationarity [50]. Most treat the model as a black box, reporting 
accuracy while offering little insight into which parameters govern 
membrane performance [51,52], and without that transparency a model 
cannot inform decisions or earn the trust of the operators who must act 
on it. And, less often acknowledged, hourly plant records are strongly 
autocorrelated, so the random train–test partitions used almost univer­
sally in this literature place near-duplicate observations on both sides of 
the split, making reported accuracies difficult to read as performance on 
genuinely unseen operating conditions.
Two gaps follow. The first concerns interpretability. Explainable 
artificial intelligence methods, principally SHAP and partial dependence 
plots, have been applied across wastewater treatment, including aera­
tion control, nutrient removal and anaerobic digestion monitoring, but 
their application to MBR fouling is limited and confined to single models 
and narrow parameter ranges. What is missing is multi-model inter­
pretability, in which SHAP is applied across algorithmic families to 
expose model-dependent bias in the importance rankings [53]; such bias 
matters because different architectures can assign conflicting impor­
tance to the same variable [54], so the recommendation depends on 
which model is consulted, a phenomenon documented for RO systems 
but not for MBRs. The second gap concerns the step from prediction to 
guidance. Existing ML-based optimisation of membrane processes either 
pairs a trained model with a search algorithm to locate point optima 
[55], or derives qualitative guidance from feature importance without 
formal optimisation [56]. The first returns single solutions without 
indicating how robust they are or how wide the feasible envelope is; the 
second gives understanding without quantitative bounds. Neither 
approach, moreover, takes account of the fact that the operating vari­
ables of a full-scale plant are strongly interdependent, so that a feasible 
region is informative only if it is mapped over the joint operating space 
the facility can actually reach. For semiconductor MBR systems, where 
biology and filtration are coupled through shared variables such as 
MLSS, SRT and aeration, a unified framework is needed that treats TMP, 
permeate flow and membrane tank level simultaneously and that sam­
ples only states the plant can actually reach.
This study introduces an interpretable artificial intelligence frame­
work for optimising MBR operation in semiconductor wastewater 
treatment, developed from 4593 hourly measurements collected over 
306 consecutive days at a full-scale industrial facility in Asan City, Re­
public of Korea. The MBR, comprising submerged PVDF ultrafiltration 
membranes integrated with a multi-stage biological process, is the 
pretreatment step ahead of RO polishing in a reclamation train rated at 
1125 m3/h. Unlike laboratory datasets acquired under controlled con­
ditions, the analysis uses SCADA records that carry the full complexity of 
industrial operation, including variable organic loading from upstream 
manufacturing, routine maintenance and recovery cleaning events, and 
progressive membrane ageing over the record.
The complete framework is set out stage by stage in Fig. 1, and the 
work makes three contributions. First, it provides the most compre­
hensive ML benchmarking reported for semiconductor-wastewater 
MBRs, sixteen algorithms across six families predicting TMP, permeate 
flow and membrane tank level simultaneously, evaluated under four 
validation designs so that genuine predictive skill is separated from the 
accuracy conferred by temporal autocorrelation. Second, it applies 
SHAP across all sixteen models and distinguishes consensus process 
conclusions from artefacts of a particular learner, a distinction unavai­
lable from the single-model analyses that dominate this literature. Third, 
it maps the feasible operating region by manifold-constrained resam­
pling rather than independent sampling of marginal ranges, and vali­
dates the resulting window against the plant's measured attainment rate 
and out of time on months the models never saw. The framework is 
directly transferable in method, though quantitative windows are site- 
specific and require retraining on facility data.
2. Models and methods
2.1. System description and data collection
The experimental data were collected from a full-scale submerged 
membrane bioreactor (MBR) system at a semiconductor wastewater 
treatment plant in Asan City, Republic of Korea. The facility utilizes an 
anoxic–oxic–anoxic–oxic (A-O-A-O) biological configuration, followed 
by submerged UF membranes, which combine biological nutrient 
removal with solid–liquid separation in a single integrated process. The 
MBR system operates at a design capacity of 1125 m3/h, treating 
semiconductor fabrication wastewater before it is sent to the down­
stream reverse osmosis system. The UF system consists of 9700 poly­
vinylidene 
fluoride 
(PVDF) 
hollow-fibre 
membrane 
modules 
manufactured by SUEZ, providing a total membrane area of 329,800 m2.

Section S1 and Table S1 of the supplementary document.
The membrane tank and biological reactor volumes are reported 
here for the first time so that the hydraulic terms can be reproduced. The 
A-O-A-O bioreactor has working volumes of 3841 m3 for the anoxic 
zones and 8604 m3 for the oxic zones, together with a 376 m3 pH

Data was collected continuously from 1 January 2025 to 3 November

fouling behaviour. Because the record covers ten consecutive months 
rather than a full annual cycle, no seasonal decomposition was 
attempted and no seasonal effect is claimed anywhere in this work; the 
long-timescale variation present in the record is treated as progressive 
operational drift, principally membrane ageing and the evolving 
cleaning history, rather than as a seasonal signal.
2.2. Process parameters and operating ranges
Seven input parameters describing biological treatment conditions 
and membrane operating state were monitored continuously: glucose 
dosing rate (Glu, a surrogate for supplemental organic loading), mixed 
liquor suspended solids (MLSS), aeration rate (Air), food-to- 
microorganism ratio (F/M), the ratio of total organic carbon to total 
nitrogen (C/N), hydraulic retention time (HRT) and sludge retention 
time (SRT). Three target variables were predicted: transmembrane 
pressure (TMP) as the primary fouling indicator, permeate flow rate 
(Flow) as the measure of productive output, and membrane tank water 
level (Level) as an indicator of hydraulic balance. The abbreviations 
defined in this paragraph, namely Glu, MLSS, Air, F/M, C/N, HRT, SRT, 
TMP, Flow and Level, are used consistently thereafter in the text, tables 
and figures, including the abstract and keywords. The carbon- 
to‑nitrogen ratio is measured as TOC/TN; the two terms denote the same 
quantity and only C/N is used hereafter. Table 1 summarises the sta­
tistical characteristics of all measured parameters.

standardised using z-score normalization to ensure that all variables 
contributed equally to model training, regardless of their original 
measurement scales. The visualization and distribution of features

drift, so the system exhibits both gradual operational movement and 
abrupt transitions across all three outputs (Fig. 2A). Standardisation 
preserves the shape of each empirical distribution, including skewness,

2.4. Data split and robustness evaluation
Because a continuously logged hourly record carries temporal 
structure by construction, validation was deliberately made more 
demanding than a single partition, and four complementary schemes 
were applied. A random 70/30 partition (3215 training and 1378 test 
samples) is the design used throughout this literature and is retained for 
comparability with it. A multi-split analysis over 20 independent 
random partitions, retraining all 16 models each time, quantifies the 
variability attributable to partitioning. Blocked partitions divide the 
record into contiguous blocks of 1, 6, 24, 72, 168, 336 and 720 h and 
assign whole blocks at random, preserving coverage of the operating 
envelope while separating adjacent hours, so that generalisation from 
one operating day to another is measured directly. And a strictly chro­
nological partition trains on the first 70% and tests on the final 30%,

2.5. Machine learning model selection and training
Sixteen regression algorithms spanning six modelling families were 
evaluated for the three targets from the seven operational parameters 
(Table 2). The portfolio was not assembled for its own sake: each family 
tests a falsifiable hypothesis about the structure of the bio­
logy–membrane relationship, so the comparison is a structured hy­
pothesis test rather than an undirected modelling exercise. Linear and 
regularised linear models test whether the relationship is adequately 
additive and monotonic, with regularisation additionally probing 
whether apparent importance is an artefact of collinearity. Support 
vector regression with an RBF kernel tests whether a smooth global non- 
linear surface suffices. K-nearest neighbours tests whether fouling 
behaviour is locally regular in operating space, that is whether similar 
conditions reliably give similar responses, which is the assumption un­
derlying any operating-window recommendation. Tree-based ensembles 
test whether threshold effects and feature interactions dominate, as 
classical fouling theory predicts, and the bagging-versus-boosting 
contrast within that family tests whether variance reduction or 
sequential bias reduction is the better response to noisy industrial sensor 
data. A multi-layer perceptron with two hidden layers tests whether 
hierarchical representations add anything beyond these. The pattern of 
which families succeed and which fail is therefore itself the evidence for 
the structure of fouling in this system, and it is reported in Section 3.1. A 
single model was carried forward for interpretation and optimisation to 
keep the framework consistent across the three targets. Formulations, 
hyperparameters and training configurations for all 16 algorithms are 
given in Section S3 and Table S2; all work was performed in Python 3.10 
using NumPy, pandas, scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM and SHAP.
2.6. Interpretability analysis through multi-model SHAP
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied across 
all 16 models and 3 targets, giving 48 interpretability profiles. This 
multi-model framework reveals whether different algorithms agree on 
feature importance and exposes model-dependent bias that single-model 
analyses cannot detect. TreeSHAP was used for the nine tree-based 
models and KernelSHAP for the seven non-tree models, and beeswarm 
plots, mean absolute SHAP values and dependence plots were generated 
for each model–target combination. Because SHAP quantifies each fea­
ture's contribution to the model output, it reflects association within the 
training distribution rather than verified physical causation; the mech­
anistic explanations offered in Section 3.2 are physically plausible and 
literature-consistent interpretations, not demonstrated mechanisms.
2.7. Optimal operating condition identification
A multi-objective feasibility framework was used to identify oper­
ating regions satisfying the target criteria for all three outputs simulta­
neously. Because the seven operating variables are interdependent in 
practice, candidates were generated so as to respect that joint structure; 
the rationale and a comparison against independent sampling of the 
marginal ranges are given in Supplementary Section S5. Feasibility was 
mapped by local-covariance kernel resampling constrained to the plant's 
real joint operating manifold: a seed hour is drawn uniformly from the 
4593 logged observations, the local covariance of its 30 nearest neigh­
bours in standardised input space is formed, the seed is perturbed by a 
Gaussian increment scaled by a bandwidth factor of 0.6 applied to that 
covariance, and the candidate is rejected if it leaves the observed mar­
ginal range of any input or if its nearest-neighbour distance to the real 
record exceeds the 99th percentile of real-to-real nearest-neighbour 
distance (0.539 in standardised units). The resulting 500,000 candidates 
have a median nearest-neighbour distance of 0.121 against 0.119 be­
tween real hours, so the sample explores continuously around the 
manifold without leaving it. A candidate was classed feasible only where 
all three predictions fell within their target ranges, with predictive un­
certainty taken from the dispersion of the 100 individual trees. Windows 
were then defined from the conditional feasibility rate, the probability 
that all three targets are met given the value of an input, rather than 
from the density of feasible points, which reflects how often the plant 
visited a region and not how well it performed there. Each input was 
divided into deciles and the recommended band taken as the widest 
contiguous run whose measured attainment remains within 5 percent­
age points of the maximum, subject to a minimum support of 250 logged 
hours; four of the seven bands are unchanged by this criterion, indi­
cating genuinely sharp optima. Kernel density estimation (KDE), a non- 
parametric method that reconstructs a continuous density by summing a 
smooth kernel centred on each observation and so avoids the bin- 
placement dependence of histograms, was used with a Gaussian kernel 
and Scott's-rule bandwidth. Detailed algorithms are given in Supple­
mentary Section S5.
2.8. Evaluation metrics
Prediction accuracy was quantified using three complementary 
metrics: the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean squared error 
(RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE). Their mathematical formula­
tions are provided in Supplementary Section S4.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. ML model prediction performance
3.1.1. Output prediction performance
The predictive performance of all 16 models was evaluated for TMP, 
permeate flow and membrane tank water level. Fig. 4 shows the R2 
heatmap and predicted-versus-observed scatter across model–target 
combinations, revealing a clear hierarchy in both model capability and 
target predictability: TMP was predicted most accurately, followed by 
permeate flow and then water level. Effluent quality was deliberately 
excluded as a target. Permeate TOC is measured by an online analyser 
whose 0.03–1000 ppb range leaves it near its detection limit for most of 
the record, because the UF membrane retains essentially all suspended 
and high-molecular-weight material, so the signal is dominated by 
analyser noise. More fundamentally, UF permeate quality in a sub­
merged MBR is governed by membrane integrity, a step-change phe­
nomenon associated with fibre breakage rather than a continuous 
function of the variables considered here, making it an integrity- 
monitoring problem rather than a regression target. The three targets 
selected instead are those the operator can trade against one another 
hourly.

For TMP, tree-based ensembles were clearly superior. Extra Trees 
achieved the highest accuracy (R2 = 0.988, RMSE = 0.010 bar, MAE =
0.005 bar), followed by Bagging, Random Forest and LightGBM (0.978 
each) and XGBoost (0.977) (Fig. 4, Fig. S1), all five above R2 = 0.97. The 
strong predictability of TMP is physically interpretable: in submerged 
systems TMP is governed by fouling resistance, which develops pro­
gressively with MLSS, aeration scouring intensity and SRT-dependent 
sludge characteristics, and all of these were supplied to the models 
directly [28,29,35,36]. TMP is also measured as suction pressure on the 
permeate side, giving a more stable signal and a higher signal-to-noise 
ratio than pressurised configurations [35].
Permeate flow prediction followed a similar model ranking but with 
moderately lower R2 values. Extra Trees again led (R2 = 0.933, RMSE =
0.066 m3/min, MAE = 0.052 m3/min), with Bagging (R2 = 0.910), 
Random Forest (R2 = 0.909), XGBoost (R2 = 0.906), and Hist Gradient 
Boosting (R2 = 0.899) completing the top five (Fig. 4 and Fig. S2). The 
reduced accuracy compared to TMP is consistent with the mechanics of 
MBR operation. Permeate flow is influenced not only by the state of 
membrane fouling but also by operator-controlled setpoints, backwash 
cycling, and transient hydraulic disturbances that may not be fully 
captured by the hourly-averaged input parameters [23–26]. The influ­
ence of intermittent aeration scouring on instantaneous permeate flow 
introduces variability that high-fidelity models cannot fully account for 
based solely on the available biological and operational features.
Membrane tank water level proved the most challenging target, the 
best model reaching R2 = 0.908 (Extra Trees, RMSE 0.341%, MAE 
0.227% under the random partition), with the leading ensembles be­
tween 0.82 and 0.91 and all other families substantially below. The 
ordering across the three targets has a direct physical reading. TMP is 
the most predictable because it integrates fouling resistance over hours 
to days, and the variables governing that accumulation, principally SRT 
and MLSS, are slowly varying and were measured directly. Permeate 
flow is intermediate because it is only partly a membrane property, 
being set also by throughput demand, backwash cycling and short hy­
draulic transients that hourly averaging cannot resolve [57–59]. Water 
level is least predictable because it is a fast hydraulic state governed by 
the instantaneous balance of influent, permeate extraction, internal 
recirculation, sludge wasting and level-controlled pump logic, several of 
which act over minutes and none of which is a model input. The gradient 
therefore ranks the targets by how far their dynamics are captured by

better managed by the fast levers identified in Section 3.3. Level accu­
racy remains operationally useful even so, with MAE below 0.23% 
against a 2% control window.
3.1.2. Model comparison, consistency and independent validation
A large and systematic gap separated linear from non-linear models 
across all three targets (Table 3). Linear and Ridge Regression reached 
R2 of only 0.499 for TMP, 0.469 for flow and 0.195 for water level, the 
regularised variants slightly worse as aggressive shrinkage removed 
small but informative coefficients, while the best ensembles exceeded 
0.90 on all three. This is the structured hypothesis test anticipated in 
Section 2.5: the failure of the additive family together with the success of 
recursive partitioning is evidence that the biology–membrane relation­
ship is governed by thresholds and interactions rather than proportional 
effects, consistent with fouling theory in which cake resistance accel­
erates beyond critical MLSS, aeration shows diminishing returns above a 
critical scouring velocity, and F/M and SRT interact non-additively 
[25,29,36,38]. Within the ensemble family the bagging variants 
consistently outperformed sequential boosting, although XGBoost 
(0.977) and LightGBM (0.978) nearly matched them for TMP. AdaBoost 
was markedly worse on every target (0.781, 0.655, 0.308), consistent 
with the sensitivity of adaptive reweighting to the sensor artefacts of 
industrial SCADA data, and the advantage of Extra Trees over Random 
Forest is attributable to the additional randomisation of split thresholds. 
Among non-tree non-linear models KNN performed strongly (0.969, 
0.894, 0.813), indicating that membrane response is locally regular in 
operating space, the property that makes an operating-window recom­
mendation meaningful at all. Only the Decision Tree for water level 
showed substantial overfitting (0.890 against 0.650), and the 20-split 
analysis (Table S3, Figs. S4–S6) confirmed Extra Trees as the best 
model on mean R2 and the lowest RMSE and MAE for all three targets.
Transfer to a physically distinct membrane system was assessed on 
an independent set of 4593 hourly observations from a parallel B stream 
in the same facility, operating under different influent conditions and 
with an independent fouling history (Fig. S7). Extra Trees, applied 
without retraining, achieved R2 = 0.996, 0.980 and 0.972 for TMP, flow 
and water level (Table S4, Fig. 5A–C), with near-zero mean errors and 
error standard deviations of 0.014 bar, 0.052 m3/min and 0.306% 
(Fig. 5D). The TMP error distribution is slightly right-skewed (skewness 
1.025), indicating occasional underestimation under extreme fouling, 
which is the conservative direction operationally because it prompts 
earlier rather than later cleaning. The B stream runs over the same 
calendar period and shares the same influent, so this establishes transfer 
across membrane trains rather than across time; temporal transfer is 
addressed next.
Taken together, the primary test-set performance, the 20-split 
robustness analysis and the parallel-stream validation identify Extra 
Trees as the best model for all three targets, and it was carried forward 
for the interpretability analysis in Section 3.2 and the optimisation in 
Section 3.3. Using one model for all three targets keeps the operational 
framework and the feature-importance interpretation consistent across 
the three performance indicators.
3.1.3. Robustness of the model selection to the validation design
Model selection for a full-scale plant record should not rest on a 
single partition, so the choice of Extra Trees was tested under four in­
dependent designs: the conventional random 70/30 partition, a blocked 
design in which contiguous blocks are allocated as whole units so that 
coverage of the operating envelope is preserved while adjacent hours are 
separated, a strictly chronological partition, and the independent par­
allel B stream. The outcome is a strong one. Extra Trees ranks first in 
eight of the nine model–design–target combinations and first for every 
target under both the random and the 24-h blocked designs (Table S10), 
and accuracy under 24-h blocking remains high for a full-scale industrial 
system, with R2 = 0.830 for TMP corresponding to an RMSE of 0.036 bar 
against an operating range of 0.43 bar and a mean absolute error of 
16.3% of the interquartile range of the measured series. Because the 
record is a continuously logged hourly series and therefore autocorre­
lated by construction, blocked and random values are reported side by 
side throughout; the dependence structure, the full per-model results 
and the divergence between R2 and absolute error as blocks lengthen are 
given in Supplementary Section S6 and Fig. S17. More important than 
absolute accuracy is the stability of the conclusions: SRT remains the 
first-ranked driver of TMP and of water level under random, blocked 24- 
h, blocked 168-h and chronological training alike, so the process con­
clusions are properties of the plant record rather than of any particular 
partition.
The chronological partition deserves separate comment, because as 
usually implemented it describes a deployment scenario no plant would 
adopt: it fits the model once on the first seven months and applies it 
frozen to the following four, although every new hour reaches the his­
torian within the hour and refitting Extra Trees on the entire record 
takes about a second. Refitting at a fixed interval on all data logged so 
far, with every prediction still made strictly ahead of the data used to 
produce it, resolves the result. Out-of-time accuracy improves mono­
tonically as the interval shortens, and at a daily refit R2 on the withheld 
final 30% reaches 0.871 for TMP, 0.580 for water level and 0.574 for 
permeate flow, with TMP predicted to 0.008 bar, an 80% reduction

3.2. Process interpretability analysis
3.2.1. Cross-model feature importance consensus and divergence
SHAP was applied across all 16 models and three targets, giving 48 
feature-importance profiles (Figure 6A1–3; TreeSHAP–KernelSHAP 
comparison in Fig. S8A). The central result is a consensus one. Despite 
spanning six algorithmic families, every one of the 16 models ranked 
SRT first for TMP (consistency score 1.00) and 15 of 16 ranked it first for 
water level (mean rank 1.07 ± 0.25), the MLP being the sole exception; 
for permeate flow the picture is less unanimous, HRT ranking first in 7 of 
16 models (2.28 ± 1.39) against 4.67 ± 1.89 for SRT. This convergence 
establishes that the dominance of SRT is a property of the data rather 
than of any single learner's inductive bias, a distinction the single-model 
analyses that constitute most of this literature cannot draw, and it sur­
vives the validation design, SRT remaining first-ranked for TMP and 
water level under blocked 24 h, blocked 168 h and chronological 
training even where predictive accuracy degrades. Flow rankings 
beyond the top two features are less stable and should be treated as 
indicative. Where the models diverge, the divergence is systematic and 
traceable to the explainer: linear models ranked C/N third or higher for 
TMP while every tree-based model ranked it last, and aeration ranked 
first for flow under KernelSHAP but fourth under TreeSHAP. Kernel­
SHAP approximates Shapley values using random feature coalitions and 
so conflates correlated features [60], whereas TreeSHAP exploits the 
exact tree structure and is more reliable under correlation [61]; since Air 
and HRT co-vary here, higher aeration being deployed during higher- 
loading periods that also compress HRT, KernelSHAP absorbs part of 
the HRT contribution into aeration. All mechanistic discussion below 
therefore rests on the Extra Trees attributions.
3.2.2. Mechanistic interpretation of the dominant drivers
The dominance of SRT is the most physically interpretable finding of 
the analysis. SRT governs the composition, morphology and activity of 
the sludge community, and at this facility it ranged from 43.3 to 108.3 
days, placing the entire operating range within the extended-aeration 
regime. Across the Extra Trees dependence and beeswarm plots 
(Fig. 6B1, Fig. S9), SHAP values are positive at 45–75 days, pass through 
a wide-scatter transition at 80–90 days where the effect becomes 
strongly conditional on co-occurring variables, and fall sharply beyond 
90 days, reaching −0.3 to −2.0 at 100–108 days. That increasing SRT 
worsens TMP appears to contradict the expectation that longer sludge 
age reduces fouling through endogenous respiration and hydrolysis of 
EPS and SMP, but that expectation was established below 30–40 days. 
Here even the shortest SRT is already deep in the extended-aeration 
domain, so the EPS and SMP benefit is largely exhausted, and what 
changes between 45 and 108 days is accumulated biomass: less sludge is 
wasted, MLSS rises, and the accompanying increases in viscosity, cake 
thickness and cake compressibility outweigh any residual SMP reduc­
tion [61,62]. At very long SRT the floc becomes dominated by inert and 
recalcitrant particulates that deposit as a dense, poorly reversible layer, 
a mechanism with particular force in semiconductor wastewater where 
non-biodegradable fractions from photoresist and cleaning chemistries 
adsorb preferentially onto PVDF. The same logic explains the other 
targets: permeate flow is suppressed at the longest SRT as viscosity raises 
internal hydraulic resistance [63], and water level rises monotonically 
with SRT. The 80–90 day transition band is where operating choices 
decide the outcome, and the HRT × SRT interaction (0.056; Fig. S8B1) 
quantifies the coupling: at short HRT the biomass-driven penalty of 
rising SRT is partly offset, whereas at long HRT the two compound.
3.2.3. Hydraulic and aeration effects
HRT ranked second for TMP (2.33 ± 0.47) and first for permeate 
flow among the leading tree-based models, reflecting its dual role as a 
control on organic loading and on hydraulic capacity. Short HRT of 6–7 
h yields positive SHAP for TMP and the contribution declines steadily 
above 7 h, reaching −1.3 to −2.2 at 10–11 h. The conventional expec­
tation is the opposite, since short HRT should leave more residual SMP, 
but three features of full-scale operation reconcile it: short HRT corre­
sponds to high throughput, which at this fab coincides with active 
production and a more dilute wastewater; long HRT periods above 9 h 
co-occur with reduced wasting and therefore with biomass accumula­
tion; and during high-throughput operation the control system main­
tains more frequent backwash and relaxation cycles. For permeate flow 
the dependence is non-monotonic with an optimum near 6.5–7.5 h

high aeration is deployed reactively during high loading or active

3.2.4. Secondary biological drivers
MLSS ranked third for TMP (3.33 ± 1.35) and for water level (3.87 
± 1.15), with an inverted-U dependence for TMP: near-zero contribution 
at 2000–3000 mg/L, a maximum near 6000–6500 mg/L, then a sharp 
fall to −1.5 at 7000–7500 mg/L as cake resistance rises disproportion­
ately, consistent with the near-quadratic scaling documented for PVDF 
hollow-fibre systems [65]; the MLSS × SRT interaction (0.042) confirms 
amplification at shorter SRT where EPS-rich sludge raises cake 
compressibility. F/M ranked fourth for TMP, with a maximum near 
0.030–0.035 day−1 and a decline at the highest values where SMP 
overproduction accelerates gel-layer fouling, and a U-shape for water 
level with a steep rise above 0.05 day−1. Glucose ranked fifth for TMP 
with a modest, largely indirect effect mediated through MLSS and F/M. 
C/N ranked seventh for TMP among the tree-based models, decreasing 
monotonically from positive contributions at 5–7 to −0.3 at 14–16, since 
carbon-rich, nitrogen-limited conditions favour carbohydrate-rich EPS 
and filamentous growth with poor filterability [66]; the divergence 
between model families for this variable is attributable to its correlation 
with MLSS and F/M and is a further illustration of the value of the cross- 
model comparison.
3.2.5. Interaction structure and methodological implications
Comparing TreeSHAP across the nine tree-based models with Ker­
nelSHAP across the seven non-tree models reveals consistent directional 
bias (Fig. S8A). For TMP the two agree on SRT dominance (0.530 against 
0.458) and on HRT second (0.143), MLSS diverging most (0.144 against 
0.109). For permeate flow the divergence is largest for aeration, first 
under KernelSHAP (0.243) and fourth under TreeSHAP (0.150) behind 
HRT (0.318), C/N (0.230) and F/M (0.160) [67]. For water level the two 
agree. The systematic pattern is that KernelSHAP overstates the leverage 
of readily adjustable short-term variables at the expense of the slower 
biological variables that dominate outcomes, reinforcing the use of 
TreeSHAP for operational guidance. The interaction matrices (Fig. S8) 
place the strongest couplings at HRT × SRT for TMP (0.056) and flow 
(0.0510), MLSS × SRT for TMP (0.042) and Air × SRT for water level 
(0.1318). These non-additive dependencies are the quantitative basis for 
treating the seven inputs jointly in Section 3.3 rather than optimising

3.3. Optimal operating conditions for semiconductor MBR wastewater 
treatment
3.3.1. From feature importance to viable operating space
The interpretability analysis in Section 3.2 established, with 
consensus across 16 algorithms, which variables govern each perfor­
mance indicator and in which direction they act. SRT is the dominant 
driver of TMP, worsening fouling monotonically across the facility's 
extended-aeration range through biomass accumulation, elevated vis­
cosity and cake compaction; HRT sets the hydraulic ceiling on permeate 
flow; and aeration acts on TMP through a non-monotonic optimum with 
diminishing returns. What SHAP cannot supply is the set of input con­
ditions under which all three targets are met at once, nor whether the 
direction that improves one target conflicts with the constraints of 
another. Section 3.3 addresses that gap, and it is the step that converts 
model insight into operating guidance.
Feasibility was mapped with the Extra Trees model using the 
manifold-constrained resampling scheme of Section 2.7, under three 
simultaneous constraints: TMP between −0.09 and −0.03 bar, permeate 
flow between 1.5 and 2.2 m3/min and water level between 65.0 and 
67.0% (Figs. S12–S13). Of 500,000 candidate states on the operating 
manifold, 207,238 (41.4%) satisfied all three. Ensemble dispersion 
across the 100 trees gives 95% prediction intervals of ±0.008 bar for 
TMP, ±0.17 m3/min for flow and ± 0.71% for water level, so operation 
toward the interior of the envelope rather than at its edge is advisable for 
the two hydraulic targets in particular. Fig. 7 sets out the feasible range 
of each input against the attainment rate the plant actually achieved 
there. Two features of the scheme give the map its practical value. Every 
sampled state lies on the manifold the plant has explored, the median 
nearest-neighbour distance from sample to record being 0.121 stand­
ardised units against 0.119 between real hours, so every prediction is an 
interpolation among conditions the facility has genuinely operated and 
every recommendation is physically attainable. And windows are 
graded by the conditional feasibility rate rather than by the number of 
feasible points, which identifies where the plant performs best rather 
than where it has spent the most time, and is what allows each recom­
mendation in Table 4 to be stated with the frequency at which the plant 
has actually met its targets there.
The feasible region is not a rectangular box but a structured, corre­
lated set. Fig. 8 maps the probability of meeting all three targets across 
every pair of operating variables, and in most pairs the high-probability 
region forms a narrow ridge rather than a rectangle, most clearly for 
MLSS against F/M, which run in strict opposition, and for C/N against 
HRT, where favourable operation follows a rising band. Treating the 
seven recommendations as independently adjustable set-points would 
therefore overstate the freedom available, and Fig. 8 is the reference for 
which combinations can be held simultaneously. In 20 of the 21 pairs the 
intersection of the two recommended bands lies in a high-probability 
region, with feasibility rates of 60–100% against a plant-wide rate of 
about 40%, so the per-variable recommendations compose without 
conflict. The single exception is a genuinely non-additive result that per- 
variable analysis cannot reach. Taken individually, SRT of 43.2–72.2 
days and HRT of 5.875–6.381 h are the two strongest levers in the study, 
with measured attainment of 77.0% and 82.4%; taken together they are 
not. Only 35 logged hours combine both, and on those hours attainment 
falls to 34.3%, below the 37.1% plant-wide baseline. The reason is 
visible in the record: when SRT is held at 43.2–72.2 days the plant runs 
at an HRT of 6.2–7.5 h, whereas the 5.9–6.4 h band occurs at sludge ages 
of 72–108 days. Short HRT is a high-throughput condition, and sus­
taining high throughput at low sludge age demands a wasting rate the 
plant does not operate, so forcing both moves the system into a regime it 
has essentially never occupied. SRT and HRT should therefore not be set 
independently at their marginal optima: sludge age is the variable to fix 
first, being the slower of the two and the dominant driver of TMP, and 
HRT chosen conditionally on it, which is the structure of the joint rule 
validated in Section 3.3.4.

a high-organic-loading sequence.

least stable of the seven over time, as reported in the out-of-time analysis 
below, and C/N guidance is therefore presented as a period-specific 
observation rather than as a transferable set-point.
3.3.3. Validation of the operating window on a withheld period
The most demanding test available for guidance derived from his­
torical data is whether guidance obtained from one period improves 
outcomes in a later period that played no part in obtaining it. This in­
volves no model prediction and is therefore independent of the accuracy 
assessment of Section 3.1. The record was divided chronologically, the 
window re-derived using only the first 70%, and its effect evaluated on 
the final 30% (Fig. 11B). The window from the first seven months places 
its principal condition on SRT (≤84.5 days) with HRT secondary, and 
identifies 415 h in the withheld period, of which 61.9% met all three 
targets against a baseline of 39.3% (odds ratio 3.89, Fisher exact p = 3 ×
10−29). Lever by lever, the principal biological controls carry the result 
individually as well: MLSS transferred with a gain of 30.9 percentage 
points, SRT with 22.7 and F/M with 15.7 (Fig. S18). Sludge age, biomass 
concentration and organic loading, the three variables an operator can 
act on over hours to days, are therefore the levers for which the guidance 
is most firmly established, and the ones the protocol places at its centre.

clearest case is F/M, whose importance for water level rises by 87% from

3.4. Comparison and perspectives
3.4.1. Predictive performance
Most prior studies focus on municipal or domestic wastewater 
treatment, often conducted at pilot or lab scales, and typically provide 
single-target predictions, such as TMP or fouling indicators (e.g., 
permeability, water flux, Table S8). For instance, [68] employed 
multilayer perceptron (MLP) and long short-term memory (LSTM) net­
works for TMP prediction in a large pilot-scale anaerobic MBR (AnMBR) 
treating municipal wastewater, achieving R2 > 0.91. Similarly, [69]
used random forest for fouling behaviour prediction in AnMBRs with R2 
= 0.906, while [70] applied adaptive neuro-fuzzy inference systems for 
water flux in osmotic MBRs, yielding R2 between 0.9755 and 0.9861.
In contrast, the present study applies an Extra Trees regressor at full 
scale in a semiconductor wastewater plant, a domain of wide loading 
swings and stringent effluent requirements, and predicts three interde­
pendent outputs simultaneously. Under a random partition, comparable 
to the designs used in the studies above, R2 is 0.988 for TMP, 0.933 for 
permeate flow and 0.908 for water level; under a 24-h blocked partition 
that removes temporal near-neighbours, 0.830, 0.788 and 0.564. Both 
are reported because comparison with published single-target accu­
racies is meaningful only on a like-for-like partitioning basis, and the 
studies cited use random partitions of similarly autocorrelated records. 
Rather than claiming the highest accuracy reported to date, the appro­
priate conclusion is that a single ensemble learner achieves competitive 
accuracy on three coupled targets at full industrial scale without the 
computational overhead of recurrent architectures, and that the multi- 
target formulation addresses the operational reality that hydraulic dy­
namics and fouling cannot be optimised independently.
3.4.2. Optimization approach
The largest gap in the comparative literature lies not in predictive 
accuracy but in interpretability, which most comparison studies do not 
report at all. The framework used here combines kernel density esti­
mation, feasible-region analysis, SHAP beeswarm and dependence 
analysis and Pearson correlation, applied in two analytical contexts: the 
full operational record (Section 3.2) and the feasible operating region 
(Section 3.3). This dual-context analysis is a methodological contribu­
tion in itself, because the importance ranking of several variables shifts 
when the analysis is confined to the viable regime, and those shifts have 
implications for real-time control that a single-context analysis cannot 
reveal. The 87% increase in F/M importance for water level within the 
feasible region is the clearest example, identifying a near-real-time 
control lever that full-dataset SHAP leaves hidden behind the domi­
nant slow variables.
Perhaps the sharpest distinction between the present study and the 
comparative literature lies in the meaning and scope of the term “opti­
mization.” In most comparative studies that report any optimization 
activity, the term refers to model optimization (Table S8) includes 
hyperparameter tuning architecture search, or training strategy selec­
tion. Only reference [71] applies optimization to operating conditions 
rather than model parameters, using a genetic algorithm to identify the 
input combination that minimizes a single fouling objective.
The present study is, to our knowledge, the first in the MBR machine- 
learning literature to perform multi-objective operating-condition 
optimisation within a manifold-constrained feasible input space, satis­
fying three membrane performance constraints simultaneously and 
mapping the joint structure of the 207,238 states that achieve this. This 
differs qualitatively from a single-objective genetic-algorithm search. 
Rather than returning one optimal point, the analysis produces a con­
ditional probability surface over the attainable operating space, which 
allows the identification of regions where the system is most resilient to 
routine perturbation, distinct from the technically feasible boundary. 
The methodological point that emerges, and that we suggest is trans­
ferable well beyond this plant, is that a feasible region must be sampled 
on the joint operating manifold rather than across independent marginal 
ranges, and that operating recommendations must be graded by condi­
tional attainment rate rather than by the density of feasible points.
The distinction between the feasible range and the high-attainment 
core is a practically significant contribution. For SRT the feasible en­
velope extends beyond 95 days while measured attainment above 87 
days is below 20%; for HRT the feasible envelope reaches 7.65 h while 
attainment above 7.95 h is zero; for F/M the feasible envelope extends to 
0.037 day−1 while attainment above 0.037 day−1 is 15%. An operator 
relying on the feasible boundary alone, that is on the output of a con­
ventional constraint-satisfaction analysis, would conclude that these 
upper ranges are acceptable, when in practice they carry a high proba­
bility of violating one or more targets.
The rate-graded operating targets in Table 4, derived from 207,238 
evaluated feasible states and cross-checked against 4593 logged hours, 
therefore provide a level of evidential support that is not available from 
a feasibility boundary alone.
3.4.3. Perspectives and future strategies
The structural contributions of this study, namely full-scale semi­
conductor wastewater application, simultaneous three-output predic­
tion, benchmarking of 16 algorithms across six families under four 
validation designs, dual-context multi-model interpretability, and 
manifold-constrained optimisation validated against measured attain­
ment and out of time, advance practice in three areas. Models trained on 
historical operating data from a complex industrial MBR can support 
operational guidance without additional laboratory characterisation, 
provided they are evaluated as response surfaces over the attainable 
operating space and validated against measured outcomes rather than 
on forecasting skill alone. Interpretability conducted within the feasible 
region rather than across the full historical envelope reveals control- 
relevant shifts in feature importance, a template applicable to any 
multi-output industrial process model. And the study delivers an 
evidence-graded operating protocol in which each recommendation 
carries an explicit measured attainment rate and an explicit statement of 
whether it transferred to a held-out period.
The boundaries of the framework should be stated alongside its 
contributions. The operating window has been verified three indepen­
dent ways: against the plant's measured attainment across the operating 
space, against a three-month period withheld entirely from the analysis, 
and against an independent parallel membrane train. This is a stronger 
evidential base than a single-period, single-split analysis, but it remains 
a verification on historical data rather than a controlled trial, and part of

4. Conclusion
This study set out to determine whether the operating record of a 
full-scale semiconductor-wastewater membrane bioreactor contains 
enough information to tell operators where to run the plant, and con­
cludes that it does, provided the models are evaluated and used in the 
right way. Three findings support this. First, sludge retention time is the 
foundational determinant of membrane behaviour here, and the evi­
dence is unusually robust: all 16 algorithms rank it first for trans­
membrane pressure, the ranking survives validation designs under 
which predictive accuracy itself degrades, and measured attainment 
falls monotonically from 77% at 43–72 days to below 20% beyond 87 
days. Because the facility operates entirely within the extended-aeration 
range, the mechanism is biomass accumulation, elevated viscosity and 
cake compaction rather than the EPS and SMP dynamics of conventional 
short-sludge-age systems, so the expectation that longer sludge age 
mitigates fouling is inverted. Second, the question a plant record an­
swers well is not what the plant will do next but which attainable 
operating states satisfy its performance constraints; mapped on the real 
joint operating manifold and graded by conditional attainment rate, the 
resulting surface reproduces measured attainment across 70 operating 
bins with a correlation of 0.99 and yields a window substantially nar­
rower than the technically feasible envelope. Third, that window carries 
value beyond the period it came from: bands fixed on the first seven 
months raised simultaneous attainment from 39.3% to 61.9% over the 
following three, and refitted daily on the accumulated record the model 
predicts TMP to 0.008 bar with R2 = 0.871 four months beyond its 
original training data, so temporal generalisation is governed by the 
refresh schedule rather than by any intrinsic limit of the model. The 
wider implication concerns how such models should be judged. Hourly 
industrial records are strongly autocorrelated, and accuracy measured 
on random partitions describes interpolation between neighbouring 
hours rather than generalisation to new operating conditions, whereas 
feature attributions prove stable across partitioning schemes. Inter­
pretability, not headline accuracy, is therefore the property that survives 
scrutiny and should carry the operational weight, and the most useful 
output is not a predictor but a graded map of the operating space in 
which every recommendation carries the frequency with which the 
plant has actually achieved its targets there. The transition from reactive 
to proactive membrane management depends less on further gains in 
predictive accuracy than on validating guidance against outcomes the 
plant can verify and, ultimately, on prospective trials that test whether 
following such guidance causes the improvement these data associate 
with it.
