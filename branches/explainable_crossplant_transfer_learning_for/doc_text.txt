A B S T R A C T
Accurate membrane fouling prediction is essential for feedforward control and low-energy operation of mem­
brane bioreactors (MBRs). However, reliable model development for data-limited plants is constrained by sparse 
measurements of mechanistically informative extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and soluble microbial 
products (SMP). Here, we developed an explainable cross-plant transfer learning framework for transmembrane 
pressure (TMP) prediction in data-limited pilot-scale MBRs. Long short-term memory (LSTM) and extreme 
gradient boosting (XGBoost) base models were pretrained on three data-richer source MBRs treating domestic 
wastewater, and fine-tuned with limited data from a domestic target plant. The base models predicted source- 
domain TMP (R2 = 0.87 and 0.86). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) identified EPS carbohydrates 
(EPSc), EPS proteins (EPSp), and SMP proteins (SMPp) as the dominant fouling-related drivers. Direct transfer to 
the target plant yielded limited accuracy (R2 = 0.39–0.41), suggesting that the base models did not fully capture 
its fouling behavior. In contrast, fine-tuning improved prediction accuracy, and the fine-tuned LSTM (LSTM-FT) 
reached R2 = 0.89 at a 40% fine-tuning ratio. SHAP and leave-one-feature-out (LOFO) analyses revealed that 
EPSc remained the critical transferable fouling signal, whereas EPSp became increasingly important after fine- 
tuning. This shift points to an EPSp-related fouling pathway linked to the plant's Fe-enriched influent. The 
framework was validated on a pilot-scale MBR treating industrial wastewater, where LSTM-FT achieved R2 ≈0.9, 
with SMPp emerging as a critical feature. Collectively, the framework preserves shared EPS-centered fouling 
information while adaptively recalibrating plant-specific drivers, offering a practical route for mechanism- 
informed fouling prediction in data-limited MBRs.
1. Introduction
Urbanization and increasing wastewater reuse requirements 
continue to push wastewater treatment toward intensified processes, 
which need to deliver stable, high-quality effluent within constrained 
infrastructure footprints [1]. Membrane bioreactors (MBRs), which 
integrate biological treatment with membrane separation, have there­
fore been widely adopted for domestic and industrial applications, due 
to their superior effluent quality and compact configuration. However, 
membrane fouling remains the principal obstacle to fully realizing these 
advantages [2]. Under constant-flux operation, fouling progressively 
increases filtration resistance and is reflected by a rise in transmembrane 
pressure (TMP), which in turn increases aeration and pumping demand, 
intensifies chemical cleaning requirements, accelerates membrane 
aging, and raises lifecycle costs [3]. Consequently, MBRs typically 
consume more energy than conventional activated sludge processes 
(0.4–1.6 vs. 0.3–0.8 kWh m−3) [4]. Accurate prediction of fouling evo­
lution/TMP is therefore essential for enabling feedforward fouling 
control and low-energy MBR operation [5,6]. Nevertheless, despite 
substantial progress in fouling characterization and mitigation,

To support proactive management, both mechanistic and data- 
driven approaches have been explored to predict fouling evolution. 
Mechanistic and hybrid biological–physical models can enhance inter­
pretability, but they are often challenging to parameterize and recali­
brate in real plants [7]. In parallel, machine learning (ML) and deep 
learning (DL) models have emerged as attractive alternatives, because 
they can capture nonlinear relationships among operational, 
water-quality, and biomass-related variables [7–9]. Within this land­
scape, tree-based ML models (e.g., gradient-boosting families such as 
Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)) are attractive for tabular process 
data, whereas recurrent DL architectures (e.g., long short-term memory 
(LSTM) networks) are particularly suitable for fouling prediction, 
because membrane fouling is an intrinsically cumulative and 
path-dependent process, driven by temporal changes in sludge proper­
ties and hydrodynamic conditions [10–12].
Notably, comprehensive and rationally designed inputs for model 
development are key to achieving accurate AI modeling [13]. However, 
many existing TMP prediction models, particularly those developed at 
pilot-scale/full-scale MBRs, are built primarily on routinely monitored 
“conventional” variables, such as mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS), 
mixed liquor volatile suspended solids (MLVSS), hydraulic retention 
time (HRT), and sludge retention time (SRT). Thus, the current ap­
proaches for tracking fouling dynamics in MBRs are often insufficient 
[14,15]. In contrast, mechanistically informative biochemical de­
scriptors such as extracellular polymeric substances (EPS) and soluble 
microbial products (SMP) are often missing or severely under-sampled 
at pilot-scale/full-scale MBRs [15,16]. This is a critical limitation, 
because extensive evidence shows that EPS and SMP, particularly their 
proteinaceous and polysaccharide fractions, strongly influence fouling 
propensity 
by

cake

compressibility, 
and 
pore-blocking behavior [17], although humic and fulvic acids can also 
contribute to membrane fouling [18–20]. Collectively, this evidence 
suggests that explicitly introducing EPS and SMP as model inputs could 
be both scientifically justified and a promising route to improve TMP 
prediction and interpretability in MBR plants.
Even when these key biochemical indicators are available, pilot-/ 
full-scale MBR datasets are often still insufficient to support the devel­
opment of robust site-specific fouling prediction models. In practice, 
measurements of EPS and SMP fractions are usually sparse and irregular, 
because they rely on labor-intensive laboratory analyses rather than 
online sensing. Together with missing values, sensor errors, and outliers 
in routine operational records and key biochemical datasets, these 
limitations make it difficult for a single data-limited MBR plant to 
independently train a high-quality predictive model [21,22]. Most 
existing ML-based MBR fouling prediction models have also been 
developed and validated using relatively long or dense plant-specific 
historical records, leaving their cross-plant generalizability under 
data-limited conditions insufficiently established [7,8,23]. Neverthe­
less, different MBR plants often share comparable fouling mechanisms, 
including cake-layer accumulation, pore blocking, and the contribution 
of microbial products to membrane fouling. This suggests that trans­
ferable models for fouling formation/prediction may exist across 
MBR plants. From this perspective, transfer learning provides a prom­
ising strategy to address both data scarcity and data silos in MBR fouling 
prediction [24,25]. Specifically, a base model can be pretrained on 
multi-source data from different MBR plants to learn generalized re­
lationships among operational conditions, EPS/SMP signatures, and 
TMP evolution. Then it could be fine-tuned using a small amount of data 
from the target MBR to learn the plant-specific fouling drivers.
However, cross-plant transfer learning for MBR fouling prediction is 
not a simple model-porting problem. First, source and target MBRs may 
differ substantially in influent composition, sludge physicochemical 
properties, EPS/SMP profiles, metal–organic interactions, and operating 
conditions. Moreover, even after fine-tuning, limited target plant data 
may still be insufficient for the model to fully capture newly dominant 
fouling drivers that were weak or absent in the source plants. Further­
more, the success of transfer learning should not be evaluated by pre­
dictive accuracy alone, but also by how and why physically meaningful 
fouling signals are retained, discarded, or reweighted during transfer. 
Explainable analyses, such as SHAP and leave-one-feature-out (LOFO) 
analysis, are needed to determine how key EPS/SMP-related indicators 
function as transferable fouling drivers or plant-specific recalibrated 
signals. Prior applications of these methods to membrane-fouling pre­
diction have focused mainly on model-level feature ranking [26]. 
However, SHAP attributions are inherently model-dependent and lack a 
ground truth for validation, so high predictive accuracy alone does not 
ensure that the highlighted features reliably reflect the true fouling 
behavior [27]. Accordingly, independent physicochemical character­
ization, including excitation–emission matrix (EEM), liquid chroma­
tography–organic carbon detection (LC-OCD), and foulant analysis, is 
needed to evaluate the plausibility of these attribution shifts and support 
their mechanistic interpretation.
To address these gaps, this study proposes an explainable transfer 
learning strategy for fouling prediction in pilot-scale MBRs under data- 
limited conditions. Specifically, (1) base models (based on LSTM and 
XGBoost) were first pretrained on multi-source datasets collected from 
three pilot-scale MBR plants (source plants) treating domestic waste­
water, covering diverse operating conditions, routine biochemical 
characteristics, and key components in EPS and SMP. We then (2) fine- 
tuned the base models using the target plant dataset to establish a 
fouling prediction model for the data-limited target plant (treating do­
mestic wastewater), while quantifying the relationship between fine- 
tuning ratio and predictive performance and examining how LSTM 
and XGBoost architectures influenced transfer performance after fine- 
tuning. Next, (3) the transfer behavior and transferable fouling-related 
information were examined using explainable analyses, including 
SHAP and LOFO analyses, together with physicochemical character­
ization by EEM, LC-OCD, and foulant analysis. These analyses were used 
to clarify how key fouling-related indicators, particularly EPS and SMP, 
were retained or reweighted during pretraining and fine-tuning, and 
provided mechanistic support for these changes. Finally, (4) the 
transfer learning framework was further examined in a pilot-scale MBR 
treating industrial wastewater to test whether cross-plant transfer 
learning remains feasible under a more distinct wastewater background. 
Collectively, this study aims to provide a practical and explainable route 
for establishing fouling prediction models in data-limited MBR facilities 
through cross-plant transfer learning.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data collection
A pilot-scale MBR plant treating domestic wastewater in Singapore 
was selected as the target plant. The system received Fe-enriched 
influent, with an Fe dosage of approximately 10–30 mg/L. It had a 
treatment capacity of 24 m3/d and a process configuration comprising 
pretreatment, a five-pass step-feed bioreactor with anaerobic/oxic 
zonation, and a membrane tank (Fig. 1(a)). The TMP profile of the target 
plant during the monitoring period is shown in Fig. 2(a), the membrane 
module is shown in Fig. 2(b), and the detailed process and operating 
parameters are summarized in Text S1. The target plant dataset covered 
the period from September 2021 to June 2022. Owing to the twice- 
weekly measurement frequency of EPS and SMP, model development 
was based only on dates with complete matched measurements for all 
input variables and TMP, yielding 71 valid temporally matched records. 
This data-limited target plant setting reflected the practical monitoring 
constraints of pilot-scale MBR operation and provided a realistic sce­
nario for evaluating the proposed cross-plant transfer learning 
framework.

2.2. Physicochemical analytical methods
HRT, SRT, and FLUX were recorded as routine operational parame­
ters. TCOD and SCOD were determined by the APHA 5220D closed 
reflux colorimetric method, and MLSS and MLVSS were measured ac­
cording to Standard Methods [28]. SMP was defined as the dissolved 
fraction of the supernatant after 0.45 μm membrane filtration and EPS 
was extracted from the residual biomass using the heat method [29]. 
Proteins and polysaccharides in SMP and EPS, including SMPp, SMPc, 
EPSp, and EPSc, were quantified using the Modified Lowry Protein 
Assay and Dubois Method, respectively [30]. Liquid chromatography­
–organic carbon detection (LC-OCD) was employed to characterize the 
dissolved organic matter, including biopolymers with molecular weight 
(MW) greater than 20 kDa and low-molecular-weight (LMW) com­
pounds with MW less than 1000 Da, such as humics, building blocks, 
LMW acids, and LMW neutrals. The particle size distribution (PSD) of 
mixed liquor was analyzed using a laser diffraction particle analyzer. 
Capillary suction time (CST) was measured using a CST apparatus [31]. 
Total organic carbon (TOC) was measured using a TOC analyzer 
(TOC-VCSH, Shimadzu, Japan). Excitation–emission matrix (EEM) 
spectra were measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer [32]. 
Total iron was measured using inductively coupled plasma optical 
emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES).

2.3. Data preprocessing and feature selection
To ensure reliable model development, the raw datasets were first 
preprocessed by outlier treatment and missing-value imputation (Text 
S2). To place variables on comparable scales and prevent variables with 
larger numerical ranges from dominating model training, z-score stan­
dardization was applied during preprocessing (Text S3). The scaler was 
fitted using the training data only, and model outputs were inverse- 
transformed to the original scale after training.
The final prediction target of this study was next-day TMP, whereas 
dTMP was used only as an intermediate training target to alleviate non- 
stationarity and improve output comparability across plants [33,34]. 
Compared with raw TMP values, dTMP reduced inter-plant differences 
in TMP level and produced distributions more centered around zero 
(Fig. S3). Predicted dTMP values were then added to the current-day 
TMP to reconstruct next-day TMP for model evaluation and 
presentation.
To assess source–target distribution discrepancy and overlap rele­
vant to transfer learning, joint principal component analysis (PCA) was 
performed using the input variables together with dTMP. The input and 
output blocks were standardized separately and assigned equal overall 
weights (Text S4).
The sample-size-to-feature ratio (SFR) is an important indicator of 
whether model complexity is appropriate for a given dataset size, and in 
small-sample modeling an SFR greater than 10 is generally considered 
desirable [35]. Therefore, Pearson correlation analysis was used as a 
coarse pre-screening step (Text S5). Because the final prediction target 
remained next-day TMP, this initial screening was performed against 
TMP rather than dTMP. It considered both feature–target association 
and inter-feature collinearity, but served only as a pragmatic 
dimensionality-control procedure rather than a definitive assessment of 
nonlinear predictive relevance.
2.4. Construction of base models based on source plants
Given the time-series nature of the data, the source datasets were 
split within each plant according to chronological order rather than 
randomly, thereby reducing the risk of information leakage between 
temporally adjacent samples. Considering the limited sample sizes and 
short input window, LSTM was selected as a compact recurrent archi­
tecture for sequence learning [36]. Transformer- and TCN-based models 
may offer advantages when larger MBR datasets or longer temporal 
contexts are available, and systematic benchmarking under such con­
ditions remains a direction for future work [37,38]. Accordingly, two 
base models were developed: LSTM-base for recurrent sequence learning 
and XGBoost-base for tree-based learning (Fig. 1(c)). LSTM-base used 
standardized feature sequences with a sequence length of 3 time steps, 
whereas XGBoost-base used the same three-step historical information 
encoded as lagged features. This design ensured that the two base 
models used the same amount of historical information, allowing a fair 
comparison of their predictive performance. Additional implementation 
details are provided in Text S6.
Hyperparameters for both base models were optimized using the 
training split of the source plant datasets. Because the data were time- 
ordered, five-fold time-series cross-validation was combined with 
Optuna-based hyperparameter search to prevent leakage from future 
samples and improve the robustness of model selection. After optimi­
zation, both base models were trained on the same training split, and the 
trained models were saved to initialize the transfer learning models. The 
hyperparameter search spaces, final settings, and training details are 
provided in Table S2–S3 and Text S7.
2.5. Construction of the baseline model for the target plant
Before establishing transfer learning models, a baseline model was 
developed using only the target plant dataset, and its performance was 
compared with that of the transfer learning models. To ensure compa­
rability, the target plant dataset was partitioned in chronological order, 
with the first 50% used for model training and the final 50% reserved for 
testing. Under this split, only a limited amount of target plant data was 
available for model training. With such a small training set, a standalone 
target-trained LSTM baseline would be more prone to unstable fitting 
and overfitting, making the resulting comparison less reliable [39]. An 
independent XGBoost model was therefore adopted as the baseline. It 
was trained on the training portion of the target plant dataset using the 
same feature set as the transfer learning models, and the scaler for the 
target variable was fitted independently using the same training portion. 
Detailed settings are listed in Table S4.
2.6. Construction of transfer learning models
For transfer learning, the target plant dataset was partitioned chro­
nologically using the same 50:50 split: the first 50% was used as the fine- 
tuning pool, whereas the final 50% was kept unseen during fine-tuning 
and reserved as an independent testing set (Fig. 1(d)), thereby reducing 
the risk of temporal information leakage. The fine-tuning ratio (FT) was 
defined as the proportion of the total target plant dataset used for fine- 
tuning, and was examined at 0, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%. The 
exact numbers of target plant records in the fine-tuning pool, testing set, 
and each FT setting are summarized in Table S5. The resulting transfer 
models were denoted as LSTM-FT and XGBoost-FT. At FT = 0, no target 
plant samples were used for fine-tuning, and the two models therefore 
corresponded to direct transfer without target plant adaptation.
For each nonzero FT setting, multiple fine-tuning segments of the 
corresponding length were sampled from the fine-tuning pool to reduce 
dependence on any single local history (Text S8). Each segment-level 
experiment was repeated with five random seeds, and performance 
was reported as the mean ± standard deviation across all fine-tuning 
segments and repeated runs. LSTM-FT and XGBoost-FT were fine- 
tuned using different update strategies, with implementation details 
and hyperparameter settings provided in Table S6–S7 and Text S9.
All experiments were implemented in Python 3.10.18 using relevant 
third-party libraries. The deep-learning models were built and trained in 
PyTorch 2.7.0. The computing environment included an Intel Core i7- 
14700KF processor (28 logical cores), 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA 
GeForce RTX 5060 Ti GPU.
2.7. Evaluation of model performance
Model performance was evaluated using R2, RMSE, and MAE (details 
in Text S10). Model interpretability was examined using LOFO and 
SHAP analyses (Fig. 1(e)), as detailed in Text S11 and Text S12, 
respectively.
2.8. Model adaptability under cross-scenario conditions
Additional validation was conducted using data from a pilot-scale 
MBR treating mixed petrochemical and pharmaceutical industrial 
wastewater in Singapore. Detailed process configuration and operating 
parameters are provided in Table S8. This validation was performed 
using LSTM-FT under two fine-tuning conditions, FT = 0 and FT = 40%. 
To maintain consistency with the main analysis, the pretrained model, 
chronological fine-tuning/testing logic, data preprocessing procedure, 
and model settings were kept the same. Accordingly, this test was 
intended to assess whether the established workflow could remain 
effective under industrial wastewater conditions, without additional 
model optimization.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Source–target distribution and PCA analysis
The distributions of the measured variables across the source and 
target plants are presented in Fig. 2(c), and the corresponding average 
values are summarized in Fig. 2(d). At the level of core operating vari­
ables, the target plant remained within or close to the ranges of the 
source plants (HRT of 5.5 h versus 5.0–5.5 h, SRT of 6.8 d versus 
5.0–7.0 d, and FLUX of 25 LMH versus 25–44 LMH). Across the source 
plants, the measured variables covered relatively broad ranges of 
biomass-related and fouling-related properties (e.g., MLSS of 
900–9730 mg/L, MLVSS of 639–7671 mg/L, EPSp of 25.0–658.8 mg/L, 
EPSc of 0.0–117.1 mg/L, SMPc of 0.8–33.3 mg/L, and SMPp of 
2.7–44.5 mg/L). By contrast, the target plant showed comparatively 
narrower distributions for the biomass-related and fouling-related var­
iables (e.g., MLSS of 3580–7340 mg/L, MLVSS of 3340–5920 mg/L, 
EPSp of 73.5–469.9 mg/L, EPSc of 17.1–81.3 mg/L, SMPc of 2.6 to 
20.1 mg/L, and SMPp of 4.8–10.8 mg/L).
These distributional relationships were further examined by PCA 
(Fig. 3(a)). The first two principal components explained 74.0% of the 
total variance, indicating that the major variability in the dataset was 
captured in a two-dimensional projection. The source plant datasets 
showed substantial overlap, indicating broadly similar fouling-related 
variable patterns across the source plants. By contrast, the target plant 
dataset formed a more compact cluster with partial separation from the 
source plants, indicating a measurable distribution shift. Importantly, a 
clear overlap region remained between the target and source datasets. 
This overlap provided a reasonable basis for developing base models 
using the source plant datasets and supported the feasibility of subse­
quent transfer learning.

3.2. Construction and evaluation of base models
3.2.1. Correlation-based feature selection
Feature–target (X–y) Pearson correlation analysis was further con­
ducted, and the ranking results are shown in Fig. S4. Overall, FLUX, 
EPSc, HRT, and SMPp showed relatively strong correlations with TMP, 
with | rx,y
⃒⃒values of 0.471, 0.462, 0.440, and 0.347, respectively. In 
contrast, SCOD, SMPc, and TCOD showed weak correlations with TMP, 
with | rx,y
⃒⃒values all below 0.1, and were therefore excluded from the 
reduced input set used for subsequent modeling. The remaining vari­
ables were kept for the subsequent collinearity screening step.
Feature–feature (X–X) Pearson correlation analysis was then per­
formed to further control inter-feature collinearity (Fig. 3(b)). HRT was 
strongly negatively correlated with FLUX (rxj,xk = -0.86), likely reflect­
ing their operational coupling: HRT is inversely related to treatment 
flow rate, whereas FLUX represents the permeate throughput per unit 
membrane area [8]. MLSS and MLVSS showed a very strong positive 
correlation (rxj,xk = 0.96), consistent with MLVSS representing the vol­
atile biomass fraction of MLSS [40]. Within these two collinear feature 
pairs, FLUX and MLVSS had higher | rx,y | values with TMP and were 
therefore retained. Accordingly, FLUX, SRT, MLVSS, EPSc, EPSp, and 
SMPp were retained as a reduced input set for subsequent base model 
development.
3.2.2. Performance evaluation of base models
The prediction scatter plots for both base models show that most 
training and testing points were distributed close to the line of equality, 
indicating good agreement between the predicted and observed TMP 
values in the source plants. XGBoost-base achieved an R2 of 0.86, an 
RMSE of 3.01 kPa, and an MAE of 1.94 kPa on the test set (Fig. 3(c)). 
LSTM-base showed slightly better performance, with an R2 of 0.87, an 
RMSE of 2.86 kPa, and an MAE of 1.68 kPa (Fig. 3(d)). Larger prediction 
deviations were observed at higher TMP values in both models, sug­
gesting that TMP prediction became more difficult under more severe 
fouling conditions, possibly due to intensified fouling-layer compression 
and nonlinear fouling resistance at elevated TMP levels [41]. Overall, 
both models captured the main TMP variations in the source datasets, 
with LSTM-base showing a small but consistent advantage over 
XGBoost-base.
3.2.3. SHAP interpretation of base models and physicochemical 
characterization
SHAP attribution patterns were further analyzed for both base 
models. ChatGPT-5.4 was used only as an auxiliary tool to support the 
summarization of these attribution patterns, while the final interpreta­
tion was performed by the authors based on the SHAP plots and corre­
sponding feature-attribution results (Fig. 6(d)). For LSTM-base, the 
SHAP summary plots showed that EPSc, MLVSS, EPSp, and SMPp made 
the largest contributions (Fig. 6(a); Fig. S5), whereas FLUX and SRT 
were comparatively less influential. XGBoost-base showed a much more 
concentrated attribution pattern, with FLUX dominating the SHAP 
attribution and contributing more than the other input variables (Fig. S6 
(a); Fig. S6(b)). These results indicate that the two base models achieved 
similar predictive performance but relied on different predictive struc­
tures during pretraining on the source plants. The broader dependence 
of LSTM-base on EPSc, MLVSS, EPSp, and SMPp may partly reflect the 
greater suitability of sequence-learning architectures for variables with 
stronger temporal continuity and gradual variation [42]. In contrast, the 
FLUX-dominated attribution pattern in XGBoost-base was consistent 
with the X–y correlation analysis and may partly reflect the tendency of 
tree-based models to rely heavily on a dominant predictive signal [43,
44]. These results were consistent with the subsequent transfer learning 
results, which showed that XGBoost-FT achieved only limited 
improvement compared with LSTM-FT (discussed in Section 3.3).
Given that LSTM-base achieved strong source-domain predictive 
performance and showed much more promise in transfer learning (dis­
cussed in Section 3.3), the physicochemical characteristics of biomass in 
the source plants were further examined to determine whether its 
learned attribution pattern was consistent with real fouling mechanisms. 
EPS and SMP were emphasized, because they were identified as 
important features in the SHAP analysis. Specifically, EPS is more 
closely associated with sludge flocs and membrane-associated fouling 
[45], whereas SMP mainly represents soluble organics [46].
Concretely, the three source plants showed a consistent EPS-related 
signature, including a shared aromatic protein-like peak in the EPS 
EEM spectra (Ex/Em ≈220–225/325–355 nm; Fig. 4(a)–(c)), and sludge 
particle-size distributions dominated by particles of 20–40 μm (Fig. 4
(d)). These features generally suggested a readily depositable sludge 
structure that could promote cake-layer formation in MBRs [47]. 
Moreover, the protein fraction represented by EPSp likely comprises 
aromatic protein-like components, which could be associated with 
sludge floc aggregation and the initial adhesion of foulants to the 
membrane surface [48]. By comparison, EPSc may be more closely 
related to the gel-forming and resistance-enhancing effects of the poly­
saccharide matrix, thereby further increasing the hydraulic resistance of 
the fouling layer [49]. The relatively high attribution of MLVSS 
(revealed by SHAP) was also physically reasonable, as MLVSS represents 
the biomass fraction that carries EPS and contributes to sludge deposi­
tion on the membrane surface [3].
Additionally, SMP from the three source plants exhibited similar 
EEM spectral features, with a shared aromatic protein-like fluorescence 
peak (Ex/Em ≈220–230/330–350 nm; Fig. S7(a)–(c)). These aromatic 
protein-like SMP components may contribute to fouling development 
through membrane-surface adsorption or retention within the devel­
oping fouling layer, particularly during the early stage of membrane 
fouling [50,51]. This interpretation was further supported by the 
LC-OCD results (Fig. 4(e) and (f); Table S9), which showed that SMP in 
the three source plants was dominated by hydrophilic DOC, with humics 
and biopolymers as the major fractions. For each LC-OCD fraction, 
rejection was calculated as the percentage decrease from its concen­
tration in SMP to that in the corresponding effluent, relative to its 
concentration in SMP. On this basis, biopolymers showed the highest 
rejection across the three source plants, ranging from 90.0 to 95.3%. 
These results suggest that SMPp may mainly reflect the aromatic 
protein-like component within the biopolymer pool that was preferen­
tially retained by the membrane and could therefore participate directly 
in organic membrane fouling [52].
Overall, the shared physicochemical characteristics of source plants 
provide clear mechanism-informed support for the EPS- and SMP- 
related attribution pattern learned by LSTM-base. This indicates that 
the learned structure was not merely statistical but grounded in real 
fouling-related properties. In turn, this supports the use of LSTM-base as 
a reliable pretrained model for subsequent transfer learning.
3.3. Transfer learning performance and interpretation
3.3.1. Effect of fine-tuning ratio on transfer learning performance
The effect of fine-tuning ratio (FT) on transfer learning performance 
was examined to determine the amount of target-plant data required for 
effective adaptation. At FT = 0, representing direct transfer without 
target plant adaptation, the pretrained LSTM and XGBoost models 
showed similarly low R2 values of 0.41 and 0.39, respectively, indicating 
limited predictive power under zero-shot transfer. Together with the 
PCA results showing a measurable source–target distribution shift, this 
finding suggests that direct transfer alone was insufficient for reliable 
TMP prediction in the target plant [53].

FT = 40% (Fig. 5(b) and (c)). At FT = 50%, the improvement relative to

3.3.2. Performance comparison of baseline model and transfer learning 
models
To compare predictive performance on the target plant, the baseline 
model (specifically trained based on target plant datasets) together with 
LSTM-FT and XGBoost-FT was evaluated (Fig. 5(d)).
The testing samples were mainly distributed within a TMP range of 
approximately 19–21.5 kPa, with fewer samples at lower TMP values of 
around 15.5–18 kPa. Within the main TMP range, LSTM-FT showed the 
tightest clustering around the line of equality, indicating the best 
agreement between predicted and observed TMP values. The baseline 
model showed moderate deviations, whereas XGBoost-FT exhibited the 
largest scatter and the most evident departure from the line of equality. 
These patterns suggest that transfer learning improved target plant 
prediction when implemented with LSTM, whereas XGBoost-FT did not 
provide a performance advantage under the present setting.
The quantitative metrics showed the same trend. Relative to the 
baseline model, LSTM-FT reduced RMSE from 0.63 to 0.50 kPa and MAE 
from 0.45 to 0.33 kPa, corresponding to decreases of 20.6% and 26.7%, 
respectively, while increasing R2 from 0.82 to 0.89. By contrast, 
XGBoost-FT increased RMSE to 0.96 kPa and MAE to 0.81 kPa, and 
reduced R2 to 0.58. Taken together, these results indicate that LSTM-FT 
achieved the best predictive performance under limited target plant 
data, outperforming both the baseline model and XGBoost-FT. There­
fore, the following sections focus on LSTM-FT to further interpret the 
observed transfer behavior using LOFO and SHAP analyses.
3.3.3. LOFO and SHAP interpretation of feature contributions during 
transfer
LOFO and SHAP analyses were used to identify the variables that 
supported transfer performance in LSTM-FT. LOFO analysis was first 
conducted to evaluate the effect of each input variable on LSTM-FT 
performance at FT = 40% (Fig. 5(e)). The reference model was the 
LSTM-FT model trained with all input variables, which achieved an MAE 
of 0.33 kPa. Each variable was then removed individually, and the 
resulting MAE was compared with that of the reference model using 
paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Table S10). Removing 
EPSc caused the largest performance loss, increasing MAE to 0.37 kPa 
(p ≤0.001), indicating that EPSc was the most important variable for 
maintaining LSTM-FT performance. Removing EPSp or SRT also 
increased MAE to 0.35 kPa (both p ≤0.001), whereas removing FLUX 
caused a smaller but still significant increase (p ≤0.01). Overall, the 
LOFO results confirmed that EPS-related variables, especially EPSc and 
EPSp, were important for LSTM-FT performance, while SRT and FLUX 
also showed smaller but statistically significant effects.
SHAP analysis was further used to examine how the feature- 
attribution pattern evolved with increasing FT. Under zero-shot trans­
fer (FT = 0), EPSc was the top-ranked feature, contributing the largest 
share of the total attribution, well above MLVSS and EPSp (Fig. 6(b); 
Fig. S8(a)). This suggests that, under zero-shot transfer, the transferred 
model retained a strongly EPSc-centered attribution pattern, reflecting 
limited recalibration before fine-tuning. From FT = 10% to 30%, the 
attribution pattern became more distributed, with increased attribution 
to EPSp and more comparable contributions from EPSc, SRT, and MLVSS 
(Fig. S9–S10). At FT = 40%, EPSc and EPSp were the two top-ranked 
features and together accounted for more than half of the total attri­
bution (Fig. 6(c); Fig. S8(b)), indicating a more balanced EPS-centered 
attribution structure after fine-tuning. The feature ranking and

3.3.4. Why did transfer learning enable accurate membrane fouling 
prediction in the target plant?
Because SHAP and LOFO identify model-relevant variables rather 
than causal mechanisms, the attribution changes were interpreted 
against independent physicochemical evidence from the target plant. At 
FT = 0, the attribution pattern remained dominated by EPSc, and the 
model retained moderate predictive performance (R2 = 0.41), indicating 
that the EPSc-centered predictive structure learned by the base model 
was partially applicable to the target plant. The relatively high EPSc 
concentration in the target plant (49.6 mg/L) further suggests that 
polysaccharide-related fouling remained important. Elevated poly­
saccharide content can enhance water retention and reduce sludge 
dewaterability, thereby promoting the formation of a highly hydrated, 
compressible, and high-resistance fouling layer. This interpretation was 
consistent with the relatively high CST (49.45 s) and specific CST 
(4.36 s/(g/L)) observed in the target plant (Fig. 7(a)). Taken together, 
these results suggest that EPSc represented a shared membrane-fouling 
matrix characteristic across plants that remained stably informative 
during transfer prediction.
Performance improved when FT increased to 40% (R2 = 0.89), and 
the most notable change in the SHAP results was the rise of EPSp to the 
second most important feature. This shift suggests that fine-tuning 
increased model sensitivity to protein-related EPS characteristics in 
the target plant. The EPS EEM spectrum of the target plant showed a 
dominant aromatic protein-like peak (Fig. 7(b)), consistent with the 
protein-related EPS signature observed in the source plants. Of note, it 
was hypothesized that the increased importance of EPSp (aromatic 
proteins) could be due to the Fe-enriched influent in MBRs [54]. To 
further support this interpretation, two Fe-dosing stages in the target 
plant were compared (Table S11). Specifically, upstream Fe was dosed 
into the influent mixing tank before pretreatment at target dosages of 9 
and 26 mg/L, allowing Fe accumulation and EPS compositional re­
sponses in the MBR system to be examined. As the Fe dosage increased, 
the sludge-associated Fe content increased from 45 to 101 mg/g-MLVSS, 
while EPSp increased from 47.96 to 62.30 mg/g-MLVSS. These obser­
vations suggest that Fe-enriched conditions were associated with a more 
protein-enriched EPS composition, which may explain the increased 
attribution of EPSp after fine-tuning. Higher EPS protein content is 
generally associated with stronger hydrophobicity and greater floc ag­
gregation, which may favor membrane-surface adhesion and cake-layer 
densification [46,55]. This interpretation was consistent with the rela­
tively high viscosity and specific viscosity of the target sludge 
(50.03 mPa s and 9.46 mPa s/(g/L), respectively; Fig. 7(d)). Taken 
together, the increased SHAP attribution of EPSp suggests that 
fine-tuning recalibrated the model toward the protein-related EPS 
characteristics that were more pronounced under the Fe-enriched con­
ditions of the target plant.
The two-step CIP results further supported the association between 
the Fe-related EPS response and the composition of the retained fouling 
layer in the target plant. During the citric acid (CA) cleaning stage, total 
Fe increased sharply from 0.62 to 43.02 mg/L (Fig. 7(e)), indicating the 
release of substantial Fe-bearing components from the fouling layer. 
During the subsequent NaClO cleaning stage, TOC increased from 5.19 
to 43.22 mg/L (Fig. 7(f)), while total Fe also increased from 1.85 to 
11.16 mg/L, suggesting the release of oxidizable organic matter together 
with Fe-bearing components. Together with the lower SCOD than TCOD 
in the target plant (93 vs. 271 mg/L), these results suggest that the 
dominant fouling layer was not formed by dissolved organics alone, but 
was more closely associated with a sludge-bound organic matrix

Furthermore, the decline in SMPp attribution after fine-tuning sug­

which was less representative of the dominant fouling layer.
3.3.5. Cross-scenario adaptability to an MBR treating industrial wastewater
Because the preceding analysis focused mainly on the domestic MBR 
scenario, additional validation was conducted using a pilot-scale MBR 
treating mixed petrochemical and pharmaceutical industrial waste­
water. This case provided a preliminary test of the established transfer 
learning workflow under a more distinct wastewater background. 
Compared with the domestic target plant, the industrial MBR presented 
a more challenging transfer scenario, with a more complex influent 
composition, higher organic loading, and disturbance-driven biomass 
dynamics. The TMP profile of the industrial MBR showed a clear long- 
term trend together with multiple abrupt perturbations associated 
with real operational events (Fig. 8(a)), reflecting complex membrane- 
fouling behavior under practical engineering conditions.
The industrial MBR differed substantially from the source plants in 
wastewater and sludge properties, with consistently higher TCOD, 
SCOD, MLSS, and MLVSS (Fig. 8(b)). Importantly, despite these back­
ground differences, EPSc (43.6 ± 20.2 mg/L) remained close to the 
source plant range (32.3–43.5 mg/L), suggesting that EPSc-related 
fouling information was still shared across scenarios. EPSp 
(256.8 ± 126.7 mg/L) was also of the same order of magnitude as in the 
source plants (184–229 mg/L), and SMPc (6.9 ± 5.0 mg/L) fell within 
the source plant range (5.2–11.5 mg/L). These overlaps suggest that part 
of the fouling-related information learned from the source plants could 
still be transferred to the industrial MBR, even under a substantially 
different wastewater background.
Under zero-shot transfer (FT = 0), the base model achieved only 
moderate performance in the industrial MBR (R2 = 0.46; Fig. 8(c)), 
suggesting that the fouling-related information learned from the source 
plants was partially applicable but insufficient to fully capture the TMP 
dynamics in the industrial MBR. Fine-tuning with industrial MBR data 
improved prediction performance. This validation used the same chro­
nological fine-tuning/testing logic, pretrained model, preprocessing 
procedure, and model settings as the main target plant analysis. At 
FT = 40%, R2 increased to 0.89, while MAE and RMSE decreased by 
68.6% and 54.7%, respectively, relative to FT = 0. This improvement 
indicates that the established workflow could be adapted to the indus­
trial wastewater background without additional model optimization.
SHAP analysis further showed that EPSc retained the highest attri­
bution after fine-tuning, whereas SMPp increased to the second-highest 
attribution level (Fig. 8(d)). This pattern differed from that observed in 
the domestic target MBR plant, where fine-tuning mainly increased the 
attribution of EPSp. Thus, the two target cases shared stable EPSc- 
related fouling information, but differed in the feature requiring 
target-specific recalibration: EPSp in the Fe-enriched domestic MBR and 
SMPp in the industrial MBR. The increased attribution of SMPp suggests 
that fine-tuning recalibrated the model toward a more prominent solu­
ble proteinaceous signal under the industrial wastewater background.
The increased attribution of SMPp after fine-tuning was mechanis­
tically plausible in the industrial MBR. SMPp was higher in the industrial 
MBR (27.6 ± 17.4 mg/L) than in the source plants (10.8–13.8 mg/L), 
indicating a stronger background of dissolved proteinaceous organics. 
Previous characterization of this industrial MBR showed pronounced 
SMP protein peaks during operational disturbances, together with de­
creases in MLSS and MLVSS, consistent with biomass loss, cell decay, 
and proteinaceous SMP release into the mixed liquor [56]. Moreover,

Overall, this additional validation suggests that the proposed transfer 
learning workflow remained applicable to the industrial MBR, not 
because fouling mechanisms were identical across scenarios, but 
because the model retained transferable EPSc-related fouling informa­

dominant target-specific signal. In the domestic target MBR plant, this 
recalibration was reflected by the increased attribution of EPSp under 
Fe-enriched conditions, whereas in the industrial MBR, it was reflected 
by the increased attribution of SMPp associated with disturbance-driven 
soluble protein release. These results further support the ability of the 
transfer learning framework to preserve transferable fouling-related 
information while adapting to plant-specific fouling signals across 
distinct wastewater backgrounds.
4. Implications and outlook
The significance of this study lies not only in improving fouling 
prediction for a single pilot-scale MBR, but also in providing a basis for 
more mechanism-informed prediction in data-limited plants. By trans­
ferring knowledge learned from data-richer source plants, the LSTM 
model achieved reliable prediction in a data-limited target plant, sug­
gesting that data-limited plants may not need to build fouling prediction 
systems entirely from scratch.
More importantly, the present results suggest that indicator selection 
for fouling prediction should begin with identifying the dominant 
fouling processes, followed by extraction of representative fouling fin­
gerprints for prediction. Within this framework, indicators such as EPSc 
and EPSp appear to contain transferable fouling information across MBR 
plants. However, these variables are expensive, labor-intensive, and 
difficult to monitor routinely, which limits their direct practical use. A 
more feasible direction may therefore be to translate key fouling char­
acteristics into monitorable surrogate fingerprints; in this regard, online 
spectral signals such as UV–vis and fluorescence fingerprints may pro­
vide a practical basis for plant-adaptive prediction and proactive fouling 
control [57].
Nevertheless, several limitations remain and point to directions for 
future work. The present models were developed from a limited number 
of pilot-scale MBRs with relatively similar operational backgrounds. At 
full scale, membrane fouling is further shaped by scale-dependent hy­
drodynamics and aeration and by greater operational variability than in 
pilot systems [58], so the pretrained relationships may not directly 
capture full-scale fouling dynamics. For systems with substantially 
different influent or operating conditions, the sludge and EPS/SMP 
composition and the dominant foulants can change, weakening the 
transferable EPS-centered signal and possibly requiring additional 
plant-specific indicators for reliable adaptation. The framework should 
therefore be further validated on larger datasets and extended to 
full-scale, geographically diverse plants to establish its robustness and 
generalizability. In addition, the prediction accuracy did not approach 
unity, indicating that the current indicator set is still incomplete. Besides 
organic foulants, the target plant also showed evidence of inorganic 
contributions, particularly iron-associated fouling revealed by the CIP 
results, which may not yet be fully captured by the present model inputs. 
Identifying more comprehensive indicators that better capture the 
combined effects of organic and inorganic foulants may in turn help 
support earlier fouling warning, better timing of operational adjustment 
and membrane cleaning, reduced energy and chemical consumption, 
and improved process stability (Fig. S13).
5. Conclusions
● Reliable base models for MBR fouling prediction were developed by 
pretraining on the three data-richer source plants. SHAP analysis 
showed that LSTM-base learned physically meaningful fouling- 
related patterns mainly through EPSc, EPSp, and SMPp, rather 
than relying on a single dominant operational signal. This was sup­
ported by the shared source plant characteristics, including EPS ar­
omatic protein-like fluorescence, flocculated sludge particle-size 
distributions, and preferential retention of SMP by the membrane. 
These results indicate that LSTM-base provided a mechanistically 
interpretable and reliable pretrained basis for subsequent cross-plant 
transfer.
● Fine-tuning improved TMP prediction for the target plant treating 
domestic wastewater, with LSTM-FT achieving R2 = 0.89 at 40% FT. 
SHAP and LOFO analyses showed that EPSc remained dominant, 
while EPSp gained importance after fine-tuning. Under the Fe- 
enriched condition of the target MBR plant, the increased impor­
tance of EPSp was associated with a shift toward protein-enriched 
EPS characteristics. Together, these findings explain why fine- 
tuning improved prediction accuracy: it retained the transferable 
EPSc-centered fouling basis while recalibrating the model toward Fe- 
associated, protein-enriched EPS characteristics that were more 
closely associated with fouling in the target plant.
● Validation in the industrial MBR further demonstrated that the 
framework remained effective under a markedly different fouling 
background. Unlike the target plant treating domestic wastewater, 
SMPp became the second most important feature in the industrial 
MBR, reflecting a more prominent proteinaceous SMP signal linked 
to operational disturbances and biomass decay. The continued 
dominance of EPSc in the industrial MBR further supported the cross- 
scenario stability of this transferable fouling basis, while the 
increased attribution of SMPp showed that fine-tuning could adap­
tively reweight plant-specific fouling drivers.
●Overall, this study demonstrates that shared fouling-related infor­
mation can be transferred across MBR plants and selectively recali­
