2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data collection
A pilot-scale MBR plant treating domestic wastewater in Singapore 
was selected as the target plant. The system received Fe-enriched 
influent, with an Fe dosage of approximately 10–30 mg/L. It had a 
treatment capacity of 24 m3/d and a process configuration comprising 
pretreatment, a five-pass step-feed bioreactor with anaerobic/oxic 
zonation, and a membrane tank (Fig. 1(a)). The TMP profile of the target 
plant during the monitoring period is shown in Fig. 2(a), the membrane 
module is shown in Fig. 2(b), and the detailed process and operating 
parameters are summarized in Text S1. The target plant dataset covered 
the period from September 2021 to June 2022. Owing to the twice- 
weekly measurement frequency of EPS and SMP, model development 
was based only on dates with complete matched measurements for all 
input variables and TMP, yielding 71 valid temporally matched records. 
This data-limited target plant setting reflected the practical monitoring 
constraints of pilot-scale MBR operation and provided a realistic sce­
nario for evaluating the proposed cross-plant transfer learning 
framework.

2.2. Physicochemical analytical methods
HRT, SRT, and FLUX were recorded as routine operational parame­
ters. TCOD and SCOD were determined by the APHA 5220D closed 
reflux colorimetric method, and MLSS and MLVSS were measured ac­
cording to Standard Methods [28]. SMP was defined as the dissolved 
fraction of the supernatant after 0.45 μm membrane filtration and EPS 
was extracted from the residual biomass using the heat method [29]. 
Proteins and polysaccharides in SMP and EPS, including SMPp, SMPc, 
EPSp, and EPSc, were quantified using the Modified Lowry Protein 
Assay and Dubois Method, respectively [30]. Liquid chromatography­
–organic carbon detection (LC-OCD) was employed to characterize the 
dissolved organic matter, including biopolymers with molecular weight 
(MW) greater than 20 kDa and low-molecular-weight (LMW) com­
pounds with MW less than 1000 Da, such as humics, building blocks, 
LMW acids, and LMW neutrals. The particle size distribution (PSD) of 
mixed liquor was analyzed using a laser diffraction particle analyzer. 
Capillary suction time (CST) was measured using a CST apparatus [31]. 
Total organic carbon (TOC) was measured using a TOC analyzer 
(TOC-VCSH, Shimadzu, Japan). Excitation–emission matrix (EEM) 
spectra were measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer [32]. 
Total iron was measured using inductively coupled plasma optical 
emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES).

2.3. Data preprocessing and feature selection
To ensure reliable model development, the raw datasets were first 
preprocessed by outlier treatment and missing-value imputation (Text 
S2). To place variables on comparable scales and prevent variables with 
larger numerical ranges from dominating model training, z-score stan­
dardization was applied during preprocessing (Text S3). The scaler was 
fitted using the training data only, and model outputs were inverse- 
transformed to the original scale after training.
The final prediction target of this study was next-day TMP, whereas 
dTMP was used only as an intermediate training target to alleviate non- 
stationarity and improve output comparability across plants [33,34]. 
Compared with raw TMP values, dTMP reduced inter-plant differences 
in TMP level and produced distributions more centered around zero 
(Fig. S3). Predicted dTMP values were then added to the current-day 
TMP to reconstruct next-day TMP for model evaluation and 
presentation.
To assess source–target distribution discrepancy and overlap rele­
vant to transfer learning, joint principal component analysis (PCA) was 
performed using the input variables together with dTMP. The input and 
output blocks were standardized separately and assigned equal overall 
weights (Text S4).
The sample-size-to-feature ratio (SFR) is an important indicator of 
whether model complexity is appropriate for a given dataset size, and in 
small-sample modeling an SFR greater than 10 is generally considered 
desirable [35]. Therefore, Pearson correlation analysis was used as a 
coarse pre-screening step (Text S5). Because the final prediction target 
remained next-day TMP, this initial screening was performed against 
TMP rather than dTMP. It considered both feature–target association 
and inter-feature collinearity, but served only as a pragmatic 
dimensionality-control procedure rather than a definitive assessment of 
nonlinear predictive relevance.
2.4. Construction of base models based on source plants
Given the time-series nature of the data, the source datasets were 
split within each plant according to chronological order rather than 
randomly, thereby reducing the risk of information leakage between 
temporally adjacent samples. Considering the limited sample sizes and 
short input window, LSTM was selected as a compact recurrent archi­
tecture for sequence learning [36]. Transformer- and TCN-based models 
may offer advantages when larger MBR datasets or longer temporal 
contexts are available, and systematic benchmarking under such con­
ditions remains a direction for future work [37,38]. Accordingly, two 
base models were developed: LSTM-base for recurrent sequence learning 
and XGBoost-base for tree-based learning (Fig. 1(c)). LSTM-base used 
standardized feature sequences with a sequence length of 3 time steps, 
whereas XGBoost-base used the same three-step historical information 
encoded as lagged features. This design ensured that the two base 
models used the same amount of historical information, allowing a fair 
comparison of their predictive performance. Additional implementation 
details are provided in Text S6.
Hyperparameters for both base models were optimized using the 
training split of the source plant datasets. Because the data were time- 
ordered, five-fold time-series cross-validation was combined with 
Optuna-based hyperparameter search to prevent leakage from future 
samples and improve the robustness of model selection. After optimi­
zation, both base models were trained on the same training split, and the 
trained models were saved to initialize the transfer learning models. The 
hyperparameter search spaces, final settings, and training details are 
provided in Table S2–S3 and Text S7.
2.5. Construction of the baseline model for the target plant
Before establishing transfer learning models, a baseline model was 
developed using only the target plant dataset, and its performance was 
compared with that of the transfer learning models. To ensure compa­
rability, the target plant dataset was partitioned in chronological order, 
with the first 50% used for model training and the final 50% reserved for 
testing. Under this split, only a limited amount of target plant data was 
available for model training. With such a small training set, a standalone 
target-trained LSTM baseline would be more prone to unstable fitting 
and overfitting, making the resulting comparison less reliable [39]. An 
independent XGBoost model was therefore adopted as the baseline. It 
was trained on the training portion of the target plant dataset using the 
same feature set as the transfer learning models, and the scaler for the 
target variable was fitted independently using the same training portion. 
Detailed settings are listed in Table S4.
2.6. Construction of transfer learning models
For transfer learning, the target plant dataset was partitioned chro­
nologically using the same 50:50 split: the first 50% was used as the fine- 
tuning pool, whereas the final 50% was kept unseen during fine-tuning 
and reserved as an independent testing set (Fig. 1(d)), thereby reducing 
the risk of temporal information leakage. The fine-tuning ratio (FT) was 
defined as the proportion of the total target plant dataset used for fine- 
tuning, and was examined at 0, 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, and 50%. The 
exact numbers of target plant records in the fine-tuning pool, testing set, 
and each FT setting are summarized in Table S5. The resulting transfer 
models were denoted as LSTM-FT and XGBoost-FT. At FT = 0, no target 
plant samples were used for fine-tuning, and the two models therefore 
corresponded to direct transfer without target plant adaptation.
For each nonzero FT setting, multiple fine-tuning segments of the 
corresponding length were sampled from the fine-tuning pool to reduce 
dependence on any single local history (Text S8). Each segment-level 
experiment was repeated with five random seeds, and performance 
was reported as the mean ± standard deviation across all fine-tuning 
segments and repeated runs. LSTM-FT and XGBoost-FT were fine- 
tuned using different update strategies, with implementation details 
and hyperparameter settings provided in Table S6–S7 and Text S9.
All experiments were implemented in Python 3.10.18 using relevant 
third-party libraries. The deep-learning models were built and trained in 
PyTorch 2.7.0. The computing environment included an Intel Core i7- 
14700KF processor (28 logical cores), 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA 
GeForce RTX 5060 Ti GPU.
2.7. Evaluation of model performance
Model performance was evaluated using R2, RMSE, and MAE (details 
in Text S10). Model interpretability was examined using LOFO and 
SHAP analyses (Fig. 1(e)), as detailed in Text S11 and Text S12, 
respectively.
2.8. Model adaptability under cross-scenario conditions
Additional validation was conducted using data from a pilot-scale 
MBR treating mixed petrochemical and pharmaceutical industrial 
wastewater in Singapore. Detailed process configuration and operating 
parameters are provided in Table S8. This validation was performed 
using LSTM-FT under two fine-tuning conditions, FT = 0 and FT = 40%. 
To maintain consistency with the main analysis, the pretrained model, 
chronological fine-tuning/testing logic, data preprocessing procedure, 
and model settings were kept the same. Accordingly, this test was 
intended to assess whether the established workflow could remain 
effective under industrial wastewater conditions, without additional 
model optimization.
