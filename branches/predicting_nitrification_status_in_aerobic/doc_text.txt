A B S T R A C T
Membrane bioreactor (MBR) systems for on-site greywater treatment and reuse have been widely 
investigated, where robust nitrification monitoring is critical to prevent operational disruptions 
and maintain effluent standards. This research proposed an interpretable machine learning-based 
framework to predict nitrification efficacy (classified as "sufficient" or "insufficient") in MBR 
systems, involving model-based and post hoc interpretability. Three interpretable machine 
learning algorithms—logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting 
(XGB)—were systematically evaluated, accounting for model bias-variance trade-offs during bi­
nary classification. Six input features were selected based on direct measurability, compatibility 
with commercial sensors, and operational relevance. Model optimization criteria focused on 
maximizing precision to prevent erroneous controls for insufficient nitrification, achieved by low 
false positive rate (FPR: misclassified insufficient nitrification) and high true positive rate (TPR: 
correctly identified sufficient nitrification). Results showed that both LR and XGB achieved 
identical precision scores > 0.85 under standard conditions. Cross-scenario validation demon­
strated RF’s superior generalizability (precision=0.87) when predicting nitrification in biocarrier- 
amended MBRs using training data from non-biocarrier systems, reflecting the potential trans­
ferability of the model. The post hoc interpretability and stability analysis further identified 
prediction sensitivity to data limitations and distributional biases, providing critical insights for 
improving and even properly deploying models in practice.

processes—including heterotrophic oxidation and autotrophic nitrification—with membrane filtration for effective solids separation

Nitrification—converting ammonium (NH4

reuse standards. Centralized plants typically use the proportional integral differential (PID) control strategy to maintain fixed dissolved 
oxygen (DO) setpoints, yet this strategy adapts poorly to influent fluctuations and incurs high energy costs (Gu et al., 2023). On-site 
greywater systems face amplified load variability: daily/seasonal usage patterns cause sustained low loads punctuated by sharp peaks, 
while highly variable water quality challenges empirical control parameters (DelaPaz-Ruíz et al., 2024; Hassan et al., 2024). Although 
PID adjusts aeration valves, its static DO targets lack real-time responsiveness to dynamic NH₄⁺-N loads (e.g., dynamic adjustment 
based on the NH₄⁺-N load) (Li et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2024). Furthermore, PID relies on submerged DO probes, which are prone to 
fouling and drift in small-scale MBRs due to continuous aeration and sludge accumulation. This compromises measurement reliability, 
potentially masking sensor failure as load variation.
Reliable nitrification monitoring remains challenging for decentralized MBRs, where inconsistent efficiency risks process stability 
despite compliant effluent (observed in the current trial). Notably, real-time monitoring of nitrification processes within 
MBRs—particularly under decentralized operational scenarios—remains unaddressed in existing studies. Commercial NH4
+-N/NO2
- -N/ 
NO3
- -N sensors are often impractical for installing in the reactor: high sludge concentrations cause rapid fouling and maintenance 
burdens, increasing operational costs (Huang et al., 2024). Organic matter, suspended solids, and ionic interference further distort 
readings, while sensor response lags fail to capture rapid nitrogen dynamics—hindering real-time control.
Machine learning (ML) offers promising data-driven solutions for monitoring MBR nitrification, using effluent water quality pa­
rameters to assess performance without intrusive sensors, avoiding sensor installation in the reactors with challenges mentioned above 
(Van de Walle et al., 2023). These methods enable rapid, low-cost, and real-time monitoring—facilitating prompt process interventions 
and enhancing safety (Bahramian et al., 2023). Compared to mechanistic models, ML requires simpler inputs and incurs minimal 
maintenance, ideal for on-site systems (Duarte et al., 2024). Given greywater treatment’s critical need for regulatory and public trust, 
prioritizing interpretable ML methods provides transparent prediction rationales for operator action and model refinement (Allen 
et al., 2024). Additionally, utilizing interpretable ML methods allows us to further understand the limitations of current training

Previous studies on data-driven prediction in MBR-based wastewater treatment have primarily focused on effluent quality, targeted 
pollutant removal efficiency, and membrane fouling (Muniz de Queiroz et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2022; Zhong et al., 2022). However, 
relying solely on effluent monitoring provides limited process controllability and fails to enable timely interventions during abnormal 
operational events. This approach is also labor-intensive, time-consuming, and inadequate for tracing underlying process flaws before 
effluent quality violations occur (Moretti et al., 2024). In contrast, real-time process monitoring enables proactive control, enhances 
system resilience, and supports early fault detection—critical for ensuring consistent water quality across diverse reuse applications.
This work aims to develop an interpretable ML model to monitor the nitrification process in MBRs via effluent parameters, reducing 
the difficulty of input feature acquisition in practice. Features were selected based on direct measurability, sensor compatibility, and 
operational relevance. To mitigate overfitting and balance bias-variance tradeoffs, three interpretable algorithms—logistic regression 
(LR), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient boosting (XGB)—were evaluated, simultaneously considering the bias and variance of 
each algorithm. Model training prioritized operational stability by maximizing precision, depending on the higher true positive rate 
(TPR) and lower false positive rate (FPR), trained on data from a suspended-sludge MBR. Furthermore, SHapley Additive exPlanations 
(SHAP) analysis, feature importance, and pairwise assessments elucidated factor-prediction linkages and informed model refinement. 
The models were further validated on a hybrid system, integrating suspended sludge and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) biocarriers, 
performing simultaneously nitrification-denitrification, demonstrating cross-scenario prediction applicability. Finally, an automated 
aeration control strategy is proposed to operationalize the monitoring framework.

2.2.1. Binary classification: nitrification process evaluation
To effectively predict nitrification status, the relationship between the concentrations of NO₃⁻-N, NO₂⁻-N, and NH₄⁺-N in the reactor 
must be understood. In the simultaneous nitrification and denitrification process within the MBR, the concentration of NO3
- -N typically 
exceeds that of NO2
- -N and NH4
+-N under sufficient aeration, which is a key factor for effective nitrogen conversion and removal (Huang 
et al., 2022; Paetkau and Cicek, 2011). Therefore, sufficient nitrification was defined as the condition in which the concentration of 
NO3
- -N exceeds the combined concentrations of NO2
- -N and NH4
+-N, labeled as “Positive”; otherwise, the condition was labeled as 
“Negative”. The primary goal of the data-driven model for treatment process prediction was to classify whether nitrification was 
sufficient or insufficient.
2.2.2. Qualitative analysis of inputs selection
Three data categories were collected during the experiment: 1 > Hydraulic: hydraulic retention time (HRT) and sludge retention 
time (SRT); 2 > Operational: transmembrane pressure (TMP), air/influent/effluent flow rates, gross/net flux, flux recovery; 3 > Water 
quality: COD, TN, NH₄⁺-N, NO₃⁻-N, NO₂⁻-N (in influent, effluent, and mixed liquor in the reactor), TSS (influent/effluent), and DO, 
MLSS, MLVSS in the reactor. Removal efficiency was also calculated for each parameter. Input features were selected from the 
perspective of practical deployment, including: 1> Direct measurability, 2> Commercial sensor compatibility, 3> Operational rele­
vance (e.g., TMP).
Indirect variables (SRT, removal efficiencies) were excluded. Water quality parameters (DO, COD, TN, NH₄⁺-N, NO₃⁻-N, NO₂⁻-N) 
and operational factors (flow rates, air flow, TMP) were initially considered. However, > 50 % DO data absence led to its exclu­
sion—simulating probe failure scenarios. Air flow rate was retained as an oxygen proxy. Online sensor compatibility was prioritized to 
enhance model applicability and robustness. Influent water quality was excluded due to sensor reliability concerns: complex matrices 
cause fouling on sensors and sensor degradation (e.g., Ag/AgCl electrodes dissolution) (Ching et al., 2022; Haimi et al., 2013). Effluent 
NO₂⁻-N was also excluded due to measurement instability versus stable NO₃⁻-N. TMP was included to monitor membrane performance, 
contributing to long-term operation monitoring. Finally, the dataset was refined by removing inappropriate variables and simplifying 
the inputs to facilitate dataset splitting.
2.2.3. Data preprocessing
From 128 datasets collected over 235 days from dual MBRs, eight incomplete entries were excluded. Biocarriers were added to one 
reactor in Phase 3 to enable denitrification (Fig. 1), altering parameter relationships. To evaluate cross-scenario applicability, 23 data 
groups from Phase 3 were used as an independent test group for models trained exclusively on 97 no-biocarrier data groups (activated 
sludge only). The 97 data groups were utilized for training, validation, and testing of the model’s performance in predicting nitrifi­
cation with activated sludge only. This approach assessed performance under altered operational conditions. Given inherent influent 
variability in onsite greywater systems, outliers were intentionally retained to evaluate model robustness against operational fluc­
tuations, reflecting real-world dynamics.

2.2.5. Statistical correlation analysis for inputs determination
Based on the preliminary input feature selections by qualitative analysis in Section 2.2.2, Spearman’s rank correlation was used to 
assess statistical relationships between inputs and outputs. This nonparametric method quantifies the strength of monotonic associ­
ations between variables without assuming normal distribution, making it suitable for nonlinear yet monotonic classification contexts 
(Hastie et al., 2009). Statistical significance was determined using p-values. Specifically, the p-value indicates the probability of 
obtaining the observed Spearman correlation coefficient, or a more extreme value, under the null hypothesis that no monotonic 
relationship exists between the variables. A p-value < 0.05 was used to reject the null hypothesis and confirm a significant correlation. 
The "spearmanr" function (SciPy, Python) computed coefficients and p-values for all variable pairs. (Van Rossum and Drake, 2009).
2.2.6. Model-based interpretability: interpretable ML algorithms
To predict the nitrification status in the MBR and ensure stable operations, this study considered not only the statistical accuracy of 
ML methods but also integrated algorithm-specific characteristics. In ML, bias stems from simplifying assumptions, while variance 
reflects sensitivity to fluctuations in the training data (Reynaert et al., 2023). Minimizing one typically increases the other (Belkin 
et al., 2019).

Previous studies have indicated that simpler models generally offer advantages for small datasets, including reduced overfitting 
risk, improved interpretability, and lower computational demands (Reynaert et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2025). For small, non-temporal 
datasets, complex structures such as artificial neural networks can increase the risk of overfitting (Chiroma et al., 2019).
Therefore, three interpretable algorithms with distinct bias–variance properties were selected. The logistic regression and decision 
tree-based models are normally cited as simulatable, i.e., interpretable (Breiman et al., 2017; Murdoch et al., 2019). 
– Logistic regression (LR): High bias, low variance. Applies logit transformation to linear regression for binary probability pre­
diction, handling continuous/categorical variables with high interpretability and simple structure (Hosmer et al., 2013).
– Random forest classification (RF): Intermediate bias-variance tradeoff. An ensemble method aggregating multiple decision trees 
via voting, improving accuracy and noise resistance for high-dimensional data (Breiman, 2001).
– Extreme gradient boosting classification (XGB): Low bias, high variance. An optimized implementation of the gradient boosting 
algorithm with successive decision trees, each correcting the errors of its predecessor to minimize the loss function and improve 
predictive performance. Compared to traditional gradient boosting decision trees, XGB introduces enhancements such as regula­
rization, parallel computing, and custom loss function definition (Chen and Guestrin, 2016).
All models were implemented using scikit-learn 1.0.2 (Python 3.9.13), with hyperparameters optimized via grid search.
2.2.7. Evaluation metrics and hyperparameter tuning criteria
The performance of the classification models was evaluated using a confusion matrix, contrasting predicted outcomes ("Sufficient"/ 
"Insufficient") against actual conditions. The “Positive” label indicated sufficient nitrification, while the “Negative” label indicated 
insufficient nitrification, as defined in Section 2.1 Key metrics included true positives (TP: correctly classified sufficient nitrification), 
false positives (FP: erroneously classified sufficient under insufficient conditions), false negatives (FN: insufficient misclassifications 
despite actual sufficiency), and true negatives (TN: accurate classified as insufficient). Section 2.2.1
Minimizing the FPR was crucial for ensuring safe effluent water quality. For instance, in a control system integrated with this 
nitrification monitoring model, when insufficient nitrification occurs, more air should be supplied. However, if an FP occurs, the model 
incorrectly predicts sufficient nitrification, leading the control system to refrain from adding additional oxygen and unsafe effluent 
unsuitable for reuse. Meanwhile, a higher TPR was necessary to detect sufficient nitrification under conventional operation conditions, 
reducing operational costs by preventing unnecessary oxygen supply triggered by FN.

et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2019). Therefore, a balanced approach considering both TPR (Eq. 1) and FPR (Eq. 2) was adopted, with 
precision (Eq. 3) serving as a key optimization metric. Precision reflects the proportion of TP predictions among all positive predictions

2.2.8. Post hoc interpretability
Despite the inherent interpretability of decision tree-based methods, complex models necessitate post hoc interpretation (Murdoch 
et al., 2019).
Feature importance quantifies the contributions of input variables, aiding model’s interpretability improvement and guiding data 
collection strategies for enhanced performance. In LR, feature coefficients are derived by minimizing the loss function (negative log- 
likelihood) using optimization methods such as gradient descent (Hastie et al., 2009). They indicate the strength and direction of each 
feature’s linear relationship with the target variable (Murphy, 2012). For RF, feature importance is assessed by the reduction in 
impurity (e.g., Gini index) when a feature is used to split a node. Scores are averaged across all trees, capturing the overall contribution 
of each feature to the model (Liaw and Wiener, 2002). The feature importance in XGB is typically calculated using the Gain method. 
This method sums up and normalizes the gains from all splits involving a feature to compute its importance score (Chen and Guestrin, 
2016).
SHAP analysis was implemented to leverage game theory to assign each feature an importance value for specific predictions. SHAP

3. Results analysis: data-driven model prediction
3.1. MBR performance and data collection
Phases 1–2 (no biocarriers) showed consistent COD (>85 % removal), NH₄⁺-N, and TN trends. Phase 1 effluent contained high 
NH₄⁺-N (14 ± 5 mg/L; 28 % removal) and TN (14 ± 2 mg/L; 23 % removal) with minimal NO₃⁻-N, indicating insufficient nitrification. 
Increasing aeration in Phase 2 improved NH₄⁺-N removal to 95 ± 1 % (effluent: 0.9 ± 0.2 mg/L). Meanwhile, the MLSS/MLVSS ratio 
stabilized at 0.92 ± 0.07 during Phase 1 and remained around 0.9 in Phase 2, indicating that most of the sludge consisted of organic 
fractions—a sign of healthy sludge conditions. In Phase 3, adding PVDF biocarriers to MBR-2 improved denitrification performance: 
TN removal reached 58 ± 21 %, compared to 34 ± 15 % in MBR-1 without biocarrier addition, while COD removal (90 ± 4 %) and 
NH₄⁺-N removal (94 ± 5 %) were maintained. The MBR-2 also exhibited reduced membrane fouling (TMP < 10 kPa) and higher 
biomass concentrations (MLSS: 2832 ± 831 mg/L; MLVSS: 2655 ± 806 mg/L) compared to the MBR-1 (MLSS: 2439 ± 648 mg/L; 
MLVSS: 2191 ± 543 mg/L). Overall, effluent COD and TN met EU Directive 91/271/EEC standards, though TN removal in Phase 3 was 
inconsistent, likely due to biofilm acclimatization. More detailed results are described in SI 2.
3.2. Input features determination
Following qualitative feature selection as shown in Section 2.2.2, nine input variables remained and were statistically evaluated. SI 
3 identifies variables with significant correlations labeled with circles (p-value < 0.05) among the remaining input features. To 
simplify the model structure, only the influent flow rate was retained due to its similarity to the effluent flow rate, which demonstrated 
over 97 % flux recovery. Despite TMP’s weak correlation with nitrification, its inclusion could enhance model transferability by ac­
counting for acyclic membrane fouling in long-term operations. Meanwhile, while the membrane cleaning record correlated with TMP, 
reflecting membrane maintenance needs, it was excluded to avoid material-specific biases. In addition, TN was omitted due to its 
marginal predictive contribution and impracticality for real-time monitoring. Although TN has certain correlation with the target 
output, TN sensors are costly and feature long measurement intervals (30–60 min, based on commercially available TN sensors), 
compared with sensors monitoring NO3
- -N and NH4
+-N.
Finally, six input features were selected for nitrification status prediction, including three operational factors (air flow rate, influent 
flow rate, and TMP) and three water quality variables (effluent COD, NO3
- -N, and NH4
+-N concentrations). This streamlined feature set 
balances model simplicity, accuracy, and field feasibility for nitrification monitoring.

distributions for Predicted Positive (red) and Predicted Negative (blue) classes. Curve peaks revealed value concentration regions (e.g.,

Feature importance and KDE analysis confirmed effluent NH₄⁺-N and NO₃⁻-N as primary predictors of nitrification status. Although 
mechanistically relevant, influent flow rate (11.7–20.6 ML/min) and air flow rate (2–6 Lpm) showed limited contributions due to low 
operational variability. Effluent COD likewise had a minor impact, evidenced by its lower coefficient of variance (55.25 %) versus 
NH₄⁺-N (93.82 %) and NO₃⁻-N (101.72 %)—calculated as standard deviation relative to mean. This variability disparity explained 
differential feature importance.
SI 7 and 8 display pairwise plots across models and feature importance quantification across validation groups respectively, which 
confirmed effluent NO₃⁻-N/NH₄⁺-N as primary predictors, aligning with their biochemical centrality in nitrification. However, XGB 
validation groups showed unexpected prominence of stable influent and airflow rates over N-species. This stemmed from XGB’s 
boosting mechanism: sequential trees prioritize features correcting predecessor errors, dynamically weighting features during in­
cremental optimization (Bent´ejac et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 2024). Such adaptability could enhance model flexibility across data 
groups but may inflate transient feature importance and overfitting risks.
Fig. 4 illustrates the SHAP analysis results for three predictive models—LR, RF, and XGB—including global importance (beeswarm 
plots, Fig. 4a–c) and instance-level decomposition (waterfall plots, Fig. 4d–f). The beeswarm plots revealed consistent feature 
importance rankings across all models: Effluent NH₄⁺-N and Effluent NO₃⁻-N dominate, followed by Effluent COD and TMP, suggesting 
robust, model-agnostic relevance of nitrogen and organic matter indicators in treatment performance prediction, consistent with the 
results of feature importance analysis.
More nuanced insights arose from the waterfall plots. In the LR model (Fig. 4d), the output was strongly driven by a single high 
NH₄⁺-N value (+2.84), counteracted by negative contributions from NO₃⁻-N and COD, reflecting the additive nature of linear models. 
Such strong reliance on a single dominant feature highlighted an overfitting risk: when NH₄⁺-N deviates from training patterns, the 
model struggles to generalize, explaining the decline in test TPR. In contrast, the RF model (Fig. 4e) showed a more balanced 
contribution from multiple features, with lower-magnitude but distributed impacts, consistent with ensemble decision averaging. The 
XGB model (Fig. 4f) exhibited stronger nonlinearity and interaction effects. Here, COD and NO₃⁻-N jointly exerted dominant negative 
influence, while NH₄⁺-N contributed positively but was insufficient to overcome process stress signals, resulting in a negative pre­
diction. Nevertheless, the complexity of these nonlinear feature interactions in XGB, combined with limited training samples, raised 
the likelihood of memorizing dataset-specific noise, another hallmark of overfitting. Notably, TMP and Air Flow Rate emerged as 
minor but non-negligible modulators, indicating sensitivity to operational states.

The SHAP analysis revealed that effluent NH₄⁺-N and NO₃⁻-N were the most influential predictors across all models, underscoring

process strategies, promoting adaptive control in MBR operations.

3.4. Cross-scenario test
Building upon the demonstrated efficacy of the model in conventional nitrification systems (Section 3.3), its cross-scenario pre­
diction capability was further evaluated in MBRs incorporating biocarriers for enhanced simultaneous nitrification-denitrification. The 
base model trained on no-biocarrier operations was directly tested to predict nitrification status in biocarrier-augmented systems 
without retraining.
As shown in Table 1a, the cross-condition testing yielded three principal observations: 
1) Compared with prediction performance of the test group without biocarriers, the RF model exhibited improvement in both ac­
curacy (0.79 → 0.83) and precision (0.83 → 0.87) under biocarrier-amended conditions, suggesting superior adaptability to 
simultaneous nitrification-denitrification system complexities.
2) TPRs of all these three algorithms were greater than or equal to 0.8, especially that of LR, which reached 1, showing a significant 
increase compared with the test group without adding biological carriers.
3) Corresponding FPR values rose significantly, particularly for LR (FPR=0.50), indicating only 50 % negative sample identification 
accuracy.
Full confusion matrices are provided in SI 9. While these results are theoretically not directly comparable to those from non- 
biocarriers data (Table 1a) due to differences in operating conditions and dataset characteristics, these findings provide operational 
insights into cross-condition model behavior.
The comparatively high TPR and FPR observed in the test group of the MBR with biocarrier addition can be explained by the 
following reasons. First, feature consistency in the test set played a significant role. In the training set, the input feature "air flow rate" 
included two values (2 and 6 Lpm), whereas all samples in the test set had an air flow rate of 6 Lpm. This consistency simplified the 
model’s decision rules, reducing the likelihood of misclassifying negative label samples (as shown in the KDEs in Table 1b). Second, the 
limitations of the decision boundary also contributed to the results. Despite effective positive classification at 6 Lpm, the model 
retained residual misclassification tendencies for negative samples. This effect was amplified by the test set’s limited negative sample 
size (n = 8), rendering FPR measurements particularly sensitive to individual misclassifications.
Finally, bootstrap sampling (n = 1000) revealed that models without biocarriers exhibited high precision/accuracy but wide 95 % 
confidence intervals (indicating instability), whereas biocarrier addition narrowed confidence intervals—particularly for RF, which 
achieved optimal stability and performance (detailed confidence interval analysis for both scenarios is provided in SI 10).
4. Discussion and future perspective of sustainable operation
4.1. Data limitation and data collection
Data scarcity remains a fundamental constraint in developing ML models for environmental applications, particularly when 
adapting pre-trained models to novel operational scenarios through transfer learning (Dansawad et al., 2023; Imen et al., 2023; Lowe 
et al., 2022; Zhang, 2022; Zhu et al., 2022). This dataset—collected from experimental operations instead of being designed for ML 
development—limited model evaluation despite > 0.8 test accuracy. Larger datasets spanning diverse operation conditions are 
essential for robust decision boundaries, particularly when distinguishing transient process states during simultaneous 
nitrification-denitrification.
In addition to the prediction overfitting due to this smaller dataset discussed in Section 3.3, data constraints impact optimization 
criteria. Although precision maximization (high TPR/low FPR) was prioritized, limited data hindered boundary definition for TPR-FPR 
optimization curves. The optimization curve, as demonstrated by Reynaert, et al. (2023), is a useful tool for visualizing the model 
calibration process and guiding the selection of an appropriate FPR to evaluate performance across the training, validation, and test 
sets. Beyond mathematical FPR determination, water reuse contexts require quantitative risk assessment of FPs. Identifying reasonable 
operating points before cross-scenario testing would benefit model validation and set-point adjustments. Meanwhile, wide confidence 
intervals further underscore the need for larger datasets to evaluate model stability and feature contributions comprehensively. 
Current reliance on effluent NH₄⁺-N/NO₃⁻-N should expand to include operational parameters, water quality dynamics, and temporal 
data for credible sensitivity analysis.
Online sensors are vital for consistent data collection. Sensor-compatible features (Section 2.2.2) were prioritized in this research to

Incorporating more challenging operational conditions, such as system failures (e.g., aeration malfunction or membrane damage), 
is essential for improving the model’s applicability in real-world treatment scenarios (Reynaert, et al., 2023). Though this study 
included suboptimal operations (e.g., low-aeration nitrification), systematic data collection across failure modes remains essential.
4.2. Trade-offs of input features for data-driven model structure
The model uses effluent quality to assess concurrent nitrification status, effectively employing historical data to predict current 
conditions. However, influent flow variations affecting hydraulic retention time (HRT) introduce input-output time lags that may 
compromise accuracy. Accounting for inflow dynamics is thus critical, especially with highly variable greywater generation (Pinto and 
Maheshwari, 2015). Future data collection should address flow variability to enhance predictions, potentially through flow-specific 
sub-models. Due to extensive DO data gaps, air flow rate served as the sole oxygen proxy. However, observed DO hetero­
geneity—driven by fluid dynamics, uneven oxygen transfer, microbial activity gradients, and reactor design (Gillot and H´eduit, 
2000)—necessitates distributed DO sensors for accurate future modeling.
These findings underscore the need for strategic input feature selection. Features must balance statistical correlations with practical 
relevance, as imbalanced datasets can distort input-output relationships. Systematic comparison of feature combinations is essential, 
noting that added inputs do not guarantee accuracy gains despite correlations (Linardatos et al., 2020; Reynaert et al., 2023). Besides, 
feature stability and reliability are equally critical: water-quality-only inputs risk long-term bias without rigorous sensor maintenance. 
Measurement errors and recalibration schedules must be integrated into robust model design. Finally, sensor costs should be factored 
into monitoring systems to enhance real-world applicability and shorten payback periods.
4.3. Model transferability improvement
Model transferability — the ability of a model to adapt to new tasks or domains with minimal retraining — can be systematically 
optimized through structured transfer learning frameworks (Tian and Yu, 2023). Transfer learning strategies include feature align­
ment, pre-training models, meta-learning architectures, self-supervised learning, data augmentation, and scalability of model structure 
(Yu et al., 2022).
Despite broad industrial investigations and applications of transfer learning, its potential for cross-scenario adaptation in water 
treatment systems remains underexplored. Current interdisciplinary advances demonstrate promising pathways: Li et al. (2021)
improved the short-term traffic prediction accuracy via fine-tuning pre-trained models from data-rich areas; Zhou et al. (2025)
enhanced battery performance transferability through condition-robust data augmentation; Nieves-Avendano et al. (2023) success­
fully adapted a pre-trained MobileNetV2 model for mechanical fault detection via targeted domain fine-tuning.
Building on the cross-scenario test (Section 3.4), three enhancement pathways are proposed for enhancing transferability of water 
treatment models: 
– Data-centric optimization: systematic experimental designs should prioritize wide operational condition coverage and represen­
tative data distributions during collection. Data augmentation efficacy directly depends on baseline dataset diversity
– Pre-trained frameworks: development of domain-specific pre-trained models requires cross-institutional data sharing, particularly 
for systems handling varied water qualities or scale-dependent processes. Input features should explicitly incorporate treatment 
facility scale parameters due to their hydraulic and biochemical implications.
– Model structure innovation: Clustered sub-models using feature grouping and range-partitioned training data could further 
improve local accuracy while maintaining overall system adaptability. Further attempts can also be made in the innovative 
application of algorithms (e.g., meta learning).
This multipronged approach addresses current limitations in model generalization while aligning with water treatment’s inherent 
variability in operational scales, influent characteristics, and process configurations. Future work should prioritize establishing open 
benchmarking datasets to standardize transferability evaluation across diverse scenarios.

The binary classification framework demonstrates operational feasibility under data-constrained conditions. Extending the current 
framework to multiclass nitrification states—such as a four-level prediction scheme (25 %, 50 %, 75 %, and complete nitrification)— 
could enable proportional aeration control. This refinement would optimize energy use, reduce carbon footprint, and help ensure 
consistent effluent quality. Such models require expanded datasets from online sensors (Section 4.1) and specialized optimization 
techniques: multi-class threshold tuning addressing misclassification costs, and methods for imbalanced data (Davis and Goadrich,

misclassification, beyond purely mathematical considerations. Additionally, class definitions should align with influent characteristics 
and reuse requirements.
Integrating the multiclassification model into the control system enables more precise adjustments, potentially eliminating the 
need for incremental changes (such as A in Fig. 5), but optimal aeration reduction strategies post-complete nitrification pre­
diction—gradual vs. immediate—require lab/pilot validation. Gradual reduction may be better for maintaining efficiency for strict 
reuse standards, necessitating risk-cost evaluations.
5. Conclusions
This work demonstrates the viability of interpretable ML-based models (LR, RF, XGB) for monitoring MBR nitrification by using six 
sensor-compatible features: airflow, influent flow, TMP, and effluent concentrations of COD, NO₃⁻-N, and NH₄⁺-N. Strategic feature 
selection enabled robust classification (precision >0.85 for LR/XGB). RF excelled in cross-scenario testing (precision=0.87) for pre­
dicting biocarrier operations using training data from non-biocarrier systems, despite a decline in FPR due to feature distribution shifts. 
All models maintained TPR ≥0.80 and accuracy > 0.75 in cross-scenario testing, though limited data constrained stability. Results 
emphasized ML’s potential to enhance MBR control by balancing precision (minimizing FPs) with transferability across configurations. 
Post hoc analysis (feature importance, KDE, pairwise plots, and SHAP analysis) confirmed effluent NO₃⁻-N and NH₄⁺-N as dominant 
predictors. Wide 95 % confidence intervals reflected instability from small dataset scope.
Future research should prioritize systematic data acquisition and model training across diverse influent water quality and DO 
regimes. Concurrently, expanding boundary conditions—specifically targeted collection under high NO₃⁻-N/low NH₄⁺-N conditions is 
essential to optimize model architecture. This work establishes a framework for deploying interpretable data-driven models in 
decentralized greywater reuse, advancing intelligent MBR control while addressing transferability barriers in real-world 
implementations.
