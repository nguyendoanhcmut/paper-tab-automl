Elucidating response effects of 
anammox-based nitrogen removal 
processes for municipal wastewater 
using big data analysis and 
automated machine learning
Jia-Xin Lou1, Jia-Shun Cao2 & Run-Ze Xu2,3
Applications of anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox) processes for treating municipal 
wastewater have promising benefits in reducing carbon emissions and have been heavily investigated 
recently. However, the practical operation of mainstream anammox processes still suffers from unclear 
external regulation strategies. Therefore, this study utilized the H2O automated machine learning 
(AutoML) algorithm and interpretable analysis to capture the internal relationships existing in the 
big dataset collected from anammox-based studies for treating municipal wastewater. The eXtreme 
Gradient Boosting and gradient boosting machine models automatically generated by the AutoML 
algorithm provided the most accurate prediction results (R2 = 0.814–0.993). Moreover, the optimal 
models showed good generalization ability (R2 = 0.725–0.945) for unseen data collected from this 
study. The appropriate ranges of operation conditions and influent characteristics response to the 
high removal efficiency of nitrogen pollutants and high nitrogen removal rate through the anammox 
reaction pathway were revealed by the one-dimensional, and two-dimensional partial dependence 
plots. This work would advance the understanding of how to improve the practical operation of 
mainstream anammox-based nitrogen removal processes for treating municipal wastewater.
Keywords  Mainstream anammox, Automated machine learning, Interpretable analysis, Nitrogen removal 
efficiency, Meta-analysis
The problem of water eutrophication caused by excessive nitrogen and phosphorus emissions has been a global 
concern for a long time1,2. The traditional activated sludge is the most commonly used biological process for 
removing nitrogen and phosphorus. However, eliminating nitrogen through the nitrification and denitrification 
process by the conventional activated sludge has been identified as an energy-intensive process3. Therefore, 
anaerobic ammonium oxidation (anammox), a new technology with low energy consumption, low sludge 
productivity, and a high nitrogen removal rate, has drawn increasing attention in recent years2,4–6.
Unlike traditional two-step nitrification and denitrification processes, the anammox process uses nitrite and 
ammonia as substrates and directly oxidizes them to N2
7. Anammox is one of the most promising processes for 
saving the operating costs of wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs), reducing organic carbon requirements 
by 100%, aeration energy consumption by approximately 60%, and sludge production by approximately 90%8. 
However, the stable supply of nitrite to anammox remains an urgent problem. To provide nitrite steadily, several 
types of combined processes including simultaneous nitrification, anammox and denitrification9,10, partial 
nitrification coupling with anammox (PNA)11, partial denitrification coupling with anammox (PDA)12,13, and 
more complex processes combining PNA and PDA processes14,15 have been investigated extremely. Moreover, 
these anammox processes for mainstream application face many technical bottlenecks such as low nitrogen 
concentration of municipal wastewater, high organic matter concentration16, and low water temperature17 in 
winter. To solve these issues, numerous attempts have been made to mainstream anammox processes from 
multiple operational factors such as influent characteristics, inoculation mode, temperature, reactor type, and 
hydraulic retention time. A large amount of data has been generated during these experiments. However, the

internal knowledge about anammox-based biological nitrogen removal processes remains to be explored from

Machine learning algorithms have drawn increasing attention due to their ability to identify the internal 
relationships in big datasets and create corresponding prediction models18,19. Explainable analysis and 
interpretable methods have been developed to further explain the trained machine learning models20–22. For 
instance, Xu et al. (2022b)23 analyzed response effects of anammox processes to different antibiotics through 
a universal machine learning model (R2 inline-eq-IEq10.9). Similarly, Yang et al. (2023)24 focused on the heavy metal 
stress on anammox processes based on machine learning models. Moreover, Liu et al. (2023a)25 explored the 
N2O emissions from anammox-based processes. They revealed the relationship between N2O emissions and 
operational factors as well as microbial communities through machine learning models and corresponding 
interpretable analysis. These studies have demonstrated that machine learning models have performed well in 
big data analysis for anammox-based processes23–25.
However, the response effects of nitrogen treatment performance in the different types of anammox-based 
processes to multiple operational factors are still unclear. Moreover, previous studies for big data analysis of 
anammox-based processes established machine learning models manually23–25, which suffers from several 
drawbacks. On one hand, the procedure of training and tuning these machine learning models had a significant 
time cost22. On the other hand, the accuracy of these trained models heavily depended on the experience of 
model designers, which had poor reproducibility and robustness. To overcome these drawbacks, automated 
machine learning (AutoML) algorithms have been proposed and drawn increasing attention22,26,27.
Therefore, in this study, the AutoML algorithm was applied to predict nitrogen treatment performance and 
interpret the response effects of anammox-based processes to multiple operational factors. Data from relevant 
anammox-based studies were first analyzed to select the potential input and output variables, which formed the 
integrated dataset for running the H2O AutoML algorithm. Then, the candidate models generated by the H2O 
AutoML algorithm for 7 output variables were compared. Moreover, lab-scale anammox-based experiments were 
carried out to test the prediction performance of candidate models. Based on the optimal models, interpretable 
analysis was conducted to clarify the response effects of output variables to the important input variables. These 
results highlight previously overlooked yet crucial relationships impacting the anammox-based treatment 
performance, providing a significant step towards the improvement of mainstream anammox-based processes.
Materials and methods
Data sources and analysis methods
To reveal internal relationships among factors in anammox-based nitrogen removal processes, this study first 
conducted a comprehensive literature search on the Web of Science using keywords such as “Anaerobic ammonia 
oxidation”, “mainstream anammox”, “municipal wastewater”, “partial nitrification”, and “partial denitrification”. 
The content of this literature was examined and 28 studies were finally selected because they had comprehensive 
and consistent variables related to the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes (Table S9). Liu et al. (2020)28 
conducted a meta-analysis for anammox-based processes with data collected from 62 studies. However, they 
did not consider the operational parameters such as hydraulic retention time (HRT) and nitrogen loading 
rate (NLR) which were utilized as input variables in our study. In total, 2940 samples from different types of 
anammox-based processes in the selected study were collected by the Origin software, including 1927 PDA 
samples, 282 PNA samples, and 731 PDA combined with PNA samples.
The key variables involved in these anammox-based processes were organized, including operation condition 
(sequencing batch OR continuous flow), sludge morphology (floc OR aggregates), influent type (synthetic 
wastewater OR municipal wastewater), enrichment strategy (self-enrichment OR inoculation), anammox-based 
process type (PNA OR PDA OR PNA combined with PDA), operation time (d), HRT (h), carbon to nitrogen 
ratio (C/N), influent chemical oxygen demand (COD, mg/L), influent NH4
+-N (mg/L), influent NO3
−-N (mg/L), 
influent NO2
−-N (mg/L), influent total inorganic nitrogen (TIN, mg/L), effluent NH4
+-N (mg/L), effluent 
NO3

+-N removal efficiency 
(%), TIN removal efficiency (%), nitrification reaction rate by the ammonia oxidation bacteria (NiRR), nitrogen 
removal rate through the anammox reaction pathway (NARR), nitrogen removal rate (NRR) were calculated 
according to the equations in the Supporting information (Text S1)25. To preliminary analyze the relationships 
among these variables, box plots, exploratory data analysis (EDA), and Pearson product-moment correlation 
coefficient (PPMC) were utilized according to our previous study22.
Model development and optimization
In total, 24 variables were collected from relevant literature to be selected as the input and output variables 
of regression machine learning models (Fig. 1). Among them, the operation condition, sludge morphology, 
influent type, enrichment strategy, anammox-based process type and genera of dominant anammox bacteria 
were represented as categorical objects in the dataset (Table S1). The optional 15 input variables included 
operation condition, sludge morphology, influent type, enrichment strategy, process type, dominant anammox 
bacteria, operation time, HRT, C/N, influent COD, influent NH4
+-N, influent NO3
−-N, influent NO2
−-N, influent 
TIN, and NLR. The 7 output variables including effluent NH4
+-N, effluent NO3
−-N, effluent NO2
−-N, effluent 
TIN, NH4
+-N removal efficiency, TIN removal efficiency, and NARR were predicted separately to identify the 
relationships between input variables and the specific output variable. The selection of input variables was 
according to PPMC results.
To reduce prediction errors and avoid non-convergence of data, 2940 samples were randomly selected 
and divided into the training dataset (70%), validation dataset (15%), and testing dataset (15%) (Fig. 1). As a 
result, the training sample-size to the feature-size ratio (SFR) reached 138 (2070 training samples divided by

Standardization (mean and standard deviation) for numeric columns was embedded in the H2O AutoML

We followed the procedures introduced in our previous work22 to establish models by the H2O AutoML 
platform (Text S2). Briefly, various machine learning algorithms including Distributed Random Forest (DRF), 
Extremely Randomized Trees (XRT), Generalized Linear Model with regularization, eXtreme Gradient Boosting 
(XGBoost), gradient boosting machines (GBM), and deep neural networks were automatedly optimized and 
compared to form the modeling leaderboard (Fig. 1). Moreover, the combination of fast random search and 
fivefold cross-validation was applied to accelerate the training speed and enhance the model performance22. The 
proper tuning of numerous kinds of machine learning models is notoriously difficult and time-consuming19. The 
H2O AutoML platform was selected because it has high efficiency in both automated model development and 
model explanation30. Due to the high efficiency of the H2O AutoML platform, the maximum number of models 
was set as 200 and the maximum run time was set as 900 s. AutoML run will stop as soon as it hits either of these 
limits. Five different seeds were utilized for different data splitting because the data splitting may result in a huge 
discrepancy in the final prediction performance19. The best training, validation, and test performance of these 
candidate models were summarized based on mean absolute error (MAE) and Coefficient of Determination 
(R2).

Interpretable analysis
Interpretable methods including variable importance, one-dimensional (1D), and two-dimensional (2D) 
partial dependence plot (PDP) were utilized to analyze the optimal candidate models generated by the AutoML 
algorithm for each output variable. Variable importance is used to calculate the importance of sample features 
and quantitatively describe the contribution of features to classification or regression33. The PDP can show the 
functional relationship of one or two characteristic variables (1D PDP or 2D PDP) on the model’s prediction 
results34. In 2D PDP, the values of the two selected features are changed at the same time, while the values of the 
other features are fixed, and the prediction results are calculated to show the combined effect of the two features 
on the prediction results. The interpretable analysis was conducted on the Flow UI of H2O AutoML platform 
(https:​//h​2o-rele​as​e.s3.amaz​ona​ws.​com​/​h2​o​/​rel-​3.46.0/​6/d​ocs-​webs​ite/h2o-docs/flow.html).
Results and discussion
Statistical analysis of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes
To better understand the characteristics of data collected from the anammox-based processes, the box plots, 
EDA, and PPMC were utilized to analyze collected data (Fig. 2). There were 2940 samples collected from 28 
studies, which investigated different types of anammox-based processes and applied different operation 
conditions. Box plots illustrated the distribution of data from different groups. Outliers in all groups indicated 
occasional significant deviations among different experiments. As shown in Fig. 2(a), the sequencing batch 
experiments (median = 78.18%) showed a higher TIN removal efficiency than the continuous flow experiments 
(median = 74.81%). Meanwhile, more stable NLR, NiRR, NARR, and NRR were observed in the sequencing batch 
experiments. Extremely high NLR (2.91–7.55 kg N/m3/d), NiRR (0.33–2.53 kg N/m3/d), NARR (0.02–3.92 kg N/
m3/d) and NRR (2.63–6.55 kg N/m3/d) were found in Du et al. (2016)35 and Wang et al. (2024b)36 due to their 
high influent nitrogen concentration and short HRT in the UASB and expanded granular sludge bed reactor 
operating under continuous flow. This difference was also obtained between the floc experiments and aggregate 
experiments (Fig. 2(b)). The high NLR in continuous flow reactors operated by aggregates demonstrated that 
this combination had great potential to treat wastewater with high levels of nitrogen37,38.
For experiments using synthetic wastewater and municipal wastewater, the effluent properties were similar 
(Fig. S1(a)). However, the experiments using municipal wastewater had more stable NLR, NiRR, NARR, and 
NRR (Fig. S1(b)), suggesting that influent properties of municipal were analogous across different studies39–42. 
Instead, the synthetic wastewater utilized in different studies had a larger range of influent COD, NH4
+-N 
and NO3
−-N concentrations35,36,43,44. To further analyze the differences in influent properties of synthetic and 
municipal wastewater, the distribution of influent properties was visualized by the EDA approach (Fig. 2(c) 
and (d)). Data points in larger regions were observed in the C/N versus influent NH4
+-N, C/N versus influent 
TIN, influent COD versus influent NH4
+-N, and influent COD versus influent TIN in Fig. 2(d), indicating 
that the influent variables of synthetic wastewater were more concentrated and had less flocculation than that 
of municipal wastewater. In reality, the concentrations of pollutants in real wastewater normally fluctuate 
greatly45,46. Therefore, the experiments using synthetic wastewater need to further take into account the real 
composition and fluctuation of the municipal wastewater.
Notably, the data from different anammox-based process types (i.e., PNA, PDA, and PNA combined with 
PDA) presented high differences (Fig. S2). The PNA and PNA combined with PDA experiments had higher TIN 
removal efficiency than the PDA experiments (Fig. S2(a)). In all three types of anammox-based processes, the 
effluent NH4
+-N, effluent NO2
−-N, and effluent TIN were positively correlated to the influent NH4
+-N, influent 
TIN, and NLR, whereas the strongest positive correlation (0.55–0.95) was found in the PNA experiments 
(Fig. 2(e), (f) and (g)). Unlike PDA processes, the realization of PNA processes requires inhibition of nitrite-
oxidizing bacteria (NOB) activity and enhancement of ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB) activity47. The NOB 
and AOB activity have been found to be largely affected by the substrate concentrations (e.g., NO2
−-N, NH4
+-N, 
and COD)48,49. This may be the reason for the stronger correlation between influent and effluent variables in 
PNA experiments.
Moreover, the NiRR, NARR, and NRR had strong correlations with the variables involved in their calculations 
(Text S1). Meanwhile, the influent NH4
+-N and influent TIN had very similar pairwise relationships with other 
variables because NH4
+-N was extremely high in both synthetic and municipal wastewater. For real municipal 
wastewater, the NH4
+-N normally accounts for more than 90% of influent TIN42,50.
The PPMC for all categorical and numerical variables in the entire dataset is illustrated in Fig. S3. Strong 
pairwise relationships were found between the influent type and dominant anammox bacteria, influent NO3
−-N 
as well as influent TIN. This correlation further highlighted the differences between synthetic wastewater and 
municipal wastewater. Except for influent NH4
+-N and influent TIN, no other strong linear relationship was 
found between these potential input variables. Therefore, these variables could be utilized as the input variables 
for establishing machine learning models because they lacked potential multicollinearity19. The relationships

The best performance and optimized models for each output variable are summarized in Table 1 and the 
Supporting Information. GBM-like models (i.e., GBM and XGBoost) presented the advantages in predicting 
effluent NH4
+-N, effluent NO3
−-N, effluent NO2
−-N, effluent TIN, NH4
+-N removal efficiency, TIN removal 
efficiency, and NARR. GBM has been recognized as an ensemble and powerful supervised machine learning 
algorithm22,51. GBM demonstrates remarkable performance advantages owing to its integration of regularization 
techniques that effectively prevent overfitting. It also features built-in handling of missing values, efficient 
tree-pruning algorithms, and parallel processing capabilities. Additionally, it employs advanced methods for 
addressing imbalanced data. Collectively, these features enhance the model’s efficiency, accuracy, and robustness 
when dealing with complex data scenarios, making it an excellent choice for a wide range of machine learning 
tasks52. For instance, GBM also presented high prediction performance in simulating total nitrogen in the 
influent of WWTPs53 and energy consumption of WWTPs54.
The low training MAE and high training R2 (0.991–0.999) of these models demonstrated that the big dataset 
collected from literature-based anammox processes was useful for training machine learning models. The 
validation performance was weaker than the training performance, but it still fulfilled the prediction requirement 
(R2 = 0.727–0.981). Notably, the testing performance of these models (R2 = 0.814–0.993) was better than the 
validation performance because the modeling leaderboard was sorted based on the testing performance.

Practical implications and limitations
Forecasting the stabilization and operational performance of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes is 
essential for engineering operations, especially for treating municipal wastewater by anammox-based processes, 
which were usually unstable in practice39,70. By integrating and comparing the big data collected from different 
anammox-based studies, this study revealed the difference in data characteristics between experiments using 
sequencing batch reactor and continuous flow reactor, using floc and aggregate, using synthetic wastewater 
and municipal wastewater, as well as using different anammox-based process types. Results showed that the 
anammox-based continuous flow reactors operated by microbial aggregates had great potential to treat high 
levels of nitrogen pollutants. The big difference between the characteristics of synthetic wastewater and municipal 
wastewater applied in current experiments extremely weakened the practical meaning of anammox-based 
experiments based on synthetic wastewater30. Meanwhile, the PNA experiments and PNA combined with PDA 
experiments showed higher TIN removal efficiency than the PDA experiments. Stronger positive correlations 
(0.55–0.95) between influent and effluent nitrogen concentrations were found in the PNA experiments, implying 
the higher sensitivity of PNA processes to the influent characteristics.
Importantly, this study provided a new insight: the fast implementation and high accuracy of the AutoML 
algorithm for these important output variables (i.e., effluent NH4
+-N, effluent NO3
−-N, effluent NO2
−-N, 
effluent TIN, NH4
+-N removal efficiency, TIN removal efficiency, and NARR) makes it a useful tool to learn 
the information inside the anammox-based datasets, and predict the practical operation of anammox-based 
nitrogen removal processes. GBM and XGBoost models were identified as the most accurate machine learning 
models for predicting the effluent variables in the anammox-based processes (R2 = 0.814–0.993). The high 
accuracy of these models was not only achieved for data collected from literature but also confirmed by unseen 
data from our anammox-based experiment, which proved the excellent generalization ability in predicting the 
performance of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes.
Moreover, the potential improvement strategies for anammox-based processes could be proposed based 
on the interpretable results of optimal models generated by the AutoML algorithm. The appropriate ranges of 
key characteristics such as C/N, influent NH4
+-N, influent TIN, influent COD, HRT, and NLR were identified 
by the 1D and 2D PDP results. To reduce effluent NH4
+-N, it is recommended to keep C/N at 2.72–6.32 and 
influent TIN at 18.85–87.76 mg/L. NO3
−-N as the by-product of the anammox bacteria accounted for most of 
the effluent TIN in the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes68. Therefore, the conditions for reducing 
effluent NO3
−-N in anammox-based nitrogen removal processes deserve deep exploration. Specifically, this 
study found that the low effluent NO3
−-N could be achieved at COD of 200.42–305.91 mg/L, HRT of < 9.95 h and 
11.50–12.54 h, influent TIN of 59.38–87.76 mg/L, and NLR > 0.95 kg/m3/d. The effluent TIN and TIN removal

an appropriate mix of municipal wastewater and industrial wastewater may be helpful to achieve high NARR 
in mainstream anammox-based processes71. Integrating these data characteristics inside the big dataset could 
largely deepen the understanding of anammox-based nitrogen removal processes, and effectively reduce the 
difficulty of managing and operating them.
There are also some limitations existing in this study. First, the prediction accuracy of optimal models 
generated by the AutoML algorithm suffered from a slight decrease in the prediction of effluent NO2
−-N, effluent 
NO3
−-N, and NARR. This decreasing generalization ability may be due to the different operating conditions and 
microbial compositions in experiments between the training dataset collected from the literature and the unseen 
dataset collected from our study. Importantly, it was found that effluent NO2
−-N, effluent NO3
−-N, and NARR, 
as output variables, were more sensitive to the different operating conditions and microbial compositions in the 
anammox-based processes. The generalization ability of candidate models generated by the AutoML algorithm 
may be improved by including more essential input variables related to these output variables in the training 
datasets55.
Secondly, various organics with complex chemical structures exist in municipal wastewater. Among them, 
some substituents (e.g., −CH3, −OH) in organics impact the substrate diffusion and membrane stability by 
changing the hydrophobicity. Moreover, some substituents (e.g., −Cl, −F) in organics contribute to the biotoxicity 
and will inhibit the anammox activity72. However, COD was set as an index for representing all these organics in 
most anammox-based experiments fed with municipal wastewater. The missing concentrations of these sensitive 
organics in the datasets will limit the understanding of machine learning models for the effects of different 
organics on the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes.
On the other hand, although we included the dominant anammox genera in the dataset as a categorical variable, 
more variables related to microbes should be considered in future studies. Nowadays, the data size of microbes 
in these experiments is much smaller than the data size of influent and effluent variables. The appropriate ways 
to collect and utilize microbial data of anammox-based experiments should be further explored to overcome 
the insufficient data volume73. Employing machine learning algorithms to scrutinize these microbial datasets 
will not only facilitate a more profound comprehension of the microorganisms involved in anammox-based 
processes, but also offer valuable insights that enhance the nitrogen removal efficiency of practical anammox-
based systems for treating municipal wastewater52. Future directions for developing multifactor optimization 
strategies for anammox treatment should increasingly explore the valuable information hidden in microbial 
information. Ultimately, the fusion of high-dimensional microbial community fingerprints with process 
variables will enable the development of biology-informed digital twins, shifting the paradigm of anammox 
optimization from empirical hydraulic control toward precision microbiome management.
Conclusions
This study explored the response effects of important effluent variables to operation conditions and influent 
characteristics involved in the anammox-based nitrogen removal processes through the big data analysis and 
AutoML algorithm. Analysis of big data collected from the literature presented that the continuous flow reactors 
with anammox aggregates could address higher nitrogen loadings in municipal wastewater. High accuracy of 
the candidate models generated by the AutoML algorithm for these important output variables was achieved 
for data collected from both literature and unseen experiments. Moreover, the interpretable analysis of the 
optimal models clarified the 1D and 2D response effects between input and output variables. The appropriate 
ranges of key characteristics such as C/N, influent NH4
+-N, influent TIN, influent COD, HRT, and NLR were 
identified. These findings would deepen the understanding and support the practical operation of anammox-
based processes for treating municipal wastewater.
