2.1.1. Plant description and operational data source

This study analysed long-term operational data from a full-scale MBR 
system at the “D” Public Wastewater Treatment Plant in South Korea. 
The plant has a designed capacity of 25,000 m³ /day, and data were 
collected over a period exceeding one year. The dataset included daily 
average values for key water quality parameters (COD, TN, TP) and 
operational parameters (e.g., flow rates, aeration patterns, and tem­
peratures) from the influent and various process zones. The average 
influent concentrations were 15.50 mg/L COD, 27.91 mg/L TN, and 
1.80 mg/L TP. This comprehensive dataset was used to train and vali­
date the ML models for predicting pollutant removal performance.
2.1.2. Integrated MBR process configuration

The MBR unit primarily serves as a solid-liquid separation device, 
retaining biomass to ensure a high mixed liquor suspended solids 
(MLSS) concentration and producing a high-quality effluent. The overall 
process layout is illustrated in Fig. 1. The system incorporates two key 
modifications to a conventional MBR unit to address common limita­
tions such as oxygen imbalance and temperature sensitivity: 1) Swing 
basin for nitrification enhancement. 2) DO-R tank to prevent oxygen 
intrusion into the anoxic zone. The following sections detail the design 
and intended function of each unit, providing the process-level context 
for the operational data analyzed in this study. The primary target of 
these innovative sections is to enhance TN removal while maintaining a 
balanced removal of TP and COD.
2.1.3. Process unit design and functional rationale

2.1.3.1. Anaerobic zone. The anaerobic zone serves as the critical 
starting point for establishing the phosphorus removal process and 
strategically pre-conditioning the wastewater for subsequent nitrogen 
removal. Under strictly enforced oxygen-free and nitrate-free condi­
tions, a specialized microbial community known as Phosphate- 
Accumulating Organisms (PAOs) is selectively enriched [26,27]. These 
PAOs, unable to use oxygen or nitrate for respiration, instead hydrolyze 
their internally stored pools of polyphosphate to generate energy. This 
hydrolysis releases soluble phosphate into the surrounding mixed liquor. 
This metabolic sequence is fundamental: the release of phosphate here is 
not a failure but a prerequisite for the subsequent uptake of phosphorus 
in the aerobic zone. By sequestering a significant portion of the incoming 
soluble COD as PHA within the PAO cell mass, the anaerobic zone 
effectively "pre-packages" this carbon source, making it unavailable for 
ordinary heterotrophic bacteria in the downstream anoxic zone. These 
forces denitrifying bacteria to rely on more slowly degradable carbon 
sources, which reduces carbon competition. As a result, energy-rich 
polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) are preserved for the aerobic meta­
bolism of polyphosphate-accumulating organisms (PAOs), supporting 
their growth and enhancing biological phosphorus removal.

mary site for denitrification, which is the key process governing total

which can switch between metabolic pathways, utilize these oxidized

completeness of this process, it is paramount to minimize oxygen 
intrusion from adjacent aerobic zones, as the presence of DO would 
suppress the enzymes responsible for denitrification, causing the het­
erotrophic bacteria to preferentially utilize the more energetically 
favorable oxygen, halting nitrogen removal.
2.1.3.3. Swing basin. The swing basin is a zone with operational flexi­
bility, capable of being switched between anoxic and aerobic modes. 
This is typically controlled by turning the aeration system on or off. Its 
primary function is to act as a "buffer" for the nitrification process, which 
is carried out by autotrophic bacteria that are highly sensitive to low 
temperatures. In standard systems, low temperatures inhibit nitrifica­
tion, leading to ammonia accumulation in the effluent and ultimately, 
the failure of TN removal.
The primary role of the swing basin in TN removal is to ensure stable 
and complete nitrification, particularly during winter when low tem­
peratures suppress nitrifying activity in conventional aerobic zones. By 
operating the swing basin in aerobic mode under these conditions, the 
effective aeration volume and hydraulic retention time are increased, 
sustaining ammonia oxidation and preventing ammonia accumulation. 
This operational flexibility allows the swing basin to function as a pro­
cess buffer, compensating for reduced nitrifier activity and maintaining 
consistent nitrification performance. The nitrate produced is subse­
quently recycled to the anoxic zones, where it is reduced to inert N₂ gas 
through denitrification, enabling high and stable TN removal 
throughout the year.
The swing basin indirectly enhances COD removal by stabilizing 
nitrification, which prevents soluble organic nitrogen (e.g., ammonia) 
from contributing to effluent COD. Effective nitrification also eliminates 
the need for supplemental carbon addition to treat high ammonia levels, 
avoiding an increase in organic loading. Moreover, a stable nitrifying 
community supports the overall mineralization of organic matter, 
further improving COD reduction. For TP removal, the swing basin 
maintains stable nitrification and minimizes oxygen competition be­
tween nitrifiers and PAOs, allowing PAOs to efficiently uptake phos­
phorus in the aerobic zone and thereby enhancing TP removal.
2.1.3.4. Aerobic zone and MBR. In the aerobic reactor, continuous and 
vigorous aeration creates an environment that supports two critical, 
parallel biological processes essential for advanced treatment. Firstly, it 
facilitates nitrification, a two-step aerobic process in which autotrophic 
bacteria convert ammonium (NH₄⁺) to nitrate (NO₃⁻) via nitrite (NO₂⁻), 
making the nitrogen available for removal in the anoxic zone [30]. 
Simultaneously, this oxygen-rich environment fuels the metabolism of 
PAOs, which use their internally stored PHAs as an energy source to take 
up soluble phosphate from the mixed liquor at rates far exceeding their 
metabolic needs. This excess phosphate is then sequestered within their 
cell mass as polyphosphate, achieving the core objective of enhanced 
biological phosphorus removal.
2.1.3.5. DO reduction basin. This is a dedicated, non-aerated tank 
placed after the high-DO environment of the aerobic zone and MBR. The 
primary and most critical function of the DO-R tank is to enhance 
denitrification, leading to efficient TN removal. Denitrification is an 
anoxic process that requires the absence of free oxygen. When oxygen- 
rich mixed liquor is recycled directly from the aerobic zone/MBR to 
the anoxic zone, the facultative bacteria responsible for denitrification 
will preferentially use the free oxygen as an electron acceptor instead of 
nitrate (NO₃⁻) [31–34]. This oxygen poisoning of the anoxic zone 
severely inhibits denitrification, leading to elevated nitrate levels in the 
effluent. The DO-R tank directly addresses this by creating an environ­
ment for endogenous respiration, where microorganisms consume the 
residual DO in the absence of an external carbon source. This effectively 
strips DO from the recycle stream, ensuring that the anoxic zone receives 
a flow with minimal oxygen concentration. By securing the necessary 
anoxic conditions, the tank is fundamental to maximizing the denitrifi­
cation potential of the system.
The DO-R tank is essential for TP removal because it maintains a 
strictly anaerobic environment, preventing oxygen and nitrate intrusion 
that would disrupt PAO metabolism and inhibit phosphorus release and 
carbon uptake. By eliminating DO and nitrate, the tank enables proper 
initiation of the PAO metabolic cycle, leading to more effective biolog­
ical phosphorus removal and lower effluent TP. In addition, by 
enhancing denitrification and PAO activity, the DO-R tank ensures that 
limited influent carbon is efficiently utilized for anoxic and anaerobic

2.1.3.6. Return sludge step-feed system. This system provides dynamic 
control over the internal recycle of nitrate-rich mixed liquor from the 
aerobic zone. Instead of a single stream, it features separate lines that 
can send this flow to either the anaerobic zone or the swing/anoxic zone, 
with adjustable flow rates.
2.1.3.7. Influent bypass (Line 4). The influent bypass is an operational 
override mechanism designed to combat the common challenge of car­
bon limitation for denitrification directly. This line strategically diverts 
a portion of the raw influent, which is rich in readily biodegradable COD 
such as VFSs, directly to the anoxic zone, bypassing the preceding 
anaerobic reactor. This feature is explicitly activated when monitoring 
data indicates a drop in nitrate removal efficiency, signaling that the 
denitrifying bacteria in the anoxic zone are starved of electron donors.
