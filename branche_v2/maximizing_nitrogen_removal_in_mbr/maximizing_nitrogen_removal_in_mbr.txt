A B S T R A C T
Conventional membrane bioreactor (MBR) systems often experience reduced nitrification at low temperatures 
and limited denitrification under high dissolved oxygen conditions, resulting in insufficient total nitrogen (TN) 
removal. To overcome these limitations, this study proposes a novel configuration that integrates a swing basin 
to enhance nitrification during cold periods and a dissolved oxygen reduction (DO-R) tank to improve denitri­
fication by minimizing oxygen interference. Moreover, the complexity of the MBR system necessitated machine 
learning (ML)-based optimization to enhance TN removal while maintaining effective chemical oxygen demand 
(COD) and total phosphorus (TP) removal. Long-term operational data (>1 year) from a full-scale wastewater 
treatment plant in South Korea with a treatment capacity of 25,000 m³ /d were analyzed using ML algorithms. 
The random forest ML model outperformed the deep neural network, achieving a lower RMSE for TN prediction 
(1.73 vs. 2.81). Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis revealed that the swing basin enhances nitrifi­
cation by flexibly switching between aerobic and anoxic modes, thereby increasing the effective aerobic volume 
and hydraulic retention time under low-temperature conditions. In addition, the DO-R tank improved denitri­
fication by lowering DO levels, eliminating competition between oxygen and nitrate. The Harris Hawks bio- 
inspired optimization algorithm achieved the most accurate convergence, identifying an optimal DO-R tank 
sludge return flow rate of 146.23 m³ /d and yielding effluent COD, TN, and TP concentrations of 5.96, 5.26, and 
0.06 mg/L, respectively. Integrating the proposed configuration with ML-driven optimization provides a 
mechanism-based strategy for improving TN removal, significantly enhancing nitrogen removal in full-scale MBR 
systems.
1. Introduction
Municipal wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) integrate a 
sequence of physical, chemical, and biological processes to progressively 
remove pollutants from influent streams [1–4]. Among these, biological 
treatment is essential for the elimination of organic matter, nitrogen, 
and phosphorus via microbial transformations [5–7]. Over the past two 
decades, membrane bioreactor (MBR) technology has become an 
attractive alternative to conventional activated sludge systems due to its 
high treatment efficiency, small footprint, and ability to maintain high 
biomass concentrations [8–11]. Typically, MBRs operate through a 
combination of anaerobic, anoxic, and aerobic environments that sup­
port distinct microbial processes for nutrient and organic pollutant 
removal [12,13].
However, conventional MBR configurations frequently experience 
insufficient total nitrogen (TN) removal due to two interrelated

reducing nitrification during winter; and (ii) excessive dissolved oxygen 
(DO) in recycled mixed liquor inhibits denitrifiers, impairing denitrifi­

Numerous studies have attempted to overcome these shortcomings 
by modifying MBR configurations or operating conditions. For example, 
controlling free ammonia concentration was used to enrich nitrifica­
tion–denitrification (ND) microbial communities, achieving TN removal 
up to 97.12% with 33% lower carbon consumption [19]. An intermit­

denitrification under low chemical oxygen demand (COD)/N conditions,

While previous studies have advanced nitrogen removal in MBR 
systems, most have focused on optimizing isolated parameters, such as 
aeration mode, coagulant dosing, or carrier type, rather than on the 
synergistic integration of complementary strategies. This fragmented 
approach leaves critical challenges unaddressed. Specifically, low tem­
peratures inhibit nitrification, causing ammonia breakthrough and un­
stable TN removal [19,24]. Simultaneously, oxygen imbalance between 
aerobic and anoxic zones suppresses denitrification by exposing de­
nitrifiers to excessive DO [25]. These dual limitations are further com­
pounded by the frequent neglect of nitrogen-phosphorus interactions, 
particularly under carbon-limited conditions typical of industrial 
wastewater, where TN removal becomes even more challenging.
To address these challenges, the present study proposes a novel MBR 
configuration specifically designed to enhance nitrogen removal while 
maintaining high COD and total phosphorus (TP) removal. Given the 
influent characteristics of the studied industrial wastewater, nitrogen 
removal was prioritized in the design. The modified system incorporates 
two key engineering innovations, including swing basin and DO 
reduction (DO-R) tank: (i) The swing basin operates between anoxic to 
aerobic modes, allowing flexible adjustment of aerobic and anoxic vol­
umes to maintain effective nitrification under varying influent and 
temperature conditions. (ii) The DO-R tank temporarily halts aeration 
downstream of the aerobic zone, allowing microorganisms to lower DO 
and maintain the redox balance for efficient denitrification. This 
research presents a systematic approach for the evaluation and optimi­
zation of MBR performance, based on the integration of machine 
learning (ML) models with bio-inspired optimization algorithms. ML 
was utilized to model the complex and nonlinear relationships between 
operational conditions and treatment performance in the MBR system, 
which are difficult to describe using conventional mechanistic models. 
Random forest (RF) and deep neural network (DNN) models were 
developed to predict the simultaneous removal of TN, COD, and TP. 
Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was applied to enhance 
model interpretability and to quantify the contribution of each proposed 
innovation. Three bio-inspired algorithms were compared with the dif­
ferential evolution (DE) method to identify optimal operating conditions 
for maximizing nitrogen removal. While DE is a reliable and widely used 
optimization method, it typically requires more iterations and longer 
computation times. In contrast, bio-inspired algorithms offer faster 
calculation speeds, but their accuracy must be validated against a 
benchmark method. Therefore, this study compared the convergence 
results of each bio-inspired algorithm with those obtained from DE to 
identify which algorithm most closely approximates DE's optimal solu­
tion. The algorithm demonstrating the closest agreement with DE, while 
maintaining high computational efficiency, was selected as the most 
suitable optimization approach for practical MBR operation.
This work provides a comprehensive process and data-driven 
framework that combines engineering modifications, including swing 
basin and DO-R tank, with ML-based optimization. Unlike previous 
studies focusing on isolated operational adjustments, this study in­
troduces a synergistic process configuration validated at full scale and 
interprets its performance using explainable ML, enabling both opera­
tional optimization and mechanistic understanding. The proposed 
configuration enhances biological stability, ensures efficient pollutant 
removal under varying influent conditions, and contributes novel in­
sights into integrated process design for advanced MBR systems.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Full-scale MBR plant and data collection
2.1.1. Plant description and operational data source
This study analysed long-term operational data from a full-scale MBR 
system at the “D” Public Wastewater Treatment Plant in South Korea. 
The plant has a designed capacity of 25,000 m³ /day, and data were 
collected over a period exceeding one year. The dataset included daily 
average values for key water quality parameters (COD, TN, TP) and 
operational parameters (e.g., flow rates, aeration patterns, and tem­
peratures) from the influent and various process zones. The average 
influent concentrations were 15.50 mg/L COD, 27.91 mg/L TN, and 
1.80 mg/L TP. This comprehensive dataset was used to train and vali­
date the ML models for predicting pollutant removal performance.
2.1.2. Integrated MBR process configuration
The MBR unit primarily serves as a solid-liquid separation device, 
retaining biomass to ensure a high mixed liquor suspended solids 
(MLSS) concentration and producing a high-quality effluent. The overall 
process layout is illustrated in Fig. 1. The system incorporates two key 
modifications to a conventional MBR unit to address common limita­
tions such as oxygen imbalance and temperature sensitivity: 1) Swing 
basin for nitrification enhancement. 2) DO-R tank to prevent oxygen 
intrusion into the anoxic zone. The following sections detail the design 
and intended function of each unit, providing the process-level context 
for the operational data analyzed in this study. The primary target of 
these innovative sections is to enhance TN removal while maintaining a 
balanced removal of TP and COD.
2.1.3. Process unit design and functional rationale
2.1.3.1. Anaerobic zone. The anaerobic zone serves as the critical 
starting point for establishing the phosphorus removal process and 
strategically pre-conditioning the wastewater for subsequent nitrogen 
removal. Under strictly enforced oxygen-free and nitrate-free condi­
tions, a specialized microbial community known as Phosphate- 
Accumulating Organisms (PAOs) is selectively enriched [26,27]. These 
PAOs, unable to use oxygen or nitrate for respiration, instead hydrolyze 
their internally stored pools of polyphosphate to generate energy. This 
hydrolysis releases soluble phosphate into the surrounding mixed liquor. 
This metabolic sequence is fundamental: the release of phosphate here is 
not a failure but a prerequisite for the subsequent uptake of phosphorus 
in the aerobic zone. By sequestering a significant portion of the incoming 
soluble COD as PHA within the PAO cell mass, the anaerobic zone 
effectively "pre-packages" this carbon source, making it unavailable for 
ordinary heterotrophic bacteria in the downstream anoxic zone. These 
forces denitrifying bacteria to rely on more slowly degradable carbon 
sources, which reduces carbon competition. As a result, energy-rich 
polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) are preserved for the aerobic meta­
bolism of polyphosphate-accumulating organisms (PAOs), supporting 
their growth and enhancing biological phosphorus removal.
2.1.3.2. Anoxic zone. This zone operates under strictly anoxic condi­
tions, characterized by the absence of DO and the presence of nitrate

(NO₃⁻) and nitrite (NO₂⁻). Under these conditions, it serves as the pri­
mary site for denitrification, which is the key process governing total 
nitrogen removal [28,29]. Here, facultative heterotrophic bacteria, 
which can switch between metabolic pathways, utilize these oxidized 
nitrogen species as terminal electron acceptors for respiration replacing 
oxygen. In this biochemical reduction process, nitrate is sequentially 
transformed into a series of intermediates—nitrite, nitric oxide, and 
nitrous oxide—before its final conversion to harmless nitrogen gas (N₂), 
which is liberated into the atmosphere. To ensure the efficiency and 
completeness of this process, it is paramount to minimize oxygen 
intrusion from adjacent aerobic zones, as the presence of DO would 
suppress the enzymes responsible for denitrification, causing the het­
erotrophic bacteria to preferentially utilize the more energetically 
favorable oxygen, halting nitrogen removal.
2.1.3.3. Swing basin. The swing basin is a zone with operational flexi­
bility, capable of being switched between anoxic and aerobic modes. 
This is typically controlled by turning the aeration system on or off. Its 
primary function is to act as a "buffer" for the nitrification process, which 
is carried out by autotrophic bacteria that are highly sensitive to low 
temperatures. In standard systems, low temperatures inhibit nitrifica­
tion, leading to ammonia accumulation in the effluent and ultimately, 
the failure of TN removal.
The primary role of the swing basin in TN removal is to ensure stable 
and complete nitrification, particularly during winter when low tem­
peratures suppress nitrifying activity in conventional aerobic zones. By 
operating the swing basin in aerobic mode under these conditions, the 
effective aeration volume and hydraulic retention time are increased, 
sustaining ammonia oxidation and preventing ammonia accumulation. 
This operational flexibility allows the swing basin to function as a pro­
cess buffer, compensating for reduced nitrifier activity and maintaining 
consistent nitrification performance. The nitrate produced is subse­
quently recycled to the anoxic zones, where it is reduced to inert N₂ gas 
through denitrification, enabling high and stable TN removal 
throughout the year.
The swing basin indirectly enhances COD removal by stabilizing 
nitrification, which prevents soluble organic nitrogen (e.g., ammonia) 
from contributing to effluent COD. Effective nitrification also eliminates 
the need for supplemental carbon addition to treat high ammonia levels, 
avoiding an increase in organic loading. Moreover, a stable nitrifying 
community supports the overall mineralization of organic matter, 
further improving COD reduction. For TP removal, the swing basin 
maintains stable nitrification and minimizes oxygen competition be­
tween nitrifiers and PAOs, allowing PAOs to efficiently uptake phos­
phorus in the aerobic zone and thereby enhancing TP removal.
2.1.3.4. Aerobic zone and MBR. In the aerobic reactor, continuous and 
vigorous aeration creates an environment that supports two critical, 
parallel biological processes essential for advanced treatment. Firstly, it 
facilitates nitrification, a two-step aerobic process in which autotrophic 
bacteria convert ammonium (NH₄⁺) to nitrate (NO₃⁻) via nitrite (NO₂⁻), 
making the nitrogen available for removal in the anoxic zone [30]. 
Simultaneously, this oxygen-rich environment fuels the metabolism of 
PAOs, which use their internally stored PHAs as an energy source to take 
up soluble phosphate from the mixed liquor at rates far exceeding their 
metabolic needs. This excess phosphate is then sequestered within their 
cell mass as polyphosphate, achieving the core objective of enhanced 
biological phosphorus removal.
2.1.3.5. DO reduction basin. This is a dedicated, non-aerated tank 
placed after the high-DO environment of the aerobic zone and MBR. The 
primary and most critical function of the DO-R tank is to enhance 
denitrification, leading to efficient TN removal. Denitrification is an 
anoxic process that requires the absence of free oxygen. When oxygen- 
rich mixed liquor is recycled directly from the aerobic zone/MBR to 
the anoxic zone, the facultative bacteria responsible for denitrification 
will preferentially use the free oxygen as an electron acceptor instead of 
nitrate (NO₃⁻) [31–34]. This oxygen poisoning of the anoxic zone 
severely inhibits denitrification, leading to elevated nitrate levels in the 
effluent. The DO-R tank directly addresses this by creating an environ­
ment for endogenous respiration, where microorganisms consume the 
residual DO in the absence of an external carbon source. This effectively 
strips DO from the recycle stream, ensuring that the anoxic zone receives 
a flow with minimal oxygen concentration. By securing the necessary 
anoxic conditions, the tank is fundamental to maximizing the denitrifi­
cation potential of the system.
The DO-R tank is essential for TP removal because it maintains a 
strictly anaerobic environment, preventing oxygen and nitrate intrusion 
that would disrupt PAO metabolism and inhibit phosphorus release and 
carbon uptake. By eliminating DO and nitrate, the tank enables proper 
initiation of the PAO metabolic cycle, leading to more effective biolog­
ical phosphorus removal and lower effluent TP. In addition, by 
enhancing denitrification and PAO activity, the DO-R tank ensures that 
limited influent carbon is efficiently utilized for anoxic and anaerobic

2.1.3.6. Return sludge step-feed system. This system provides dynamic 
control over the internal recycle of nitrate-rich mixed liquor from the

can send this flow to either the anaerobic zone or the swing/anoxic zone,

2.1.3.7. Influent bypass (Line 4). The influent bypass is an operational 
override mechanism designed to combat the common challenge of car­
bon limitation for denitrification directly. This line strategically diverts 
a portion of the raw influent, which is rich in readily biodegradable COD 
such as VFSs, directly to the anoxic zone, bypassing the preceding 
anaerobic reactor. This feature is explicitly activated when monitoring 
data indicates a drop in nitrate removal efficiency, signaling that the 
denitrifying bacteria in the anoxic zone are starved of electron donors.
2.2. Data cleaning and normalization
The dataset was first preprocessed to ensure consistency and us­
ability for ML models. After data cleaning and preprocessing, a total of 
2191 observations remained and were used for the ML analysis. All 
feature and target variables were checked for formatting issues, such as 
commas or whitespace in numeric fields, which were removed to allow 
for conversion into floating-point numbers. Any missing or invalid 
values were imputed using the mean strategy through the SimpleIm­
puter function, thereby preventing data loss while maintaining statisti­
cal consistency [35,36]. To improve comparability across features with 
different scales and units, standardization was applied using the 
StandardScaler, which transforms the features into zero mean and unit 
variance. This normalization step ensures that each variable contributes 
equally to model training and avoids bias towards features with larger 
numerical ranges.
The operating parameters used in the ML modeling of the MBR 
system are provided in Table 1. The selection of these 18 feature vari­
ables was further justified by their direct relevance to the study's core 
research objectives. Temperature parameters across the anaerobic, 
anoxic, and aerobic zones were specifically included to capture seasonal 
suppression of nitrifying activity, directly addressing the low- 
temperature nitrification limitation identified as a key research gap 
[37,38]. Oxidation-reduction potential (ORP) and DO measurements 
across all stages were designated to quantify the redox conditions gov­
erning denitrification efficiency and PAO metabolism, thereby enabling 
investigation of the oxygen imbalance problem [39,40]. Mixed liquor 
volatile suspended solids (MLVSS) were incorporated alongside MLSS to 
distinguish active microbial biomass from inert solids, providing a more 
accurate indicator of the biological potential for nutrient removal. 
Sludge volume index (SVI) was included to monitor sludge settleability, 
as poor settling can lead to biomass washout and deteriorated effluent 
quality in MBR systems [41–43]. The return sludge flow rate (Sludge-R) 
was specifically selected because it controls the recycle of nitrate-rich 
mixed liquor to the anoxic zone, directly influencing denitrification 
capacity. By systematically including parameters that capture both 
environmental conditions and biological state variables, the feature set 
ensures that the ML models learn the underlying process mechanisms 
rather than merely fitting correlations, enhancing model interpretability 
and enabling meaningful SHAP analysis of each innovation's 
contribution.
2.3. Implementation of machine learning algorithms
Two types of ML approaches were employed: Random Forest Re­
gressor (RFR) and DNN. A RFR is an ensemble ML algorithm that pre­
dicts continuous values by combining the outputs of many decision 
trees. Each tree is trained on a random subset of the data and features, 
and its predictions are averaged to reduce overfitting and improve ac­
curacy [44]. DNNs are computational models inspired by the structure 
of the human brain, consisting of layers of interconnected nodes (neu­
rons) that process information [45]. They learn patterns from data by 
adjusting the weights of connections through training, typically using 
backpropagation to minimize prediction errors. DNNs can model com­
plex, non-linear relationships, making them powerful tools for regres­
sion, classification, and pattern recognition.

2.5. Model performance evaluation
The root mean square error (RMSE) values for the 5-fold cross- 
validation data were calculated to evaluate the model accuracy. In 
cross-validation, the dataset is systematically partitioned into several 
subsets (or “folds”), and the model is trained on a portion of the data 
while being tested on the remaining unseen portion [47]. This process is 
repeated multiple times so that every subset serves as a test set once, and 
the overall performance is averaged across all folds. Such an approach 
provides a reliable and unbiased assessment by minimizing the risk of 
overfitting and ensuring that the model generalizes well to independent 
data. This makes cross-validation a more effective and robust method 
compared to a single train–test split. Furthermore, the MAE and MSE 
values were also calculated to provide additional validation of the model 
performance, ensuring consistency across multiple error metrics and 
offering a more comprehensive evaluation of prediction accuracy.
2.6. Explainable machine learning models
The best-performing model was selected and further analyzed using 
explainable ML methods, including SHAP and Partial Dependence Plots 
(PDP), to investigate the interactive effects and combined contributions 
of the input features [48]. These methods clearly revealed how each 
experimental variable influenced the target response, enhancing the 
model's transparency and interpretability. By applying explainable ML, 
the study benefited from increased trust in the predictions, improved 
accountability and reproducibility, identification of potential biases or 
overfitting, and guidance for optimizing experimental conditions to 
achieve maximum performance.
2.7. Process optimization
During optimization, the process variables were adjusted to reduce 
the effluent concentrations of COD, TN, and TP, thereby maximizing the 
system's overall treatment efficiency. Based on the most accurate ML 
algorithms, the DE method, as a reliable benchmark, was compared with 
three recently developed bio-inspired optimization algorithms, 
including the Slime Mould Algorithm (SMA), the Mayfly Optimization 
Algorithm (MOA), and the Harris Hawks Optimization (HHO). These 
algorithms are inspired by distinct biological processes and incorporate 
different search mechanisms, allowing a comprehensive evaluation of 
their effectiveness in minimizing multiple targets in complex, nonlinear 
systems. Fig. 2 illustrates the concepts of the optimization algorithms 
used in this study, represented through their underlying natural 
behaviors.
DE is an evolutionary optimization technique that employs mutation, 
crossover, and selection operators to evolve a population of candidate 
solutions iteratively. It generates diversity by perturbing individuals 
with scaled differences of randomly chosen solutions, ensuring robust 
exploration of the search space [49]. DE is well recognized for its 
simplicity, low computational cost, and stable convergence behavior, 
making it one of the most widely adopted classical optimizers. Although 
its convergence can be slower compared with newer metaheuristics, 
DE’s key strength lies in its robustness and reliability across a broad 
range of continuous optimization problems, establishing it as a 
dependable baseline for comparison.
SMA is inspired by the oscillatory foraging behavior of slime mould, 
where adaptive movements are governed by positive and negative 
feedback mechanisms [50]. It utilizes bio-oscillation equations to update 
the positions of search agents, thereby dynamically balancing explora­
tion and exploitation. By adjusting the search strategy according to the 
fitness of candidate solutions, SMA demonstrates strong capability in 
avoiding local minima. It is characterized by global search efficiency, 
rapid convergence, and competitive optimization accuracy. Owing to its 
novelty and flexibility, SMA is particularly suitable for tackling 
high-dimensional and complex engineering or environmental optimi­
zation problems.
MOA mimics the swarming and mating behavior of mayflies, inte­
grating attraction, mating, and flight dynamics into its search strategy 
[51]. The algorithm divides the population into male and female may­
flies, with males performing attractive flights toward superior solutions 
and females attracted to males for mating. Offspring are generated 
through recombination processes, maintaining population diversity and 
preventing premature convergence. MOA achieves a balanced trade-off 
between exploration and exploitation, resulting in stable convergence 
performance. Its principal advantages include flexibility, high accuracy, 
and applicability to multi-objective and nonlinear optimization 
problems.
HHO is inspired by the cooperative hunting strategies of Harris 
Hawks, particularly their surprise pounce and dynamic prey-escaping 
mechanisms [52]. The algorithm models different hunting phases, 
including exploration, transition, and exploitation, based on the 
escaping energy of the prey, thus adaptively shifting between global and 
local search modes. This dynamic adaptation enables HHO to effectively 
avoid local optima and accelerate convergence toward the global opti­
mum. Key features include adaptive search capability, efficient 
convergence, and robustness in navigating complex multimodal land­
scapes. HHO has shown particular strength in solving challenging 
optimization problems where maintaining exploration–exploitation 
balance is critical.

selected parameters vary greatly in scale, including DO-Ax (0.1–0.5 mg/ 
L), ORP-Ax (–400–400 mV), Tem-An (10–24 ◦C), DO-R (0.05–0.48 mg/ 
L), Sludge-R (19–905 m³/d), and MLVSS-Ae (2000–8000 mg/L). This 
variability necessitates data normalization or standardization to prevent 
parameters with larger magnitudes from disproportionately influencing 
the model. The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) ranges from –1 to + 1 
values which indicate the direction and strength of the linear relation­
ship between variables. Positive correlations signify direct associations, 
while negative values reflect inverse trends. Despite their numerically 
weak values, several correlations hold high process-level significance. 
This reflects the complex interdependencies inherent in the multistage 
MBR configuration, which includes a swing basin and a DO-R tank.
Nitrification and denitrification performance are closely governed by 
redox regulation across the system, as indicated by the positive corre­
lation between TN and oxidation–reduction potential in the anoxic tank 
(ORP-Ax, r = 0.31). This moderate correlation suggests that maintain­
ing stable redox conditions is critical for effective nitrogen trans­
formation, rather than implying direct linear control. The swing basin 
supports this regulation by flexibly switching between aerobic and 
anoxic modes, buffering redox fluctuations. Extending the aerobic 
duration at low temperatures enhances nitrification efficiency by 
providing sufficient time for ammonia-oxidizing activity [53]. In 
contrast, dissolved oxygen reduction (DO-R, r = −0.04) and sludge re­
turn flow (Sludge-R, r = −0.04) exhibit weak but negative correlations 
with TN, reflecting their indirect roles in limiting oxygen intrusion and 
regulating biomass distribution within the anoxic zone.
A moderate positive correlation between temperature in the anoxic 
zone (Tem-Ax, r = 0.30) and COD removal suggests that temperature- 
dependent microbial activity enhances organic matter degradation, 
with the swing basin indirectly supporting this effect by stabilizing up­
stream nitrification and redox conditions through flexible aero­
bic–anoxic operation. In contrast, the weak positive correlation between 
sludge return from the DO-R basin (Sludge-R, r = 0.07) and COD 
removal indicates limited linear dependence. At the process level, the 
DO-R basin conditions the return sludge under low-DO conditions to 
enhance its heterotrophic activity. This preconditioned sludge subse­
quently improves COD removal efficiency in downstream treatment by 
utilizing organic matter more effectively while minimizing oxygen 
interference in anoxic zones [54].
The weak negative correlation between dissolved oxygen in the DO- 
R basin (DO-R, r = −0.16) and TP removal shows a limited linear 
dependence. A moderate negative correlation between MLSS-Ax 
(r = −0.37) and TP removal indicates that elevated biomass concen­
trations in the anoxic zone may hinder phosphorus removal efficiency. 
Residual oxygen in this zone can disrupt the anaerobic–anoxic condi­
tions required for effective biological phosphorus removal. The presence 
of oxygen suppresses the metabolism of polyphosphate-accumulating 
organisms, impairing the phosphorus release–uptake cycle essential 
for efficient removal [55]. The swing basin partially offsets this limita­
tion by flexibly regulating aerobic–anoxic conditions, stabilizing 
polyphosphate-accumulating organism activity and preventing exces­
sive biomass accumulation in the anoxic zone.

3.3.1. SHAP analysis
The SHAP interaction ranking plots are provided to illustrate the 
effects of key operational parameters associated with the swing basin 
and DO-R tank on TN (Fig. 7a). Among swing-basin-related parameters, 
anoxic-zone temperature (Tem-Ax) is identified as an influential factor 
affecting nitrification and TN removal. SHAP results indicate that low 
temperatures exert strong negative contributions to TN removal, 
particularly under winter conditions, with SHAP values for the TN target 
ranging from −0.23 to 0.1.05. However, the magnitude of this negative 
temperature effect is substantially reduced when swing-basin operation 
is employed. Extending the aerobic section enhances nitrification by 
providing sufficient DO and reaction time for ammonia-oxidizing and 
nitrite-oxidizing bacteria to convert ammonium into nitrate [57,58]. 
Adequate oxygen availability supports the activity of nitrifying micro­
organisms and promotes stable nitrification kinetics in biological treat­
ment systems.
DO-R and Sludge-R parameters control denitrification efficiency and, 
consequently, overall TN removal. At high DO-R values, SHAP contri­
butions approach zero, indicating stabilized total nitrogen removal. 
Denitrifying bacteria preferentially utilize oxygen as an electron 
acceptor when it is present, which suppresses the biological reduction of 
nitrate to nitrogen gas [59,60]. Therefore, elevated residual oxygen 
inhibits denitrification activity and reduces overall nitrogen removal

4. Conclusion
This study demonstrates that integrating a swing basin with a DO-R 
tank provides an effective strategy for improving TN removal in full- 
scale MBR systems while maintaining stable COD and TP removal. The 
swing basin improved nitrification by extending aerobic conditions, 
while the DO-R tank enhanced denitrification by reducing residual DO 
in recycled sludge. The ML analysis of long-term operational data 
showed that the RF model outperformed the DNN algorithm in pre­
dicting COD, TN, and TP concentrations. The HHO optimization algo­
rithm produced results closest to those of DE among the evaluated 
methods. This performance was achieved at a Sludge-R value of 
146.23 m³ /d and a DO-R value of 0.20 mg/L. Beyond the specific case 
study, this work reveals how integrating process engineering in­
novations with explainable ML and bio-inspired optimization can pro­
vide a general framework for understanding, predicting, and improving 
the performance of complex biological wastewater treatment systems. 
One limitation of this study is that the results are based on operational 
data from a single full-scale wastewater treatment plant, which may 
limit the generalizability of the ML models and optimization outcomes 
to systems with different influent characteristics or operational condi­
tions. Future research should evaluate the proposed framework across 
multiple full-scale plants to confirm its applicability under different 
operational and influent conditions. Nevertheless, the proposed frame­
work provides practical guidance for improving nutrient removal and 
offers a scalable strategy for optimizing MBR operation under variable 
environmental conditions.
