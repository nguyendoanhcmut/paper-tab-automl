A B S T R A C T
The electrochemical oxidation of perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), a persistent and toxic environmental pollutant, 
presents significant operational complexities due to nonlinear interactions among numerous process variables. 
This study introduces a fully automated, robust machine learning (ML) framework based on Fast Lightweight 
AutoML (FLAML), integrated with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to rigorously optimize and interpret 
PFOA electrochemical oxidation performance. The FLAML-optimized XGBoost model demonstrated exceptional 
predictive accuracy (RMSE=3.97, R2=0.98), significantly outperforming systematically tuned traditional ML 
models such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and Deep Learning architectures. Rigorous statistical vali­
dation confirmed the superior generalizability and stability of the FLAML-optimized model, while SHAP-based 
interpretability analyses revealed electrolysis time and anode material as primary drivers of treatment effi­
cacy, with electrolyte concentration and current density forming the next tier; consistent with established 
electrochemical degradation kinetics. Notably, FLAML-optimized XGBoost achieved a 72 % reduction in 
computational overhead compared to manually tuned models, establishing a reproducible, interpretable, and 
computationally efficient benchmark in environmental ML applications. The framework provides a systematic 
approach that improves predictive accuracy, enhances interpretability, and strengthens practical applicability to 
support optimization of remediation technologies and environmental decision-making.
1. Introduction
Perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), a legacy member of the per- and 
polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) family, is among the most persistent 
environmental pollutants identified to date. Its exceptional chemical 
stability, rooted in the strength of its carbon–fluorine bonds, has enabled 
widespread contamination of aquatic and terrestrial ecosystems [1,2]. 
PFOA’s bioaccumulation and its links to immunotoxicity, liver 
dysfunction, cardiovascular disease, and cancer have triggered esca­
lating regulatory scrutiny [3,4]. The International Agency for Research 
on Cancer now classifies PFOA as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) [5], 
and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency has set maximum 
contaminant levels as low as 4 ng/L in drinking water [6]. These de­
velopments have catalyzed an urgent search for effective, scalable, and 
sustainable remediation technologies capable of not just removing, but 
fully mineralizing PFOA into benign end products such as carbon di­
oxide and fluoride ions. This challenge mirrors a broader trajectory in 
wastewater treatment, where established processes are being optimized 
to more effectively degrade persistent industrial pollutants [7,8].
Electrochemical oxidation (EO) has emerged as one of the most 
promising destructive technologies for PFAS remediation, offering the 
potential to mineralize PFOA into benign end products such as carbon 
dioxide and fluoride ions [9,10]. The process operates through two 
principal degradation pathways: direct electron transfer at the anode 
surface, which generates perfluoroalkyl radicals and initiates 
chain-shortening reactions, and hydroxyl radical-mediated oxidation via 
water electrolysis [11,12]. However, optimizing EO systems presents 
formidable challenges due to the complex interplay of multiple opera­
tional parameters, including current density, electrode spacing, elec­
trolyte composition, pH, temperature, initial PFOA concentration, 
electrolysis time, and water matrix characteristics [13-15]. The high 
dimensionality and nonlinear interactions among these variables create 
a multifaceted optimization landscape where changes in one parameter 
can significantly influence the effectiveness of others, rendering

Machine learning (ML) approaches have demonstrated significant 
potential for addressing optimization challenges in electrochemical 
systems for water treatment applications [17,18]. Recent studies have 
successfully applied various ML algorithms to predict electrochemical 
process performance, with applications ranging from general electro­
chemical oxidation processes to specific PFAS treatment scenarios [19, 
20]. Advanced ML frameworks have shown particular promise in elec­
trochemical process optimization, with studies demonstrating the ability 
to achieve predictive accuracies exceeding R2 = 0.84 while providing 
mechanistic insights through feature importance analysis [17]. The 
integration of interpretability tools such as SHAP (SHapley Additive 
exPlanations) has enabled researchers to extract actionable insights 
from complex electrochemical datasets, revealing key parameter re­
lationships and guiding process optimization strategies [17,21]. How­
ever, most existing ML applications in this domain have relied on 
conventional modeling approaches requiring extensive manual hyper­
parameter tuning and expert-driven algorithm selection, limiting their 
accessibility and scalability for environmental practitioners [18,22,23].
Automated machine learning (AutoML) frameworks represent a 
transformative approach to data-driven process optimization by sys­
tematically exploring algorithm selection, hyperparameter optimiza­
tion, and feature engineering without human intervention [24-26]. 
FLAML (Fast Lightweight AutoML), developed by Microsoft Research, 
implements cost-aware Bayesian optimization that dynamically allo­
cates computational resources while prioritizing low-complexity models 
for rapid convergence [27]. Recent applications of AutoML in environ­
mental modeling have demonstrated the ability to significantly reduce 
computational cost while achieving predictive performance that sur­
passes traditional, manually tuned models [28,29]. For instance, 
gradient boosting algorithms selected via AutoML outperformed con­
ventional neural networks in predicting the impacts of microplastics on 
methane production in anaerobic digestion (MSE = 17.0 vs. 58.0) [29], 
while AutoML models also successfully captured complex antibiotic 
removal dynamics in constructed wetlands across varying training du­
rations (MAE = 9.94–13.68, R2 = 0.780–0.877) [28]. The integration of 
efficient AutoML frameworks such as FLAML with explainable AI tech­
niques like SHAP directly addresses two critical requirements of envi­
ronmental engineering applications: delivering high predictive accuracy 
and generating transparent, interpretable insights that support regula­
tory compliance, operational optimization, and stakeholder confidence 
[28,29].
In this study, a reproducible and interpretable AutoML workflow is 
developed for electrochemical oxidation of PFOA. The aim is to show 
that cost-aware automated model selection and tuning (FLAML), 
coupled with SHAP-based explanation, can deliver (i) reliable predictive 
performance, (ii) transparent, model-based attribution of operational 
drivers, and (iii) a portable pipeline that reduces manual trial-and-error. 
Use of a previously curated dataset enables direct comparability while 
isolating the methodological contribution (automation plus interpret­
ability). Throughout, FLAML is treated as an optimization framework 
distinct from the final predictive learner (XGBoost), and performance is 
evaluated under repeated holdout with statistical testing and learning- 
curve diagnostics to assess generalization.
2. Methodology
2.1. Dataset characteristics and preprocessing
This study leverages a dataset originally curated by Alnaimat et al. 
[30], which captures the electrochemical degradation of PFOA under 
varied conditions. The dataset comprises unique treatment scenarios, 
each defined by a combination of operational parameters and environ­
mental contexts. Key independent variables include electrolysis dura­
tion, current density, anode and cathode material types, electrolyte 
concentration and composition, and the water matrix. Exploratory an­
alyses were performed to characterize the inputs and the target prior to 
modeling. The dependence structure among predictors is shown in 
Figure S1 (Spearman rank-correlation heatmap with significance 
marks). Bivariate relationships are visualized in Figure S2 (pairwise 
scatterplots with LOWESS fits and Pearson/Spearman annotations). The 
target distribution, together with a Shapiro–Wilk normality diagnostic, 
appears in Figure S3. Several electrochemical variables exhibit moder­
ate positive association (for example, electrolysis time with current 
density; |ρ|≈0.35–0.55), reflecting typical experimental co-variation. 
Preprocessing followed algorithm-appropriate conventions. Contin­
uous variables were standardized (z-score) for distance- and 
gradient-based baselines to ensure numerical stability and commensu­
rate scaling, whereas tree-based learners were trained on unscaled in­
puts given the scale-invariance of split criteria. Categorical descriptors 
(anode, cathode, electrolyte, water matrix) were one-hot encoded to 
maintain parity across baseline algorithms and to permit later aggre­
gation by parent feature in model interpretation. Cardinalities were 
small (≤6 per field), limiting sparsity and dimensionality.
2.2. Data splitting and evaluation protocol
Model evaluation used an 80:20 holdout stratified on anode type to 
mitigate covariate shift arising from uneven anode distribution across 
the dataset. The target variable (removal, %) was not stratified, 
consistent with the regression setting. A fixed random seed (42) was 
used for reproducibility. Stability of this protocol across repeated runs 
and alternative split realizations is documented in Figure S4 (stratifi­
cation sensitivity), and the internal model-selection procedure and 
performance consistency are summarized in Figure S5 (nested cross- 
validation diagnostics)
2.3. Automated model selection via FLAML
Automated model and hyperparameter selection was conducted with 
FLAML, a cost-aware AutoML framework that replaces manual, grid- 
style tuning with budgeted, early-stopped search. A wall-time budget 
of 300 s was enforced for FLAML’s search. FLAML initializes with 
inexpensive configurations, adaptively allocates trials toward promising 
learner–hyperparameter regions, and prunes underperforming runs 
when validation error plateaus within the allotted budget. The optimi­
zation objective was 5-fold cross-validation RMSE, with early stopping 
applied at both the boosting-iteration level (for iterative learners) and 
the trial level (for unpromising configurations). Convergence was 
declared when no improvement was observed within the budget win­
dow and the incumbent remained unchanged across successive evalua­
tions. The best-performing model selected by FLAML was an XGBoost 
regressor (learning rate = 0.1403; n_estimators = 379; max_leaves = 13; 
L1/L2 regularization α = 0.0995, λ = 0.0010), with subsample = 0.85 
and colsample_bytree = 0.83. This configuration balances bias–variance 
by combining a moderate learning rate with constrained tree complexity 
and regularization. The full search space and the selected hyper­
parameters for XGBoost and all baseline learners are summarized in SI 
Table S1.
2.4. Comparative benchmarking and statistical validation
To contextualize performance, the FLAML-optimized XGBoost model 
was benchmarked against five commonly used regressors: Decision 
Trees (DT), Random Forests (RF), Gradient Boosting Decision Trees 
(GBDT), Deep Learning (DL), and k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN). Each 
model was evaluated on the same one-hot encoded dataset and sub­
jected to identical stratified train-test splits to ensure methodological

parity. Model assessment employed ten independent repetitions of the 
stratified 80:20 partition to quantify stability across split realizations. 
For each repetition, five metrics were computed: root mean squared 
error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage 
error (MAPE), correlation coefficient (CC; Pearson’s r), and coefficient 
of determination (R²). RMSE, MAE, and MAPE quantify error magni­
tude, whereas CC (r) and R² summarize association and explained 
variance. Statistical significance of pairwise differences was evaluated 
with paired two-tailed t-tests (α = 0.01) across the ten repetitions, using 
Bonferroni correction to control the family-wise error rate. Cohen’s 
d was additionally reported to quantify effect sizes. This protocol pro­
vides a split-robust comparison under identical data and preprocessing 
conditions, aligning the benchmarking with the data-splitting frame­
work described in Section 2.2.
2.5. Interpretability via SHAP analysis
Model interpretability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPla­
nations (SHAP), which attribute to each feature its marginal contribu­
tion to individual predictions under the cooperative game–theoretic 
formulation. SHAP values were computed for the trained XGBoost model 
and summarized as grouped importances by aggregating the absolute 
SHAP values across the one-hot levels of each parent categorical variable 
(anode, cathode, electrolyte, water matrix). Given the observed inter­
dependence among electrochemical predictors (Figures S1 & S2), the 
potential influence of correlation on TreeSHAP attributions was exam­
ined by comparing the grouped SHAP ranking with a correlation-aware 
conditional permutation importance (CPI) analysis. Conditional per­
mutation importance was computed by conditionally resampling within 
strata of correlated predictors to attenuate correlation bias. Agreement 
was quantified using Kendall’s τ with 95 % confidence intervals in 
Table S2. Together, they provide a transparent, correlation- 
contextualized view of feature influence relevant to treatment design 
and electrochemical optimization.
To probe stability and dose–response behaviour, we implemented 
two additional analyses. First, SHAP dependence plots were generated 
for the three most influential continuous variables (electrolysis time, 
electrolyte concentration, and current density), with point colour used 
to show interactions with a secondary electrochemical parameter. Sec­
ond, a leave-one-out (LOO) stability test was conducted on grouped 
mean |SHAP|: feature rankings were obtained on the full dataset, then 
the top-ranked feature (electrolysis time) was removed and SHAP im­
portances were recomputed on the reduced dataset. Agreement between 
the two rankings was summarized using Kendall’s τ.
2.6. Reproducibility and deployment considerations
All computations were executed in Python 3.10 using version- 
controlled dependencies: FLAML 1.2.0, SHAP 0.44.0, and scikit-learn 
1.4.0. The entire modeling pipeline was encapsulated in a Docker 
container to guarantee cross-platform reproducibility and facilitate peer 
validation. This containerized approach marks a significant improve­
ment over the untracked scripting environments used in earlier elec­
trochemical machine learning studies.
2.7. Methodological contributions and distinctions
Relative to Alnaimat et al. [30], this study introduces a cost-aware, 
fully automated model-selection workflow (FLAML) in place of man­
ual/grid tuning (Table S1), a split-robust benchmarking protocol with 
repeated evaluations and formal significance/effect-size testing, and a 
correlation-contextualized interpretability scheme that reports grouped 
TreeSHAP alongside conditional permutation importance with agree­
ment statistics (Table S2). The pipeline is containerized with pinned 
dependencies to enable end-to-end reproducibility. Taken together, 
these elements provide a transparent, efficient, and reproducible 
methodology tailored to electrochemical treatment datasets, clarifying 
performance gains and interpretability under documented assumptions 
and validation settings.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Model performance and statistical validation
The FLAML-optimized XGBoost model demonstrated exceptional 
predictive performance in modeling PFOA electrochemical oxidation 
efficiency, significantly outperforming all baseline models across every 
evaluation metric (Table 1). The FLAML-optimized XGBoost achieved an 
RMSE of 3.97 ± 0.45 and R2 of 0.98 ± 0.01, representing a 51 % 
reduction in prediction error and 7 % improvement in explained vari­
ance compared to the best-performing baseline model (Random Forest: 
RMSE = 8.05 ± 1.02, R2 = 0.91 ± 0.02). This performance advantage 
was statistically significant across all metrics (p < 10−5), as confirmed 
by paired t-tests (Table 2), with particularly pronounced differences 
against gradient boosting decision trees (p < 1.1 × 10−8 for RMSE) and 
deep learning approaches (p < 2.8 × 10−9 for RMSE). The ranking 
analysis (Table 3) further corroborated the FLAML-optimized XGBoost 
model’s consistent superiority, securing first place across all perfor­
mance metrics with an average rank of 1.0, while Random Forest ranked 
second (average rank = 2.2).
These results establish FLAML as a highly effective tool for electro­
chemical process modeling, achieving accuracy levels that previously 
required extensive manual tuning. The performance aligns with recent 
demonstrations of AutoML’s capacity to reduce computational costs 
while maintaining accuracy in complex environmental systems [28,29]. 
Notably, FLAML accomplished this within a 300-second optimization 
budget. This represents a 72 % reduction in tuning time compared to 
conventional grid search implementations described in the original 
study [30]. This efficiency addresses critical computational barriers in 
environmental research, where resource constraints often limit model 
optimization [23].
Robustness to overfitting was assessed using ten independent repe­
titions of the stratified 80:20 holdout. Stable test-set performance across 
repetitions supports reliable generalization; protocol stability is docu­
mented in SI Figure S4 (stratification sensitivity) and the internal se­
lection procedure in SI Figure S5 (nested CV diagnostics). Learning 
curves (Fig. 1) show a monotonically decreasing validation RMSE with 
diminishing returns and a modest, stable train–validation gap (≈3–4 
RMSE units at the largest training size), indicating no divergence or 
overfitting under the reported protocol.
Residual distribution analysis (Fig. 2) provided deeper insights into 
model reliability. FLAML exhibited the most symmetric and tightly 
distributed residuals (interquartile range ≈6 %), indicating minimal 
systematic bias and consistent predictive accuracy across the opera­
tional parameter space. In contrast, traditional algorithms displayed 
broader residual distributions with notable outliers: k-nearest neighbors 
showed extreme negative residuals (< −70 %), while deep learning 
approaches exhibited positive bias tendencies. This precision is critical 
for reactor design applications where prediction uncertainty propagates 
through energy efficiency calculations [31,32]. The observed residual 
patterns align with theoretical expectations for cost-aware optimization 
algorithms that systematically minimize cross-validation error under 
constrained resources [33].

Following Anode, the XGBoost model highlights Electrolyte Con­
centration in the next tier of importance (mean SHAP ≈6.5). Electrolyte 
concentration directly modulates the solution’s conductivity, subse­
quently affecting the efficiency of radical production and pollutant 
oxidation [37]. Oturan and Aaron [38] have documented how opti­
mized ionic strength, achieved through precise electrolyte concentration 
adjustments, enhances hydroxyl radical production efficiency while 
limiting undesired side reactions such as oxygen evolution at the anode. 
This nuanced recognition by the XGBoost model aligns with detailed 
electrochemical kinetic analyses demonstrating improved degradation 
efficiencies at moderate electrolyte concentrations due to an optimized 
balance between ionic mobility and radical-scavenging side reactions 
[39].
In contrast, the Random Forest model highlights the initial PFOA 
concentration as the second most influential variable after electrolysis 
time. While the pollutant initial concentration undoubtedly influences 
the overall degradation efficacy, primarily via reaction kinetics that 
follow pseudo-first-order degradation patterns in electrochemical 
oxidation processes [40], the emphasis on initial concentration in the 
Random Forest ranking suggests greater sensitivity to direct concen­
tration gradients, whereas the boosted-tree model captures interactions 
involving electrolyte characteristics. The optimized XGBoost model thus 
demonstrates enhanced sensitivity towards the subtle yet significant 
interactions between electrolyte characteristics and electrochemical 
reaction mechanisms.
Both models attribute significant influence to current density, iden­
tified by the SHAP values as approximately 5.9 for XGBoost and 5.2 for 
Random Forest. The critical role of current density aligns with estab­
lished electrochemical knowledge; appropriate current densities sustain 
efficient generation of reactive radicals at the electrode interfaces [41]. 
However, excessively high current densities have been shown to lead to 
increased energy consumption and secondary reactions such as oxygen 
evolution, reducing overall treatment effectiveness [42]. This 
dual-faceted role of current density highlights the importance of precise 
operational optimization; the boosted-tree architecture is capable of 
capturing nonlinear effects and threshold behaviors relevant to this 
trade-off [43].

3.3. Dose–response patterns and stability of SHAP explanations
Within-dataset dose–response behavior for the main continuous 
variables is shown in Fig. 4A–C. The SHAP dependence for electrolysis 
time (Fig. 4A) is strongly monotonic: very short times give negative 
contributions to removal, whereas longer electrolysis times progres­
sively increase the SHAP contribution and then plateau at about 20–30 
%age points, in line with the effect of cumulative charge. Electrolyte 
concentration (Fig. 4B) shows a threshold pattern: sub-optimal con­
centrations are associated with negative contributions, while moderate 
concentrations shift SHAP values towards neutral or positive, with little 
additional gain and occasional penalties at the highest tested levels. For 
current density (Fig. 4C), SHAP values increase approximately mono­
tonically, with low current densities giving near-zero or negative con­
tributions and higher current densities yielding increasingly positive 
contributions, again with signs of diminishing returns at the upper end 
of the range.
Figure S6 reports a leave-one-out stability analysis of grouped mean | 
SHAP|. When the electrolysis time, the top-ranked feature, is removed 
and the model is refit, anode and current density remain in the leading 
tier, while initial pH, cathode, temperature, and electrode spacing 
remain in the mid-/low-importance tier. The Kendall’s τ between the 
two rankings is 0.600 (p = 0.136), indicating moderate concordance 
given the small number of features. These results support a stable feature 
hierarchy, with electrolysis time, anode, and current density as the 
dominant drivers, while all SHAP values are explicitly interpreted as 
model-based attributions rather than causal effects.
3.4. Implications for PFAS treatment optimization
The feature importance results provide actionable guidance for 
electrochemical reactor design. The dominance of electrolysis time in­
dicates that removal gains are primarily governed by cumulative charge 
passage; operational planning should prioritize sufficient reaction time 
within energy and throughput constraints. Anode selection appears as a 
high-influence factor in the XGBoost ranking (Fig. 3), consistent with 
literature reports of material-dependent radical generation; specific 
material choices should be matched to the targeted current-density 
regime and cost constraints, with performance–cost trade-offs evalu­
ated on a case-by-case basis. Water-matrix effects rank low in the 
grouped SHAP profile (e.g., “Water Matrix” near the bottom of Fig. 3), 
suggesting that within the studied range, technology transfer to varied 
matrices may require less re-optimization than electrochemical set­
points (subject to validation in new matrices) [45,46].
FLAML’s predictive accuracy supports tighter setpoint control (e.g., 
current density and time) for energy-aware operation within the tested 
domain. Quantitative energy projections (e.g., kWh⋅m⁻³) are not drawn 
from the present model outputs and require dedicated electro-energy 
auditing under experimental validation. The model’s generalization 
capability across operational conditions within this dataset suggests 
potential for transfer-learning extensions to related PFAS (e.g., PFOS, 
GenX), contingent on coverage of comparable operating ranges and 
external validation [47].
3.5. Limitations and future directions
Although FLAML demonstrated exceptional predictive accuracy and 
robustness when applied to the dataset curated by [30], several avenues 
remain promising for further exploration. First, future research could 
assess the adaptability of this modeling framework to broader opera­
tional parameter spaces and validate its performance using external 
datasets from different experimental setups. This would also include a 
wider spectrum of PFAS compounds, such as PFOS and co-contaminants, 
to ascertain the generalizability of insights across diverse treatment 
systems. Transfer learning methodologies hold particular promise in this 
regard, as they could efficiently leverage knowledge from the current 
model to accelerate predictive capabilities in related electrochemical 
systems.
Additionally, while the current study prioritized removal efficiency, 
extending the analytical framework towards integrated multi-objective 
optimization could significantly enhance practical applicability. Such 
an extension would consider critical performance indicators beyond 
contaminant removal, including energy consumption metrics, byprod­
uct formation, and the computational trade-offs between optimization 
speed and final model performance. Furthermore, enhancing real-time 
adaptive process control represents another promising direction. Inte­
grating FLAML with in situ sensing technologies would enable dynamic 
process optimization, facilitating immediate adjustments to operational 
parameters based on real-time feedback.
Finally, while SHAP analysis provided valuable initial insights, 
deeper interpretability could be achieved by developing hybrid 
modeling frameworks that combine FLAML with physics-based elec­
trochemical models. Future methodological refinements could also 
include exploring the use of native categorical handling within tree- 
based models as a potential alternative to one-hot encoding. Collec­
tively, these future research directions promise substantial advance­
ments in predictive analytics, interpretability, and operational 
applicability, reinforcing the potential of AutoML as a transformative 
approach for environmental electrochemical remediation technologies.
4. Conclusions
This study demonstrates a reproducible AutoML-based workflow for 
integrating automated machine learning with electrochemical oxidation 
processes targeting persistent environmental contaminants. The FLAML- 
driven XGBoost model outperformed conventional manually optimized 
ML approaches, delivering significantly enhanced predictive accuracy 
and interpretability, as evidenced by rigorous benchmarking and sta­
tistical validation. The analysis identified electrolysis time and anode 
material as primary operational drivers, offering actionable insights 
grounded in fundamental electrochemical principles and validated 
through SHAP-based interpretability. Crucially, the automated FLAML 
framework substantially reduced computational demands (72 % 
reduction in tuning time), thereby enhancing the accessibility and 
reproducibility of advanced ML techniques for environmental practi­
tioners. By demonstrating superior predictive power alongside scientif­
ically robust mechanistic insights, this research directly addresses 
critical methodological limitations in the current literature. The 
containerized, version-pinned pipeline ensures reproducibility and 
transparency, promoting wider adoption and methodological stan­
dardization within environmental remediation. Moving forward, inte­
grating the developed methodology with hybrid physics-based models, 
real-time adaptive process controls, and broader PFAS compound sce­
narios can further elevate electrochemical treatment efficacy and
