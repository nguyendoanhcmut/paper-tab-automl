A B S T R A C T
The continuous-flow aerobic granular sludge-membrane bioreactor (AGS-MBR) system represents an efficient 
and sustainable technology for wastewater treatment. AGS, a spherical or ellipsoidal granular sludge formed 
through microbial self-aggregation under aerobic conditions, progresses through four distinct life-cycle stages in 
the AGS-MBR system: initial, growth, mature, and cleaved. Accurate identification and classification of these 
stages are crucial for optimizing AGS-MBR operations and maintaining system stability; however, traditional 
monitoring methods are labor-intensive and error-prone. This study utilized Artificial Intelligence (AI) to develop 
a machine learning model based on the You Only Look Once (YOLOv8) algorithm for automated AGS monitoring 
and classification. Trained on 862 annotated images, the model achieved average precision of 0.985 at an 
Intersection over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.5 (mAP50), and the mAP50-95 of 0.837, demonstrating high ac­
curacy in AGS classification. The t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) revealed distinct clusters 
of AGS features across life-cycle stages, while SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) demonstrated that the 
model focused on global features of small-grained images and edge features of large-grained images, both 
confirming the robustness of classification. The model’s statistical functionality, supported by global variables, 
enabled real-time AGS monitoring in MBR system. This study provides a powerful tool for detecting and clas­
sifying the AGS life-cycle, offering guidance for the operation and maintenance of AGS-MBR system and 
demonstrating the potential applications of AI in wastewater treatment.
1. Introduction
Aerobic granular sludge (AGS), characterized by its unique structure 
and high biomass density, offers significant advantages in the simulta­
neous removal of nutrients and organic pollutants within a single 
bioreactor, potentially surpassing the conventional activated sludge 
(CAS) process in terms of efficiency and cost-effectiveness (Bengtsson 
et al., 2019; Hu et al., 2024). The long-term stability of AGS is pivotal to 
sustaining these benefits, while the sequencing batch reactor (SBR) in 
batch mode has been the most successful method for AGS cultivation 
(Han et al., 2024), there is a burgeoning interest in continuous mode 
cultivation, especially when AGS is integrated with membrane bio­
reactors (MBRs). MBRs, which combine CAS with membrane filtration, 
are known for producing high-quality effluent and reducing wasted 
activated sludge (WAS) (Rahman et al., 2023). However, conventional 
MBRs often face issues with membrane fouling due to the deposition of 
flocculated activated sludge particles on the membrane surface (Morales 
et al., 2024). In contrast, AGS, with its aggregate form, has demonstrated 
an effective mitigation of membrane fouling, which is crucial for 
enhancing the practicality of MBR applications (Campo et al., 2021). 
Therefore, the AGS-MBR system stands out as an efficient, economical, 
and sustainable wastewater treatment technology that not only mini­
mizes membrane fouling but also ensures superior effluent quality and 
reduced WAS production.
In AGS-MBR system, the stability of AGS is paramount, with 
morphological integrity and structure being key to operational effi­
ciency. The AGS life-cycle, encompassing initial, growth, mature, and 
cleaved stages, significantly influences bioreactor performance (Yang

and size distribution, is essential for effective process control (de Sousa 
Rollemberg et al., 2018). Then, operational parameters like influent 
composition and temperature can induce variability in AGS, potentially 
leading to a transition from granular to flocculated sludge and system 
instability (Iorhemen and Liu, 2021). The self-cultivation of AGS in 
continuous flow MBR system has gained interest due to operational 
convenience and enhanced performance. However, the heterogeneity of 
AGS, with varying physicochemical, microbiological, and morpholog­
ical properties, remains a challenge (Yang et al., 2023). In the initial 
stage, the sludge particles are small; in the growth stage, the sludge 
particles have rough edges; in the mature stage, the sludge particles have 
a stable structure and smooth surface; in the cleaved stage, the sludge 
particles age, with a decrease in large particles and an increase in small 
ones. The characteristics of each stage are interrelated, reflecting the 
dynamic changes of AGS from formation to aging. Understanding the 
differences and features of these life-cycles is of great significance for 
optimizing the operation and management of AGS-MBR system, which 
can help us better control and adjust operational conditions to maintain 
the stability of AGS and improve the efficiency of wastewater treatment. 
In addition, this information also provides an important reference for 
the design of new bioreactors or the improvement of existing 
technologies.
Accurate state identification of AGS is crucial, yet methods like 
frozen section analysis, dissolved oxygen (DO) micro-electrodes, and 
CLSM have limitations. Invasive techniques can disrupt the internal 
micro-environment, while CLSM requires laborious sample preparation. 
These approaches may also be sensitive to AGS properties, leading to 
inaccuracies and a lack of reproducibility (Kirkland et al., 2020; Li et al., 
2014; Ranzinger et al., 2020). This underscores the uniqueness of AGS in 
MBR operations and the necessity for advanced diagnostic tools for its 
state assessment, which is vital for optimizing the performance of 
AGS-MBR system. The development of intelligent, non-invasive methods 
to analyze AGS states in real-time is therefore imperative, ensuring the 
long-term stability and efficiency of these system.
Artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated its prowess in waste­
water treatment modeling by offering enhanced learning capabilities, 
automation, and adaptability beyond traditional methods (Alvi et al., 
2023; Narayanan et al., 2024). AI-driven models can reveal complex 
data relationships and adapt to new data, enhancing understanding of 
wastewater treatment processes and aiding in prediction and control 
(Ignacz et al., 2025; P´erez-Beltr´an et al., 2024). Within the context of 
AGS system, artificial neural networks (ANNs) have been effectively 
utilized to predict critical process outcomes (Zhong et al., 2021), such as 
the removal rates of chemical oxygen demand, ammonia nitrogen, total 
nitrogen, and total phosphorus (Zaghloul and Achari, 2022). They have 
also been employed to predict sludge properties and water quality based 
on historical reactor data, enhancing the predictive accuracy and control 
of AGS processes (Mahmod and Wahab, 2017; Zaghloul et al., 2021). 
Despite these advancements, there is a significant gap in the application 
of AI for the image recognition and classification of aerobic granular 
sludge (AGS). You Only Look Once (YOLO) is a highly influential AI 
technology that, based on a deep learning framework, can efficiently 
identify the positions and categories of objects in images, demonstrating 
strong real-time performance and accuracy (Redmon et al., 2016). The 
YOLO algorithm has not only been widely applied in multiple fields such 
as agricultural disease monitoring (Gao et al., 2024), medical image 
diagnosis (Xu et al., 2025), infrastructure inspection (Santos and Car­
valho, 2025), and autonomous driving (Ul Islam et al., 2025), but also 
provides strong support for machine learning in target recognition tasks 
in complex scenarios due to its efficiency and adaptability. It is an 
indispensable excellent tool in the field of image recognition. Perhaps 
the YOLO algorithm could be a new approach to achieving the image 
recognition and classification of AGS.
Based on the aforementioned background, this study aimed to 
develop a machine learning approach for accurately identifying the 
states of AGS in a continuous-flow MBR over its entire life-cycle using 
the YOLOv8 object detection model. The model was trained following 
dataset screening, classification, and labeling, and then underwent 
hyperparameter tuning after pre-processing. Various evaluation metrics 
were calculated on the test set to quantify the model’s predictive 
effectiveness, guiding the optimization of model parameters based on 
key indices. Additionally, dimensionality reduction techniques were 
applied to the semantic features of all AGS images in the test set, pro­
jecting them into two-dimensional visualizations. Feature clustering 
analysis was conducted across different categories, and interpretability 
analysis was performed to identify key features within images, eluci­
dating differences and connections among them. Lastly, to facilitate real- 
world applications and enhance detection efficiency, a batch counting 
function was developed for each AGS stage, aiming to provide intelligent 
methods for obtaining timely statistical data to aid in the management 
and optimization of AGS-MBR processes.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Description of the used AGS-MBR system
The study employed a previously reported MBR system with internal 
circulation as the physical model (Dai et al., 2020), in which, a 
multi-habitat environment was created through accumulated biomass, 
facilitated by the hydrodynamic conditions of internal circulation. The 
activated sludge used for inoculation was obtained from the secondary 
sedimentation tank of a local municipal wastewater treatment plant 
located in Haizhu District, Guangzhou, China (Biological Island Sewage 
Treatment Plant). The initial concentration of mixed liquor suspended 
solids (MLSS) was maintained at approximately 3000 mg/L. Throughout 
the experiment, the hydraulic retention time (HRT) ranged between 8.0 
and 10.0 h, while the aeration rate varied from 4.8 to 7.2 m3/h to ensure 
sufficient DO supply. The AGS-MBR system used simulated wastewater 
as influent and consists of: CH3COONa (769.20 mg/L), NH4Cl (152.67 
mg/L), KH2PO4 (26.36 mg/L), CaCl2 (35.00 mg/L), FeSO4 (0.85–0.95 
mg/L), MgSO4 (20.25 mg/L), NaHCO3 (267.00 mg/L), corresponding 
COD, NH4
+-N and TP concentrations of 600 mg/L, 40 mg/L and 6 mg/L. 
All other aspects related to system configuration and operational con­
ditions remained consistent with the previously reported work (Dai 
et al., 2020).
2.2. Data collection and feature classification
In the 100-day operation process, the system transitioned from 
startup to stable operation. Sludge samples were collected daily at fixed 
locations and fixed times throughout this period. These samples were 
examined using bright field microscopy (Leica DM500, Wetzlar, Ger­
many) at 40 × magnification. Timely images were taken with an 
attached digital camera, and some images were adjusted for depth of 
field to ensure clarity. To enhance model generalization ability and 
robustness, a total of 862 images were combined from this study and a 
previous work (Dai et al., 2020). For data acquisition, conforming data 
corresponding to each stage’s characteristics in the experiment under­
went initial pre-processing and classification into four distinct stages 
based on the morphological features and size variation of AGS, namely: 
initial stage (IS), growth stage (GS), maturity stage (MS), and cleavage 
stage (CS) (Yang et al., 2023). Specific classification rules and demon­
strations were presented in Table 1 and Fig. 1.
In this study, the target detection annotation tool (Labelimg v1.8.6) 
was used to annotate the AGS image dataset across its life-cycle. The 
annotation included the location and centroid information of the targets, 
and the results were stored in TXT files. The dataset was divided into a 
training set (690 images) and a testing set (172 images) at a ratio of 80% 
and 20%. A random seed was set to ensure the reproducibility of the 
results.

2.3. Machine learning model
In this study, YOLOv8 was utilized to extract supervised information 
from each image, including the precise location and category parame­
ters of the detected objects. After all datasets were classified based on 
these criteria, an object detection dataset annotation tool (Labelimg 
v1.8.6) was employed to set the center position, height, width, and class 
of each identified object to annotate the object position in each image. 
These results were then normalized, with the entire process illustrated in 
Fig. 2.
The Darknet-53 network is based on the use of residual and skip 
connections to effectively capture features at various scales and levels 
(Redmon and Farhadi, 2018), whose core idea can be described as the 
combination of a fundamental feature extraction framework and a re­
sidual module (Kaiming and Jian, 2014; Kaiming et al., 2015). In this 
study, it served as the foundation for extracting features from the input 
images (Redmon and Farhadi, 2017), where the network backbone 
employed a comprehensive gradient flow structure, with different 
channel numbers adjusted for diverse scale models. The neck section 
incorporated a bidirectional path network to introduce a bottom-up path 
that facilitated the transmission of low-level information to the top of 
the upper layers, while, in the head section, a contemporary decoupled 
head structure was adopted to distinguish between classification and 
detection heads. This approach enables direct prediction of the object 
centers instead of calculating offsets from predefined anchor frames. 
Additionally, for enhancing model precision and efficiency, this work 
integrated the Simple Attention Mechanism (SimAM) into the utilized 
network, enabling inference of three-dimensional attention weights 
within network layers without introducing additional parameters (Yang 
et al., 2021).
The image sizes were normalized in this study, followed by initiali­
zation of the model weights for training from scratch. Comprehensively 
considering both the results of model training and computational effi­
ciency, the number of training iterations was set to 100 with a batch size 
of 16 images. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01 and gradually 
adjusted to achieve the minimum prediction error. To prevent over­
fitting, an early stop strategy was adopted (Hanwen et al., 2019), which

2.4. Model evaluation
For a more comprehensive understanding of the model’s perfor­
mance, it is crucial to load the test set in order to evaluate the effec­
tiveness of the trained model. Hence, this study employed various 
evaluation indexes, including the confusion matrix, F1-score, precision 
and recall curve for analysis, in which, the confusion matrix, consisting 
of a 5 × 5 grid that included a background class, visually represented the 
relationship between actual and predicted values across different cate­
gories, together with the instances of mis-classification and false posi­
tives. Precision refers to the predictive accuracy in identifying the 
positive samples correctly, whereas recall measures how well positive 
samples are identified from the original dataset. The F1-score, ranging 
from 0 to 1, serves as a balanced measure by combining both precision 
and recall into one metric, with the optimal performance indicated by 1, 
and the least effective by 0.

The deep SHAP algorithm is utilized for interpreting the predictions 
made by deep learning models, in which, a baseline value, typically the 
average of input features or a zero-value matrix, is selected as a refer­

feature is assigned a SHAP value that quantifies its impact on the 
model’s output (Wang et al., 2022).

2.6. Prediction and statistics of AGS over its life-cycle
The prediction pattern was utilized to predict a new image using the 
trained model, which was loaded from a checkpoint file and provided 
with a fresh image for inference. To assess the actual predictive per­
formance of this model and validate its generalization capability, the 
AGS images throughout their entire life-cycle were collected from 
another MBR and categorized into seven batches of more than 50 images 
each, resulting in 362 valid data points for analysis. Subsequently, the 
acquired microscopic images were predicted by setting an Intersection 
over Union (IoU) threshold of 0.7 to select the best detection outcome in 
the candidate boxes with considerable overlap, and these results were 
then saved in a designated directory. Additionally, global variables were 
defined to record the number of categories predicted for each input 
image, while batch statistics on the accumulated number of categories 
across iterations were used as functions. By implementing intelligent 
identification and automatic statistical functions, the role of this model 
in practical application scenarios has been significantly enriched, and 
the monitoring efficiency over the entire life-cycle of AGS can be greatly 
improved.

During the process of model training and testing, the box loss was 
used to monitor the regression of the detection box and predict the 
discrepancy between the detection and calibration boxes. In contrast, 
classification loss (cls loss) was employed to evaluate the classification 
accuracy and determine whether the anchor frame matched with its 
corresponding calibration classification. Additionally, distributional 
feature learning loss (dfl loss) was implemented to track the correlation

gradually decreased in value within the training set until they eventually 
converged to a single final curve. The results indicated that when Mosaic 
data enhancement was disabled during the last 10 epochs of training, 
there was a certain decrease in all types of losses, suggesting that this

depicted results from model training demonstrated a comprehensive 
training stage with well-suited chosen model parameters (Fig. 3(d–f)).
The effectiveness of the detector is commonly assessed through the 
mean average precision (mAP) across various classes. In this study, the 
metric was derived from ranking detection scores specific to each class 
(Everingham et al., 2010), and the IoU measured the accuracy of object 
detection in a particular dataset, which was the ratio of the predicted 
frames to the actual frames in object detection. The mAP50 set IoU to 
0.5, calculated AP for each class of images, and then averaged them 
across all classes, therefore, mAP50-95 could be understood as an 
average value calculated at the intervals of 0.05 from 0.5 to 0.95 at 
different IoUs. With an increase in iterations, there was a gradual 
improvement in precision, recall, mAP50, and mAP50-95 indicators, 
which initially fluctuated but eventually approached a plateau close to 1 
(Fig. 3(g) and (h)). The final model achieved an mAP50 score of 0.985 
(Fig. 3(i)), while the mAP50-95 reached 0.837 (Fig. 3(j)). In reality, 
Kong et al. utilized the YOLO model to identify microorganisms in 
microscopic images of activated sludge, bolstering its detection capa­
bilities by incorporating deformable convolutional networks and 
attention mechanisms (Kong and Shen, 2023). Their model achieved an 
mAP50 score of 0.937. Notably, in this experiment, the mAP50 score 
reached an impressive 0.985, demonstrating that our model exhibits 
exceptionally high accuracy at an IoU threshold of 0.5. Overall, the 
evaluation results demonstrated that the model trained with the pro­
vided data exhibited effective performance leading to accurate predic­
tion outcomes.
3.2. Confusion matrix
A confusion matrix primarily serves the purpose of juxtaposing the 
classification results with actual measurements, which can effectively 
illustrate the accuracy of the classification outcomes and is a widely used 
tool for visually evaluating the effectiveness of supervised learning al­

3.3. Analysis of the model evaluation parameter
The precision rate measures the accuracy of a model’s predictions for 
positive samples, that is, the proportion of true positives among the 
predicted positive samples. The recall rate reflects the model’s ability to 
identify actual positive samples, which is the proportion of true positives 
among the actual positive samples. There is a trade-off between preci­
sion and recall; increasing precision may decrease recall, and vice versa. 
The confidence threshold is used to adjust this trade-off by setting a

standard for accepting prediction results. A high threshold increases 
precision but may decrease recall, while a low threshold increases recall 
but may decrease precision. The F1 score, as the harmonic mean of 
precision and recall, comprehensively evaluates model performance. Its 
value ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a better balance 
between precision and recall, thus avoiding performance bias caused by 
solely pursuing one of these metrics. In practical applications, an 
appropriate confidence threshold should be set according to the task 
requirements to achieve the desired precision and recall, and the overall 
performance of the model should be measured using the F1 score (Reda 
et al., 2020). The results of analyzing the model evaluation parameters 
are shown in Fig. 5.
When the confidence interval of IS and MS was 0.4–0.6, the precision 
gradually reached 1, while, the corresponding precision for GS and CS 
also gradually reached 1 for confidence interval of 0.8–1.0. Among 
them, IS showed the fastest growth rate, which indicated lower false 
detection rates for IS and MS features, making them easier to be accu­
rately recognized by the model. In the confidence interval of 0.8–1.0, 
compared to GS and CS, IS was found to be more distinguishable from 
other AGS stages, indicating a lower false detection rate. This observa­
tion aligns with the fact that IS and MS represent relatively smaller- 
grained AGS targets, which are more susceptible to background inter­
ference and partial occlusion in images (Qian et al., 2022). For IS and 
MS, a precision of 1 could be achieved even with a confidence level of 
0.4–0.6, suggesting that under certain specific conditions, models can 
still yield accurate detection results even with lower confidence levels. 
This highlights the importance of not solely relying on confidence levels 
to assess detection performance in practical applications, but rather 
integrating other metrics and actual scenarios for a comprehensive 
evaluation. However, when the confidence threshold was set at 0.942, 
all classes achieved the maximum value of 1 (Fig. 5(a)). As recall is 
generally negatively correlated with confidence levels, setting a 
threshold of 0 resulted in the maximum average recall for all classes 
(Fig. 5(b)). The smaller granule size and dense distribution of IS in im­
ages led to a higher recall rate for detecting smaller AGS granules when 
using low confidence intervals. When the confidence interval was set 
between 0.8 and 1.0, MS and CS had higher recall rates than IS and GS, 
with IS showing a significant decline. MS and CS, with their larger size 
and distinct features, resisted background noise and occlusion better, 
leading to higher recall rates. Conversely, IS had higher recall rates at 
lower confidence levels due to less stringent detection criteria, allowing 
more potential targets to be identified. However, at high confidence 
levels, IS’s recall rate dropped significantly, indicating the model’s 
limitation in recognizing smaller-grained IS targets, which are more 
affected by background interference and occlusion. This highlighted the 
need to balance the confidence threshold. Setting it too high increases 
false negatives, especially for small targets, while setting it too low in­
creases false positives (Thatikonda et al., 2024). Thus, finding a balance 
between recall and false positive rates is crucial to optimize the model’s 
performance. In summary, the higher recall rates for MS and CS at high 
confidence levels revealed the different characteristics of targets with 
varying granularities, while the significant drop in IS’s recall rate 
emphasized the importance of balancing the confidence threshold in 
practical applications.
In this study, we determined that the confidence threshold should be 
set at 0.671. Setting the confidence threshold at 0.671 means that only 
predictions with a confidence level of 67.1% or higher were considered 
as positive samples. This threshold was used to balance precision and 
recall, ensuring that the model’s predictions were both reliable and 
capable of capturing a sufficient number of actual positive instances. 
The average F1 score of 0.95 indicated that the model performed well in 
minimizing false positives and false negatives (Fig. 5(c)), thereby 
achieving a high level of overall performance. Therefore, it is recom­
mended to use a confidence threshold of 0.671 when making predictions 
on new images to ensure optimal prediction accuracy.
3.4. Cluster analysis
The t-SNE technique, a machine learning method for visualizing and 
reducing the dimensionality of data, excels in preserving the integrity of 
relationships and structures in high dimensional data when it is pro­
jected onto a lower dimensional space (Pouyet et al., 2018). This tech­
nique is commonly employed to visually explore complex datasets and 
uncover patterns or clusters that may not be immediately discernible in 
the original high-dimensional space (Silva and Melo-Pinto, 2023). In this 
section, it was used to analyze the clusters for all classes with the results 
shown in Fig. 6.
As shown in Fig. 6, the features of AGS in the test set across its four 
different life-cycle stages were simplified to two dimensions and pre­
sented as four clusters. Overall, all types exhibited independent spatial 
distributions with low levels of coupling, indicating that the experi­
mental classification criteria for the AGS life-cycle were reasonable. 
Specifically, CS showed the tightest distribution and a high degree of 
cohesion, suggesting a consistent pattern of morphological features. In 
contrast, GS exhibited the most dispersed distribution and showed 
specific coupling relationships with the other three categories. This 
implied that AGS, at various stages of development, diversified in 
appearance due to a variety of external conditions and internal devel­
opmental laws, leading to changes from light to dark colors and from 
rough to smooth edges. The corresponding complexity results confirmed 
that each class in the confusion matrix was predicted as GS. On the other 
hand, IS and MS only showed certain coupling relationships with adja­
cent life-cycle categories while maintaining similar morphological fea­
tures within their respective clusters. This distribution pattern indicated 
that, despite certain interactions between adjacent stages, each stage 
retained unique characteristics identifiable through cluster analysis. The 
spatial independence and specific coupling patterns provided valuable 
insights into the dynamics of AGS development and confirmed the 
robustness of the classification method used in this study.
3.5. SHapley additive exPlanations (SHAP)
In the context of machine learning models, SHAP elucidates the 
impact of each sample and feature on the respective predicted value 
(Wang et al., 2024). By utilizing the SHapley value, it can quantify the 
local interpretation of the model, providing insights into the rationale 
behind the decision-making for a specific sample (Qi et al., 2025). In this 
section, it was first loaded the test images from each life-cycle, their 
dimensions were sorted out, and then a model prediction function was 
constructed after preprocessing. Considering the computation time and 
pixel granularity, an appropriate number of iterations were set to 
initialize the explainer, and the corresponding Shapley values were 
calculated for each category, test image, and pixel, and finally the heat 
map was generated as shown in Fig. 7.
The model primarily focused on capturing global features of small- 
grained images and edge features of large-grained images. As shown 
in Fig. 7, the global features in the IS and GS test images significantly 
influenced the model’s predictions for MS. Specifically, the global fea­
tures in IS and GS images, such as color variations and rough edges, 
provided the model with key information to distinguish between these 
two stages. The presence of these features enabled the model to more 
accurately identify the MS stage, as images in the MS stage typically had 
different colors and smoother edges. Furthermore, the edge features 
contained in the MS test images also had an adverse effect on the 
model’s predictions for IS and GS. This indicated that there were sig­
nificant morphological differences between IS, GS, and MS. IS and GS 
could be distinguished by their color variations and rough edges, while 
MS was characterized mainly by dark brown or black colors and smooth 
edges. This finding further validated the rationality of the aforemen­
tioned classification criteria. The internal structure and detailed features 
in the CS test images had a significant impact on the predictions for IS, as 
AGS fragments from CS re-emerged as IS AGS, thereby initiating a new
