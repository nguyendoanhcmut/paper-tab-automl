A B S T R A C T  
Membrane fouling is the primary obstacle to applying anaerobic membrane bioreactors (AnMBRs) in municipal 
wastewater treatment. This issue holds critical significance as efficient wastewater treatment serves as a 
cornerstone for achieving environmental sustainability. This study uses machine learning to predict membrane 
fouling, taking advantage of rapid computational and algorithmic advances. Based on the 525-day operation data 
of a large pilot-scale AnMBR for treating real municipal wastewater, regression prediction was realized using 
multilayer perceptron (MLP) and long short-term memory (LSTM) artificial neural networks under substantial 
variations in operating conditions. The models involved employing the organic loading rate, suspended solids 
concentration, protein concentration in extracellular polymeric substance (EPSp), polysaccharide concentration 
in EPS (EPSc), reactor temperature, and flux as input features, and transmembrane pressure as the prediction 
target output. Hyperparameter optimization enhanced the regression prediction accuracies, and the rationality 
and utility of the MLP model for predicting large-scale AnMBR membrane fouling were confirmed at global and 
local levels of interpretability analysis. This work not only provides a methodological advance but also un­
derscores the importance of merging environmental engineering with computational advancements to address 
pressing environmental challenges.

1. Introduction 
The energy crisis and CO2 emissions are two of the most pressing 
global challenges, particularly in the post-pandemic era (Aktar et al., 
2021). Despite the impact of the pandemic, population growth and ur­
banization have contributed to an increase in the volume of municipal 
wastewater (Goswami et al., n.d.), distinguished by its low organic 
strength and high particulate organic matter concentration (Ozgun 
et al., 2013). Aerobic biological processes for municipal wastewater 
treatment, such as conventional activated sludge (CAS) and its variants, 
have been used for over a century to remove pollutants and meet 
effluent regulations (Wan et al., 2016). Despite its advanced technolo­
gies and capacity to produce adequate effluent quality, the CAS process 
has been criticized for its high energy consumption, especially for 
aeration, as well as the costly procedures necessary for treating excess 
sludge (Kim et al., 2020; Martinez-Sosa et al., 2011). Following the re­
quirements of sustainable development goals, research on energy 
autarky and the reduction of CO2 emissions from wastewater treatment 
plants has received considerable attention over the last decade (Gude, 
2015; Hao et al., 2019a; L. Li et al., 2022; McCarty et al., 2011). 
Therefore, the roles of wastewater treatment plants have expanded to 
include energy generation, nutrient recovery, freshwater production, 
and pollutant removal (Hao et al., 2019b; Obaideen et al., 2022). Several 
innovative technologies were developed to achieve this transformation. 
The anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) is considered a 
promising technology for treating municipal wastewater. Integrating the 
anaerobic digestion (AD) process and membrane filtration, the AnMBR 
can completely separate the sludge retention time (SRT) and hydraulic 
retention time (HRT) (Lei et al., 2018), lessening the challenges asso­
ciated with applying the AD process to low chemical oxygen demand 
(COD) concentrations and large municipal wastewater volumes (Baek 
et al., 2010; Silvestre et al., 2015). After a decade of rigorous research, 
the lab- and pilot-scale applications of AnMBR to synthetic and real 
municipal wastewater have advanced significantly (Chen et al., 2017; 
Dong et al., 2016; Gouveia et al., 2015; Ji et al., 2021). Implementing 
AnMBR, wastewater treatment plants will ideally generate energy and 
contribute to achieving sustainable development goals (Hu et al., 2022). 
However, membrane fouling is a major impediment to the commer­
cialization and scale-up of AnMBR for municipal wastewater treatment 
(Rong et al., 2022a). 
Membrane fouling occurs owing to complicated physical and 
chemical interactions between the fouling components in the feed and 
membrane surface. The attachment, accumulation, or adsorption of 
materials onto membrane surfaces and/or inside its holes may result 
from mass transfer (Guo et al., 2012). It reduces permeate flux, causing 
high operational transmembrane pressure (TMP), performance degra­
dation, shorter membrane lifespan, and diseconomies (Hu et al., 2020; 
Martin Vincent et al., 2018). Consequently, preventing or mitigating 
membrane fouling is imperative for a robust AnMBR system. Although 
fouling control measures have facilitated the successful implementation 
of industrial-scale AnMBR for industrial wastewater treatment (An et al., 
2023), membrane fouling remains a persistent and increasingly relevant 
challenge, particularly as the technology gains traction in municipal 
wastewater treatment. The growth of wastewater volume, the 
complexity of pollutant composition, the limitations of existing tech­
nology, and stricter discharge regulations, among other considerations, 
have contributed to the scale of the issue. Membrane fouling is inevi­
table in all membrane-filtration-based wastewater treatment systems. 
However, the nature and severity of fouling can vary based on several 
factors, such as the level of technological advancement, economic con­
ditions (Andr´ee et al., 2019), and even local climatic and environmental 
conditions (Rong et al., 2022b). It remains a popular field of research 
and innovation, with researchers finding strategies to lessen its impacts 
and enhance the efficiency and sustainability of wastewater treatment 
operations. 
Given that membrane fouling occurs in both developed and 
developing regions, the development of advanced monitoring, detec­
tion, and control technologies, as well as the formulation of reasonable 
membrane operation plans, are equally important and effective in 
comparison to the development of new fouling-resistant membrane 
materials and technologies, which may require higher levels of technical 
expertise and expense. Cleaning and implementing an appropriate 
operating plan might delay membrane fouling (Lindamulla et al., 2021). 
Therefore, in addition to using membrane fouling control techniques, 
including biogas sparging (Aslan et al., 2014), chemical-enhanced 
backwashing (Kim et al., 2021; Lateef et al., 2013), performance 
enhancer dosage (Sohn et al., 2021), and other physical, chemical or 
biological antifouling strategies (Bagheri and Mirbagheri, 2018), 
membrane fouling prediction is crucial for selecting pretreatment 
methods and the development of antifouling strategies (Kim et al., 2013; 
Vrouwenvelder et al., 2006). 
The development of methodologies and technologies for detection 
and monitoring has substantially expanded the complexity and quantity 
of data concerning environmental science and engineering, particularly 
in water and wastewater industries (Zhong et al., 2021). Consequently, 
the need for improved data analysis and processing capabilities con­
tinues to increase. The rapid development of artificial intelligence (AI), 
extensively used in many fields such as chemistry, materials science, and 
healthcare, presents the potential for the water industry to optimize and 
regulate water monitoring and management (Alam et al., 2022). During 
the last two decades, machine learning (ML), a branch of AI technology 
encompassing algorithms such as artificial neural network (ANN), k- 
Nearest Neighbors, and support vector machines (SVMs), has been 
extensively investigated in conjunction with membrane fouling predic­
tion, with ANN being the dominant algorithm for various membrane 
filtration systems (Niu et al., 2022). The prediction of membrane fouling 
within AnMBR constitutes a multifaceted nonlinear challenge, for which 
ANNs offer notable flexibility and competence. These ANN frameworks 
facilitate rigorous hyperparameter optimization (HPO), a pivotal factor 
in augmenting predictive accuracy. Furthermore, the selected ANNs 
adeptly encapsulate the intricate nonlinear dynamics of the variables 
influencing membrane fouling. The ANN is a technique for evaluating 
membrane fouling in MBRs by reducing the complex fouling processes to 
a black box and considering only the input parameters related to the 
MBR and wastewater properties (Schmitt and Do, 2017). Multilayer 
perceptron (MLP) models are regarded as fundamental ANN models. 
MLP is a feed-forward NN design comprising numerous layers of linked 
nodes, each performing an essential mathematical operation on its in­
puts and transferring the output to the next layer (Gardner and Dorling, 
1998). In addition to MLP, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks 
are a more complicated variant of ANN that is ideally suited for handling 
time-series data (Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997). In general, LSTM 
networks consume more computational resources than MLPs, but as 
aforementioned, membrane fouling is an issue in both developed and 
developing regions. Therefore, when considering the use of ML models, 
it is essential to consider the trade-offs between complexity and 
computational cost, especially in resource-constrained settings such as 
developing countries (De-Arteaga et al., 2018). Although the proposed 
innovations have wide applicability, their implementation must be 
carefully evaluated and modified to local circumstances, technical ca­
pabilities, and resource availability. Collaboration, adaptability, and an 
awareness of local conditions will be crucial to the effective imple­
mentation of these advances in a variety of contexts, including facilities 
in developing areas. 
By performing a membrane fouling prediction task, the ML models 
can quantify the importance of the most crucial parameters and condi­
tions that lead to membrane fouling. By understanding these key vari­
ables, wastewater treatment operators can select pretreatment 
procedures that are suited to these conditions. The ML’s superior pre­
dictive capabilities can function as an early warning system for anti­
fouling techniques. By predicting membrane fouling events before their 
occurrence, operators can adopt antifouling measures such as

AnMBR system to automatically engage these antifouling systems. Few 
ANN-incorporated predictive modeling studies have been conducted on 
AnMBR, particularly those for treating municipal wastewater. Either the 
AD performance (G. Li et al., 2022) or membrane fouling was investi­

municipal wastewater. As the scale-up progresses, membrane fouling 
prediction models used in the AnMBR treatment of real municipal

membrane size, and treatment capacity, the long-term findings from a 
large-scale reactor are more accurate and persuasive than those ob­
tained from a small lab-scale reactor. Subsequently, in this study, based 
on the treatment of real municipal wastewater using a large pilot-scale 
AnMBR with an adequate volume of 5000 L, a dataset extracted from 
various long-term experimental conditions was built to train two ANN 
models, MLP and LSTM, to predict membrane fouling indicated by TMP. 
The hyperparameters and network architectures were optimized using 
an auto-tuning framework to determine the network design with the 
highest performance. Regression prediction was investigated to gain 
insights into membrane fouling in a large pilot-scale AnMBR, and the 
model interpretability was assessed. Regarding membrane fouling in the 
AnMBR wastewater treatment process, the findings of this study provide 
a suitable model for intelligent control and demonstrate the high 
application potential of AI in municipal wastewater treatment. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. AnMBR apparatus and operational procedure 
A large pilot-scale AnMBR plant with a 5000 L operational volume 
was constructed on-site at a municipal wastewater treatment plant. A 
diagram of the AnMBR system is shown in Fig. 1. The raw municipal 
wastewater was originally piped from the grit chamber of the WWTP 
into a 2.5-m3-capacity influent reservoir. The influent reservoir was 
equipped with a rotating drum screen with a 1-mm mesh size to aid in 
removing large particles and impurities. Table 1 shows reactor config­
urations and membrane characteristics. A water bath thermostatic sys­
tem maintained the influent reservoir and the AnMBR at a consistent 
temperature between 15 ◦C and 25 ◦C during the various operating 
phases to imitate a steady ambient environment. The membrane module 
consisted of 12 groups of polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) hollow-fiber 
membrane units with a pore size of 0.4 μm and an external diameter 
of 2.8 mm. The total membrane area of 72 m2 and the membrane module 
was submerged in the AnMBR. The operation mode of the membrane 
was 5 min per cycle (4 min for permeating and 1 min for relaxing). An 
online TMP sensor was connected to the membrane-permeating pipe­
line, and real-time TMP variation was displayed and stored in the sys­
tem. Previous studies (Kong et al., 2021a, 2021b; Rong et al., 2022a, 
2021) have provided additional information on water quality, sampling 
and analytical methods, experimental equipment, and circumstances.

Normalization or standardization of input features is beneficial for 
ML models, specifically neural networks. Without standardized inputs, 
optimization algorithms such as gradient descent may have difficulty 
achieving convergence due to the oscillatory behavior of gradients, 
particularly when the scales of the features vary. While it is true that in 
certain algorithms, a significant difference in numerical range can cause 
numbers in higher ranges to influence the model disproportionately 
(Ozsahin et al., 2022), the primary objective of standardization in our 
neural network models is to promote efficient and stable convergence 
during training.

2.3. Model development 
2.3.1. Hyperparameters optimization (HPO) 
The model development process is illustrated in Fig. 2. Across all 
machine learning techniques, hyperparameters are vital to model per­
formance. The values of hyperparameters are determined prior to the 
start of the learning process (Agarwal et al., 2023). In the case of NNs, 
these include parameters such as the learning rate, number of layers, 
number of neurons in each layer, etc. Hyperparameters can have a sig­
nificant impact on a model’s ability to learn from data and generalize to 
new, unseen data, as well as the model’s overall performance, efficiency, 
and generalizability. It is essential for the development of any machine 
learning model that these hyperparameters are effectively tuned. To 
obtain the best performance, the Optuna framework (Akiba et al., 2019) 
in Python was used to optimize the hyperparameters for the MLP, 
including the number of hidden layers, number of neurons in the hidden 
layers, activation functions in each hidden layer, optimizers and their 
learning rate during the training process, the dropout rate for each 
hidden layer, weight decay as the L2 regularization, and loss function. In 
addition to optimizing the previously described hyperparameters, the 
number of memory units was improved in the LSTM model. Table 3 
presents the available alternatives for each hyperparameter. As its 
default approach for HPO, Optuna involves employing the Tree- 
structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) algorithm. TPE is a variation of the 
Bayesian optimization technique for effectively exploring the search 
space and identifying potential hyperparameter combinations (J. Li 
et al., 2022). Notably, the prediction performances of the MLP and LSTM 
models based on the regression concept were assessed using the coeffi­
cient of determination (R2), as stated in Eq. (2), Therefore, the HPO aims 
to maximize R2 to achieve the highest prediction accuracy.

The epochs and batch sizes are vital parameters affecting the 
learning process. Each epoch was one full run through the entire dataset 
during the training. Before training, the number of epochs was a 
hyperparameter requiring definition. It is computationally expensive to 
update the weights after each training sample in NN training; therefore, 
the batch size is another hyperparameter to be specified (Masters and 
Luschi, 2018). Based on the dataset size and train-test dataset split of the 
TMP prediction, the batch size and epochs in the HPO process were set to 
32 and 1000, respectively, which were the same as the MLP and LSTM 
prediction models that followed. 
Through using the backpropagation (BP) algorithm, the MLP and 
LSTM models were trained. An optimizer was used in the BP training to 
alter the weights and biases of the NN depending on the calculated 
gradients. An optimizer is primarily used to minimize errors or loss 
functions during training (Sun, 2019). The HPO was conducted using the 
Adam, RMSProp, and AdaDelta optimizer choices. In addition, the HPO 
improves the learning rate, which is a critical hyperparameter in opti­
mization algorithms. During NN training, the learning rate controls the 
step size at which the optimizer modifies the weights and biases. The 
learning rate regulates the magnitude of model updates and may sub­
stantially affect the convergence and performance of the model (Smith, 
2017).

layers, remain unchanged. However, the s-MLP has just one hidden layer 
with a range of 1 to 10 neurons. Other HPO configurations, including the 
activation function, loss function, optimizer, learning rate, weight 
decay, and trial number, are identical to the MLP model. Due to the 
straightforward structure, Dropout has not been implemented. After the 
HPO, the s-MLP contains 10 neurons in the hidden layer, with Tanh as 
the activation function, MSE as the loss function, RMSProp as the opti­
mizer, a learning rate of 0.003987, and a weight decay of 0.0002208.

2.4. Model interpretability analysis 
Interpretability in ML refers to the capacity to comprehend and 
explain the decisions and reasoning behind machine-learning models. 
Despite their broad acceptance, ML models are mostly opaque. Under­
standing the causes of predictions is crucial for assessing trust and 
determining whether one intends to act based on a prediction or 
implement a new model (Lipton, 2017). In the membrane fouling pre­
diction assignment, interpretability analysis may explain the model and 
provide insights into fouling parameters to facilitate inspection. The 
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) (Lundberg and Lee, 2017), a 
state-of-the-art interpreting technique, was applied to the MLP model to 
evaluate the interpretability and examine the impact of input parame­
ters on membrane fouling.

3. Results and discussion 
3.1. AnMBR performance 
The performance of a large pilot-scale AnMBR for treating municipal 
wastewater during a long-term operation was investigated. The HRT 
was periodically shortened during the operation from 48 to 24, 12, 8, 
and 6 h, and the operating temperature was stabilized at three phases of 
approximately 25, 20, and 15 ◦C. The OLR of the AnMBR ranged from 
0.11 to 2.04 g/L/d. The COD removal efficiency was over 90 % at 25 and 
20 ◦C, and 85 % at 15 ◦C, with effluent COD concentrations below 50 
mg/L at all three temperature stages. The influent SS concentration 
ranged from 128 to 478 mg/L. Because such influent variations are 
shown in implementations, the feasibility of the AnMBR in counter­
acting these OLR variations has been proven. The biogas production rate 
was obtained at 0.06–0.10 L/L raw wastewater, 0.16–0.27 L/g removed 
COD throughout the stable period, and the CH4 percentage in the 
generated biogas was maintained at 76 %–79 %, indicating that the 
AnMBR could achieve a satisfactory energy recovery (Sun et al., 2015). 
The treatment performance is shown in Table 1. 
The TMP during the HRT 48–8 h at 25 and 20 ◦C were relatively 
stable, remaining below 10 kPa with flux from 2 to 15 LMH. As the MLSS 
gradually continued to increase owing to the shortened HRT of 6 h and 
the operating temperature dropped to 15 ◦C, the TMP increased rapidly 
over 25 kPa with a flux of 17.83 LMH. When the HRT was shortened, the 
effect of MLSS on membrane fouling was magnified (Kong et al., 2021a), 
whereas variations in the EPS concentration of the sludge exacerbated 
membrane fouling when the temperature was lowered (Rong et al., 
2022a). Regarding dataset construction, the model developed in this 
study containing many variations in operating conditions over an 
extended period, particularly the membrane performance, including the 
stable and fouling stages, has more generality than those of studies that 
were relatively short-term with fewer changes in operating conditions. 
3.2. HPO for the MLP and LSTM model 
Fig. 4 (a) and (b) show the HPO result of MLP and LSTM models. The 
highest test set R2 values for the MLP and LSTM models were 0.9183 and 
0.8954, respectively. The maximum R2 of the MLP model emerged in the 
789th trial, and the R2 of most hyperparameter combinations was over 
0.75. The highest R2 of the LSTM model appeared in the 210th trial, and 
the distribution of R2 values >0.50 was more uniform. The MLP model 
demonstrates not only superior maximum accuracy but also exhibits 
more consistent results throughout the entirety of the 1000 trials. This 
suggests that the MLP model offers greater stability within the specified 
hyperparameter boundaries. In contrast, while the LSTM model achieves 
a maximum accuracy level that is closely comparable to the MLP, it 
displays less consistency as its R2 values tend to fluctuate around 0.75 in 
most trials. Consequently, the overall R2 performance of the LSTM is 
inferior to that of the MLP and exhibits less stability. Notably, the R2 
value is typically not negative in linear regression; however, in 
nonlinear regression models, such as ANN, the low accuracy resulting 
from poor model performance causes a negative R2 value (Spiess and 
Neumeyer, 2010). The LSTM model exhibited more negative R2 values 
than the MLP model, also indicating that the MLP model was more 
stable.

optimizer. The learning rate and the activation function of the 1st hid­

LSTM model, the number of hidden layers, loss function, and number of

the model parameters throughout the training to minimize loss or 
maximize the model accuracy. This determines how the gradients are 
calculated and how the model weights are adjusted. Observing that the 
optimizer parameter has the greatest impact on the convergence 
behavior, generalizability, and overall performance of the ANN models 
illustrates its significance.

HPO in this study involves using the TPE algorithm to find a com­
bination that maximizes the target value R2 within the given range of 
hyperparameters, substantially improving the model accuracy and the 
efficiency of its development and significant in establishing a large-scale 
AnMBR membrane fouling prediction model. HPO is a valuable step 
regardless of the study objective since it aids in the development of a 
more accurate, robust, and reliable model. The objective of HPO is to 
improve the predictive accuracy of a model, but more crucially, the 
optimized model might find great use of the correlations between input 
features and the response variable (Zhu et al., 2023). While Optuna 
provides a sophisticated, automated approach, there are also more 
rudimentary methods like trial-and-error (Viet and Jang, 2021), where 
different hyperparameter configurations are manually chosen and 
assessed for performance. Though simplistic, trial-and-error can some­
times yield satisfactory results but generally requires more time and 
computational resources for less guaranteed optimization. Genetic al­
gorithms (GA) offer another avenue, simulating the process of natural 
selection to find optimal hyperparameter combinations (Yao et al., 
2022). This bio-inspired method encodes hyperparameters as genes and 
evolves them through generations based on a fitness function, typically 
the model validation score. Other methods include Grid Search, which 
exhaustively searches through a predefined set of hyperparameters, and 
Random Search, which samples hyperparameter settings at random 
from specified distributions. Each of these methods has its own merits 
and limitations and may be better suited for different types of problems 
and computational constraints. 
It is pertinent to note that all computational modeling in this 
research was executed on an Intel Core i5-1135G7 CPU with a clock 
speed of 2.40 GHz. The durations required for HPO for the MLP, LSTM, 
and s-MLP models were approximately 7, 12, and 3 h, respectively. 
Operating under identical conditions of 1000 trials, the s-MLP model, 
attributable to its more rudimentary architecture, incurred the least 
computational time. However, its peak R2 accuracy stood at a mere 
0.8317, which is notably subpar when compared to other models 
focused on membrane fouling prediction, as demonstrated in Table 5. 
Conversely, the LSTM model necessitated the most extensive computa­
tional time, owing to the inherent complexity of its LSTM units. Theo­
retically, increasing the number of trials could elevate the probability of 
obtaining an optimal hyperparameter configuration. Nonetheless, such 
advantages must be balanced against practical constraints, including 
computational resources and time expenditures. As such, the actual 
deployment of HPO for model development should be judiciously 
evaluated, considering both the prediction performance requirements 
and the associated computational costs within the specific application 
context and regions.

3.3. Regression models for membrane fouling prediction 
MLP and LSTM regression prediction models were based on the HPO 
results. Fig. 5 (a) and (b) illustrate the reduction in MSE curves of the 
MLP and LSTM models throughout the training. Within 1000 epochs, the 
curves of MLP and LSTM decreased to a steady value of approximately 
4.5 and 8.8 respectively, indicating that 1000 was the appropriate 
number of epochs. The observed behavior of the MSE loss function 
during training exhibited an oscillatory pattern as it descended toward a 
minimum. Specifically, the MSE did not monotonically decrease but 
instead fluctuated with intermittent increases and decreases in value. 
This oscillatory behavior could suggest several factors at play, such as an 
appropriate learning rate, issues with the dropout as an optimization 
algorithm, or the inherent complexity and non-convexity of the loss 
landscape (Fort and Jastrzebski, 2019). The learning rate defines the 
amount of each parameter update (e.g., weights and bias) during each 
iteration of model training. If the learning rate is excessively high, the 
model may overshoot the ideal solution and oscillate, causing the loss 
curve to oscillate. Similarly, a high dropout rate produces a substantial 
amount of noise and randomness during training, which may hinder the 
smooth convergence of the model. These oscillations indicate the 
inability to obtain a stable solution through the model. However, the 
MSE curves of both models exhibited strong convergence, demon­
strating the validity of the hyperparameter selection and guaranteeing 
the precision of the model structures. 
The 525-day dataset was split into training and testing sets at a 9:1 
ratio, comprising 472 days of training data in 32 batch sizes out of 1000 
epochs. The trained model was evaluated using a test set containing 53 
days of data. The results of comparing the predicted and actual values of 
the TMP using the MLP and LSTM models on the same test set are shown 
in Fig. 5 (c) and (d), respectively. As shown in Table 5, with R2 values of 
0.9183 and 0.8954 on the test set, 0.9322 and 0.9249 on the training set 
respectively, the predicted values of both models were in good agree­
ment with the actual values. Notably, the R2 of the training set was 
slightly higher than that of the test set. This could be because the sample 
size of the training set was larger than that of the test set, resulting in 
more stable and optimistic estimates of model performance. In addition, 
the slight difference in data distribution between the training set and the 
test set may also contribute to this result. Nonetheless, the distinction 
between the R2 values of the training and test sets demonstrated that 
overfitting was effectively avoided. The RMSE values of the training and 
test sets for the MLP and LSTM models were calculated using the same 
units as the output TMP, kPa. As shown in Table 5, the RMSE values of 
MLP and LSTM are 2.408 kPa and 3.098 kPa in the test set, indicating 
that the predicted values deviate from the actual values by an average of 
around 3 kPa of TMP. Considering the total TMP distribution, which 
approximately ranged from 0 to 25 kPa, the RMSE could be acceptable 
for the membrane fouling prediction in light of membrane cleaning. The 
adjusted R2 values reveal that the MLP model, with an adjusted R2 of 
0.9056, possesses enhanced predictive strength relative to the s-MLP

3.4. Insight into the membrane fouling features and the interpretability of 
the model 
The SHAP framework serves as a rigorous method for elucidating the 
influence of individual features on the predictive outcomes of ML 
models. Derived from the principles of cooperative game theory, SHAP 
values offer a unified metric for assessing feature importance, ensuring 
an equitable allocation of attribution among the features under 
consideration (Gramegna and Giudici, 2021). In the context of ML 
interpretability, the concept of feature importance is intricately linked 
to the broader notion of global interpretability. While feature impor­
tance offers an insight into the contribution of individual features to 
specific predictions, its aggregate form across multiple instances de­
lineates the general behavior of the model, thereby facilitating global 
interpretability. By computing the SHAP value for each feature, their 
average contributions to model predictions over all possible feature 
combinations were determined. A high SHAP value indicated a high 
significance. Fig. 6 (a) shows the contributions of the seven features 
based on the mean absolute SHAP values. Flux was the most influential 
factor in predicting TMP fluctuations, followed by OLR, temperature, 
MLSS, EPSp, EPSc, and SS. The large-scale AnMBR was a combination of 
a continuously stirred tank reactor and a membrane module, and the 
change in the HRT and loading rate conditions depended on the flux 
change. Therefore, a higher loading rate with a higher flux tended to 
cause a worse fouling condition, consistent with the theory that flux 
affects membrane fouling (He et al., 2017). 
In contrast to global interpretability, which seeks to understand the 
behavior of the model across the entire data space, local interpretability 
focuses on explaining individual predictions made by the model. Fig. 6 
(b) shows the SHAP value of each feature for each test sample, which can 
provide an intuitive understanding of the local interpretability and 
allow for discovering predicted outliers. Changes in the flux and OLR 
had a more diffused impact on the model output in distributing SHAP 
values, suggesting that changes in these three features may better 
differentiate the degree of membrane fouling. However, temperature, 
MLSS, EPSp, EPSc, and SS display lower SHAP value discrimination 
across test samples, indicating minimal contributions to membrane 
fouling prediction. This detailed representation allows for understand­
ing not only the magnitude of impact a feature has but also its direction 
(positive or negative). This plot shows that flux and OLR have positive 
and negative effects on model output, with varying magnitudes.

3.5. Limitations and future prospects 
AI-based membrane fouling prediction has made progress, however, 
membrane process application of AI technology still faces obstacles (Niu 
et al., 2022). AnMBRs offer a constellation of benefits, including a 
compact footprint, efficient wastewater treatment, reclaimed water 
production, and the capability for energy recovery. These attributes 
render AnMBRs particularly apt for deployment in developing nations 
(Elmoutez et al., 2023). Nevertheless, a pivotal focus of this research 
endeavor is the application of AI technologies to mitigate the persistent 
challenge of membrane fouling in AnMBRs utilized for municipal 
wastewater treatment. To operationalize this technology, several critical 
considerations such as data accessibility, computational capacity, 
internet availability, and specialized human resources must be 
addressed (De-Arteaga et al., 2018). Thus, it becomes imperative to 
consider the context and available resources during the developmental 
and implementation phases of the technology. 
The present study draws upon a pilot-scale case investigation, and 
the scale of data acquisition, as well as model intricacy, may serve as a 
relative indicator of resource expenditure requisite for model develop­
ment and deployment in real-world applications. Comparative analysis 
among s-MLP, MLP, and LSTM delineates the need for judiciously 
navigating the performance-cost nexus, tailored to specific scenarios and 
needs. As illustrated in this study in the context of the AnMBR, the 
adaptability of ANN models signifies their potential for wider imple­
mentation in the domain of wastewater treatment. Beyond AnMBRs, the 
inherent adaptability of ANN models resides in their capacity to be 
recalibrated and modified to suit various operational scenarios. The 
procedure entails a thorough methodology: the models are retrained 
using data that is particular to the new wastewater treatment context, 
feature engineering techniques are modified to account for the distinc­
tive attributes of these systems, and the models are subjected to rigorous 
validation to ascertain their dependability and precision in novel envi­
ronments. In addition, it is imperative to incorporate customized per­
formance metrics into this process of adaptation so that a nuanced 
assessment of the efficacy of the model across diverse scenarios can be 
provided. Consequently, the adaptability of ANN models is a substantial 
advantage, as it permits their efficient implementation in a wide range of 
wastewater treatment difficulties and demonstrates their capacity as a 
resilient instrument in environmental engineering. 
Given the unprecedented pace of advancement in AI tech­
nologies—far exceeding that of conventional industrial innova­
tions—the methods and models for predicting membrane fouling are in a 
state of constant evolution. Consequently, future research endeavors 
should perpetually focus on emerging HPO techniques, novel modeling 
paradigms, and new frameworks for model interpretability. Owing to 
the inherent adaptability and scalability of ML models, they hold the 
potential for application in a broader spectrum of wastewater treatment 
processes, contingent upon suitable modifications in parameters, dataset 
substitutions, and revisions in performance metric strategies. 
4. Conclusions 
Based on a dataset extracted from the long-term operation of a large 
pilot-scale AnMBR used in treating real municipal wastewater, two ANN 
models, MLP and LSTM, were successfully developed to predict mem­
brane fouling. The following key conclusions and important findings can 
be obtained from the complete and effective modeling process and 
model performance results:  
1) The HPO procedure, employing Optuna as the designated method, 
emerged as a pivotal component in the process of developing the 
predictive model. Despite HPO being among the most resource- 
intensive and time-consuming phases of the modeling endeavor, its 
inherent capability for auto-tuning proved instrumental in facili­
tating the efficacious application of both MLP and LSTM models to 
the prediction of membrane fouling in the AnMBR.  
2) In the evaluative context of regression accuracy for TMP predictions, 
the MLP demonstrated superior performance with an R2 of 0.9183 
and RMSE of 2.408 kPa, surpassing the R2 of 0.8954 and RMSE of 
3.098 kPa in the LSTM. Due to its inherent structural simplicity, the 
MLP requires fewer computational resources, making it more 
appropriate for predicting membrane fouling in AnMBR systems 
within the scope of this study. It is salient to underscore that,
