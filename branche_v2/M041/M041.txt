Computational artificial 
intelligence modeling and 
optimization of nutrient removal in 
microalgae membrane bioreactors 
using ANN and RSM
Nadia Ghezaiel Hammouda1, Karim Kriaa2, Ahmed Mohsin Alsayah3, Husam Rajab4, 
Narinderjit Singh Sawaran Singh7, Khalil Hajlaoui2, Walid Aich5 & Mohammad Jadidi6
Biological based methods for wastewater treatment are preferred because of they are more 
economical and environmentally friendly. This work sought to predict microalgae membrane bioreactor 
(MBR) levels of ammonia nitrogen, phosphate concentration, and removal efficiency. Forecasting the 
values in the study using artificial neural network (ANN) modeling and response surface methodology 
(RSM). According to the data, the RSM model accurately estimates the effectiveness of phosphate, 
nitrogen, and ammonia removal. The R2 values of 0.9733 and 0.9608 help to justify the significant 
correlation between the predictions of the model and the removal efficiencies. The model uses time 
to predict the removal effectiveness of these contaminants, along with TOC content. Moreover, the 
performance metrics for ANN designs are R2 coefficient of determination of 0.99791, mean squared 
error (MSE) of 3.57E-02 for ammonia nitrogen, and R2 coefficient of determination of 0.99722 and MSE 
of 2.75E-02 for phosphate concentration. Nonetheless, variations between the actual data and the 
ANN’s predictions suggest possible limits in the model or experimental design. Furthermore, using 
RSM and NSGA-II in the optimization process demonstrated the model’s ability to accurately pinpoint 
the ideal circumstances for removing nutrients from the bioreactor.
Keywords  Membrane bioreactor, Phosphate, Wastewater and treatment, ANN
The recovery of clean water, energy, and nutrients from wastewater is a crucial part of a circular economy. 
Hydrolytic microorganisms can be found in interesting places in the natural world, like the stomach cavities 
of ruminant animals. Inorganic nitrogen, phosphorus, organic pollutants, and other pollutants are the main 
contaminants found in wastewater. Wastewater mainly contains inorganic nitrogen, phosphorous, organic 
pollutants, and other toxins1,2. Eutrophication will result if the waste is dumped directly into the water3. 
Furthermore, quite poisonous to ammonia and nitrite are aquatic life. There have been developed several 
methods for treating wastewater4–6. Environmentally friendly and more cost-effective, biological methods are 
preferred for treating wastewater. Recently, much focus has been placed on using microalgae-bacterial systems 
to treat domestic and industrial wastewater. In tropical and subtropical regions, they are more frequently used as 
an alternative biosystem. For more than 70 years, sewage has been treated with microalgae to recover nitrogen 
and phosphorus7–10.

produces liquid effluent. However, all solids have the same residence time (particle retention time, SRT), which is 
independent of the hydraulic retention time (HRT) since membrane bioreactors (MBRs) employ membranes to

Several studies have reported applying ANN, RSM, machine learning, and multi-objective optimization in 
various computational and experimental research studies14–19. The abovementioned techniques have been used 
for process and procedure modeling, prediction, and optimization. In this regard, Viet et al.20 developed an 
ANN method to model the osmotic membrane bioreactor. The reported models displayed solid performance 
in their testing. The R2 values for the membrane fouling simulations and water flux prediction were equal to 
0.93 and 0.92, respectively. Also, the obtained TOC removal model showed high efficiency, with R2 = 0.98. 
Mojiri et al.21 improved how microalgal photobioreactors removed the contamination in different research. The 
reactors’ performance was improved using an ANN, as shown by their low MSE and high R2 values (> 0.99). The 
toxicity test findings showed that longer contact durations (> 48 h) and higher pollutant concentrations (> 10 
mg/L) reduced the chlorophyll and protein contents of microalgae. In a different research, Taheri et al.22 used 
ANN to forecast significant variables influencing anaerobic membrane bioreactor-sequencing batch reactors. 
The prediction performance of the four-member Gauss membership function was shown to be superior. 
A nine-neuron Levenberg-Marquardt training process in the hidden layer was the ideal structure; a back-
propagation technique was also used in the feed-forward training of the ANN model. To better understand the 
phenomena of membrane fouling and improve the fouling parameters in a submerged membrane bioreactor, 
Alipourzadeh and colleagues23 used RSM. The CCD model was reported to be the best for predicting essential 
factors. The results showed that using RSM was successful for this purpose. In Sultana’s study24, using microalgae 
as a biosorbent, the RSM method was used to estimate optimum parameters for removing heavy metals. The 
reported hybrid RSM-CSA approach offered the globally optimal solution, and its outcomes were comparable 
to those achieved by the RSM-DF approach. Experiments in such environments validated the model’s predicted 
ideal circumstances’ consistency. Under ideal circumstances, the experimental removal efficacy (97%) was very 
close to the model-predicted value (with less than a 5% error). Membrane fouling in a membrane bioreactor was 
modeled by Chen et al.25 using back propagation (BP) and general regression neural network (GRNN) ANN. 
The well-known GRNN model was said to have more accuracy, while the back-propagation neural network 
(BPNN) models showed good regression coefficients and accuracies. This implies that ANN models may well 
capture the intricate non-linear mapping relationships between interfacial energy and other parameters. The 
BP ANN and the GRNN have much greater quantification efficiency than the extended Derjaguin–Landau–
Verwey–Overbeek (XDLVO) approach. However, the BP ANN outperformed the GRNN model regarding 
prediction accuracy. The integration of AI with diverse optimization strategies enables predictive models to 
yield the optimal combination of potential inputs. Numerous researchers have used the ANN methodology in 
prior engine-related studies to reduce operational complexity and achieve significant outcomes. Moreover, the 
RSM approach is used to provide accurate experimental data on the performance and emission characteristics of 
wastewater treatments in recent years. The literature survey highlights the prevalent use of data-driven models 
for forecasting wastewater treatment processes. However, distinct research gaps remain. First, most prior studies 
have relied on a single modeling approach—either RSM or ANN—in isolation. There is a lack of comprehensive 
comparative studies that rigorously evaluate both linear (RSM) and non-linear (ANN) methodologies on 
identical datasets to determine which is more suitable for the complex fluctuations of microalgae MBR systems.
Second, and more critically, previous optimization efforts have predominantly focused on single-objective 
techniques. These approaches often fail to capture the conflicting nature of bioreactor performance metrics, 
where maximizing the removal of one contaminant may inadvertently compromise another or require excessive 
retention times. There is a limited application of Multi-Objective Evolutionary Algorithms, specifically the 
Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II), in this domain. This study addresses these gaps by 
establishing a holistic framework that not only compares the predictive accuracy of RSM and ANN but also 
integrates them with NSGA-II to generate a Pareto optimal front, providing a set of optimal trade-off solutions 
for simultaneous Ammonia Nitrogen and Phosphate removal.
This work introduces an innovative method for forecasting and enhancing the elimination of ammonia 
nitrogen, phosphate concentration, and TOC in a microalgae membrane bioreactor. This is achieved through 
the utilization of RSM and ANN modeling techniques. The present work intends to forecast and maximize in 
a microalgae membrane bioreactor ammonia nitrogen, phosphate concentration, and their removal efficiency. 
It has made use of Ding et al.‘s26 experimental data. TOC concentration, time, and pH are the independent 
variables applied for this work. RMS and ANN modeling have been used to estimate ammonia nitrogen and 
phosphate concentration and their removal efficiency behavior. Finally, the RSM and NSGA-II approaches 
helped maximize the given elements to identify the best processing mode. This paper presents a novel approach 
for microalgae membrane bioreactor ammonia nitrogen, phosphate concentration, and TOC forecasting and 
enhancement method. RSM and ANN modeling approaches help one to accomplish this. Important parameters 
influencing the efficiency of nutrient removal in a microalgae membrane bioreactor are TOC and pH, which 
the model demonstrates mastery in controlling time-varying variables. Furthermore, using RSM and NSGA-II 
in the optimization process demonstrated the model’s ability to accurately pinpoint the ideal circumstances for 
removing nutrients from the bioreactor.

ANN modeling results
This section used experimental data from Ding et al.26 on ammonia nitrogen, phosphate concentration, and 
removal efficiency as dependent factors. Time, pH, and TOC concentration were independent variables for 
ANN modeling. Table 1 shows the ANN with each model’s best structure and accuracy parameters. The optimal 
topology for the ANN models was determined through a trial-and-error procedure. The number of neurons 
in the hidden layer was iteratively varied from 1 to 20, and the architecture yielding the lowest MSE on the 
validation set was selected. The resulting best-fit architectures for each variable are summarized in Table 1.
Addressing model robustness and overfitting
Given the limited size of the experimental dataset (44 points), specific measures were implemented to ensure 
model interpretability and prevent overfitting. The primary mitigation strategy was the application of Early 
Stopping regularization. During the iterative training process, the MSE of the validation subset (15% of data) was 
continuously monitored. The training algorithm was configured to halt automatically if the validation error did 
not improve for six consecutive epochs. This mechanism ensures that the network learns the underlying general 
trends rather than memorizing the noise in the training subset.
Furthermore, the Levenberg-Marquardt (LM) backpropagation algorithm was selected for weight 
optimization. The LM algorithm is widely recognized for its damping parameter (µ), which naturally provides 
regularization by alternating between the Gradient Descent and Gauss-Newton methods, making it highly stable 
for smaller datasets1. The consistency of the R2 values across the Training (R2 > 0.99), Validation (R2 > 0.99), and 
Testing (R2 > 0.9) datasets, as detailed in Table 1 and visualized in Fig. 6, confirms that the model has achieved 
strong generalization capabilities on unseen data.

Practical implications and environmental relevance
The optimization results obtained from the ANN-NSGA-II framework provide critical insights for the scale-
up and operation of microalgae MBRs. The model identified the optimal pH range for nutrient removal to be 
between 7.5 and 8.5. From a practical engineering perspective, this is a highly favorable operational window. 
Microalgae cultivation naturally tends to elevate the pH of the mixed liquor due to the consumption of dissolved 
CO2 during photosynthesis. Consequently, maintaining the bioreactor within this slightly alkaline range 
requires minimal addition of buffering chemicals (acids or bases), thereby significantly reducing the Operational 
Expenditure (OPEX) and preventing secondary chemical contamination of the effluent.
Furthermore, the analysis of the “Time” variable, representing the HRT, reveals a critical operational trade-
off. The model predicts that maximizing removal efficiency (> 90%) requires retention times exceeding 12 
days. While this ensures compliance with strict environmental discharge standards for sensitive water bodies 
(preventing eutrophication), it implies the need for larger reactor volumes, which increases Capital Expenditure 
(CAPEX).
This is where the multi-objective optimization (Pareto front) proves valuable for plant operators. Instead 
of adhering to a single rigid setpoint, operators can utilize the model to dynamically adjust the process. For 
instance, during periods of peak hydraulic load, the facility could opt for a “non-dominated” solution with a 
shorter retention time (e.g., 8–10 days) that still maintains acceptable removal limits, rather than forcing the 
maximum theoretical removal that would bottle-neck the system. This adaptability validates the proposed AI 
framework not just as a theoretical exercise, but as a robust decision-support tool for sustainable wastewater 
management.

behavior based on microalgae. In the mixed trophic circumstances of glucose and other organic compounds, 
due to the elevated algal biomass in the mixed nutrient culture, the removal efficiencies of nitrogen and 
phosphorus surpassed those seen under photoautotrophic settings. The pollutant removal effectiveness achieved

Conclusions
This study successfully developed and compared computational intelligence models to predict and optimize the 
removal of Ammonia Nitrogen and Phosphate in a microalgae membrane bioreactor (MBR). The comparative 
analysis demonstrated that the Artificial Neural Network (ANN) model significantly outperformed the Response 
Surface Methodology (RSM), achieving an R2 > 0.99 and lower RMSE values for all target variables. This confirms 
that the non-linear architecture of ANN is superior for capturing the complex biological dynamics of wastewater 
treatment compared to the polynomial limitations of RSM.

Novelty and contribution
The primary novelty of this work lies in the integrated application of ANN, RSM, and NSGA-II to form a holistic 
decision-support framework. Unlike prior studies that focused on single-objective optimization, this research 
utilized NSGA-II to generate a Pareto optimal front, revealing the critical operational trade-offs between 
retention time and nutrient removal efficiency. This framework empowers plant operators to select flexible 
operating conditions—such as a pH range of 7.5–8.5 and dynamic HRT—that balance strict environmental 
compliance with operational feasibility.

A combination of RSM, NSGA-II, and ANN modeling techniques was used to accurately estimate the

Combining RSM, NSGA-II, and ANN modeling provides a robust and flexible way to predict and improve 
phosphate concentration, removal efficiency, and ammonia nitrogen removal in a microalgae membrane 
bioreactor. This technique may change how wastewater treatment systems that use microalgae are designed and 
operated, leading to increased cost-effectiveness, sustainability, and efficiency.

ANN description
A computer model modeled after the structure and operation of biological neural networks is known as an 
ANN. It comprises several neurons, or networked processing units, that cooperate to resolve challenging issues. 
An ANN with many layers of neurons, including an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer, 
is called a multilayer perceptron (MLP). To reduce the error between the expected and actual outputs, the MLP 
is trained using a backpropagation technique that adjusts the weights of the connections between neurons34.
The high processing demands of modern deep learning architectures can be avoided using the MLP, one of 
the most basic deep neural networks. An MLP model’s hidden layer count is a hyperparameter that requires 
tuning. An MLP model has an arbitrary number of hidden layers. Some broad rules can be followed, though. A 
good rule of thumb is to start with one hidden layer and increase it until the model no longer performs better. 
Another recommendation is to figure out the optimal number of hidden layers using cross-validation methods. 
Another hyperparameter must be set is the number of neurons in the buried layer. One typical rule of thumb is 
to place a certain number of neurons between the sizes of the input and output layers. Using a neuronal count 
equal to two-thirds of the total input and output layer sizes is another basic guideline35.
The activation function is another hyperparameter that needs to be adjusted in an MLP model. The activation 
function may be used to provide non-linear output from a neuron. A few activation functions that may be used 
in an MLP model include the rectified linear unit (ReLU) function, the hyperbolic tangent function, and the 
sigmoid function. Any input value may be gradually converted to a value between 0 and 1 using the sigmoid 
function. Like the sigmoid function, the hyperbolic tangent function takes any input value and outputs a number 
between − 1 and 1. Any input value less than 0 is mapped to 0 by the non-smooth ReLU function, and any input 
value higher than or equal to 0 is mapped to the input value itself36.
Overfitting is a prevalent issue in machine learning that occurs when a model performs badly on fresh data 
after doing too well on training data. Overfitting happens when a model is too complicated relative to the amount 
of training data available. In other words, the model might remember the training data instead of identifying 
underlying patterns. One strategy to prevent overfitting is to use regularization techniques such as L1 or L2 
regularization. Regularization encourages the model to have lower weights by increasing the penalty term in the 
loss function. This helps avoid overfitting by simplifying the model37.
The use of dropout regularization is another method for preventing overfitting. During training, dropout 
regularization randomly removes some of the network’s neurons. This pushes the model to acquire more robust 
properties and helps prevent it from leaning too much on any one neuron. Overfitting detection can also be 
done using cross-validation methods. Cross-validation assesses the model’s performance on both the training 
and validation data sets. Overfitting may be taking place if the model performs much worse on the validation set 
than it does on the training set38.

Multi-objective optimization setup via NSGA-II
To determine the optimal operating conditions that simultaneously maximize nutrient removal, the Non-
dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) was utilized. Unlike single-objective optimization, NSGA-
II generates a set of optimal solutions known as the Pareto Front, representing the best trade-offs between 
conflicting objectives.
Problem Formulation:
The optimization problem was defined with three decision variables and two objective functions:
•	 Decision Variables: The search space was constrained by the experimental design boundaries:
	– 0 ≤ Time ≤ 20 (days).
	– 6 ≤ pH ≤ 9.
	– 40 ≤ TOC ≤ 120 (mg/L).
•	 Objective Functions: The regression equations derived from the RSM model (Eqs. 2 and 6 for Ammonia Ni­
trogen removal; Eqs. 4 and 8 for Phosphate removal) served as the fitness functions.
	– Objective 1: Maximize Ammonia Nitrogen Removal Efficiency (%).
	– Objective 2: Maximize Phosphate Removal Efficiency (%).
Algorithm Parameters:
The NSGA-II configuration was set as follows to ensure robust convergence:
•	 Population Size: 100.
•	 Maximum Generations: 200.
•	 Crossover Probability: 0.9.
•	 Mutation Probability: 0.1.
•	 Selection Operator: Tournament Selection.
