A B S T R A C T
Machine learning (ML) is a promising approach for anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) modeling. How­
ever, its complexity poses challenges for researchers who lack expertise in ML. To address the challenges of 
model selection and hyperparameter optimization in ML, we employed automated machine learning (AutoML) to 
model the removal of chemical oxygen demand (COD) in AnMBR treating municipal wastewater. The results 
show that AutoML achieved better modeling performance than previously applied deep neural models, with a 
mean absolute percentage error of 3.11%. We further investigated the impact of expanding the feature set and 
increasing the amount of data on modeling performance. The results demonstrate that the features of operation 
time can improve performance. However, adding more data does not markedly contribute to ML-based modeling. 
An analysis of ensemble feature importance indicates that the concentration of COD in influent is the most 
important feature for predicting the removal of COD. This study demonstrates the efficacy of AutoML for 
modeling AnMBRs and provides insights into the modeling of wastewater treatment processes with small 
datasets.
1. Introduction
In the near future, humans will use and pollute freshwater at levels 
where water scarcity could limit food production, ecosystem func­
tioning, and urban water supply (Jury and Vaux, 2007). The water in­
dustry has identified wastewater as a viable and sustainable source of 
resource recovery (Winkler and van Loosdrecht, 2022), and activated 
sludge is at the core of wastewater treatment technologies (van Loos­
drecht and Brdjanovic, 2014). Among the wastewater treatment tech­
nologies, the anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) is a promising 
solution because of its low operational cost, reduced waste sludge pro­
duction, and high potential for energy recovery (Ho and Sung, 2010; 
Pretel et al., 2015; Robles et al., 2022). By integrating anaerobic 
digestion with membrane filtration, AnMBRs not only achieves effective 
wastewater treatment but also facilitates resource recovery, such as 
biogas production, rendering it an effective technology for a circular 
economy and sustainable development (Krzeminski et al., 2017; 
Moideen et al., 2023). Additionally, AnMBRs offer a fast start-up and a 
smaller footprint for wastewater treatment (Robles et al., 2018). How­
ever, the development of AnMBRs primarily relies on laboratory ex­
periments, followed by pilot-scale trial (Q. Li et al., 2024; Z. Li et al., 
2024; Ren et al., 2024). Operating such systems generally consumes 
large amounts of chemical reagents, needs professional equipment 
support (Li et al., 2022), and requires months to years, rendering them 
time- and resource-intensive. These constraints on laboratory resources 
and space further limit the number and duration of reactor runs, 
impeding the development of AnMBRs.
To overcome these constraints, data-driven modeling, particularly 
machine learning (ML), has gained considerable attention as a tool for 
advancing AnMBR technologies and processes (Li et al., 2022; Robles 
et al., 2018; Wang and Li, 2024). ML models enable simulations of 
reactor performance by utilizing operational parameters and influent 
water quality as inputs and predicting effluent water quality as outputs 
(Li et al., 2022; Mahanna et al., 2024; Pal et al., 2024). This approach 
facilitates the analysis of the complex relationships between influent and 
effluent characteristics and offers valuable insights into reactor

operation and performance optimization. In addition, ML-based simu­
lations provide a cost-effective alternative to traditional experimental 
methods, enabling the evaluation of numerous potential operational 
scenarios with minimal resource consumption. Such capabilities are 
particularly valuable for guiding the design and operation of AnMBR 
systems, particularly in contexts where real-world experiments are 
impractical or resource constrained.
However, the application of ML in AnMBR modeling remains chal­
lenging. One major obstacle is the significant heterogeneity of AnMBR 
systems, which stems from variations in the microbial community 
structures, physical configurations, and influent water quality. These 
factors fundamentally affect reactor performance and resource recovery 
efficiency. For instance, Ji et al. (2020) observed that the membrane 
pore size in an AnMBR can influence the effluent chemical oxygen de­
mand (COD) concentrations and the reactor's microbial composition (Ji 
et al., 2020), thereby affecting the effluent quality and resource recovery 
efficiency. This variability limits the development of generalized ML 
models that can be reliably applied to different reactors and environ­
mental conditions. Instead, ML models often require customization to 
account for the unique configurations of individual reactors. This de­
mands for tailored modeling for flexible and adaptive approaches for 
ML-driven AnMBR optimization. Another complication is that devel­
oping effective ML models for AnMBR systems involves a series of 
complex tasks, including ML algorithm selection and hyperparameter 
optimization, which typically require substantial ML expertise (He et al., 
2021; Karmaker (“Santu”) et al., 2021). To address these challenges, 
automated machine learning (AutoML) has emerged as a promising 
solution to reduce the complexity of repetitive tasks in ML pipelines 
(Truong et al., 2019). Although ML models have been implemented in 
MBRs, their real-world uptake remains limited. AutoML has been 
highlighted as a promising method for facilitating the practical 
deployment of ML models (Lai et al., 2024). In particular, AutoML 
automates key steps, such as ML algorithm selection and hyper­
parameter optimization, thereby reducing the technical complexity and 
time required for developing high-performance ML models. In addition, 
by democratizing access to ML techniques, AutoML has the potential to 
empower a wider range of users to leverage data-driven approaches in 
AnMBR development, thereby fostering broader adoption and innova­
tion in this field (Li et al., 2021).
This study aims to leverage an AutoML framework for rapid 
modeling of the complex nonlinear dynamics of the AnMBR during 
wastewater treatment. Within the proposed rapid modeling framework, 
we investigated the impact of increasing the features (predictive vari­
ables) and training dataset size on the model performance by comparing 
the results with those of existing models based on neural networks. 
Additionally, we proposed an ensemble learning interpretation frame­
work to explain the AutoML-based model. The findings of this study 
contribute to the broader application of AutoML in wastewater treat­
ment reactor modeling and provide valuable guidance for developing 
effective models for AnMBR systems.
2. Methods
The overall workflow includes data collection from on-site lab-scale 
experiments, AutoML model development with different inputs, and 
ensemble ranking analysis of feature importance (Fig. 1). Data collection 
is described in Section 2.1. AutoML model development is detailed in 
Section 2.2 and 2.3, and feature importance calculations and analyses 
are presented in Section 2.4.

both AnMBRs were hollow fiber polyvinylidene difluoride membranes,

total sample size of this dataset was 185, which was divided into training 
and testing sets using a 9:1 split ratio (166:19) in previous study (Li 
et al., 2022). The above data was obtained by running the reactors in 
50–414 days.
We designed comparative studies to investigate: (1) the permanence 
of AutoML, (2) the impact of feature selection on modeling performance, 
and (3) the effects of data volume (Fig. 1). First, for comparing with the 
previous models, we used the same training set and testing data as in a 
previous study that applied deep learning models, in which T-R, T-in, T- 
env, pH-in, COD-in, and flux were used as inputs and COD-re was used as 
a label (Li et al., 2022). The results of deep learning networks from Li 
et al. (2022), including a fully connected network (FCN), a convolu­
tional neural network (CNN), and a densely connected convolutional 
network (DenseNet), were adopted. These existing models serve as 
baselines (Fig. 1). Subsequently, we examined feature contribution by 
integrating OD, ORP, HRT, MLSS, and MLVSS (more feature modeling 
experiment in Fig. 1). These variables were analysed through both in­
dividual and combined inclusion to delineate their effects on modeling 
performance. Lastly, to explore whether the size of the training dataset 
would improve ML model performance, we used additional data 
collected from longer operational times of the same AnMBRs (more data 
modeling experiment in Fig. 1). The sample size of the additional data 
was 135, which was obtained by running the reactors in 7–546 days. In 
addition to benchmarking against Li et al. (2022), we employed 10-fold 
cross-validation to evaluate the AutoML models. During 10-fold 
cross-validation, 10 independent models were trained, with each itera­
tion using a distinct 90% training and 10% testing split. This approach 
assesses the variance in model performance and ensures that the re­
ported results are robust to data perturbations.
2.2. Modeling
The model of AnMBR is constructed as follows: 
Y = fθ(X),
(1) 
where Y is the COD-re, X is the matrix of input features, and fθ is the 
model parameterized with θ. Data-driven modeling aims to estimate the 
optimal set of parameters, θ, that best represents the given data.
However, identifying suitable model parameters can be difficult 
because the process often depends on appropriately configuring hyper­
parameters (e.g., learning rate, batch size, epochs for neural networks, 
estimator number, learning rate, and maximum depth of tree for tree- 
based models) to guide the learning process. Hyperparameters are set­
tings that influence the training process and model performance but are 
not learned through the training process.
Hyperparameter tuning is a crucial step in optimizing the model's 
performance. Common methods for hyperparameter optimization 
include grid search, random search, Bayesian optimization, and genetic 
algorithms. Although these methods assist in hyperparameter tuning, 
they often require substantial ML expertise and computational re­
sources, which limit their accessibility. Consequently, AutoML has been 
developed to streamline the process of hyperparameter tuning and make 
it more accessible.
In this study, we employed a Fast Library for AutoML and tuning 
(FLAML), supporting fast and economical automatic tuning for machine 
learning (Wang et al., 2021b). This library selects a search order opti­
mized for both computational cost and model error, iteratively selecting 
the models, hyperparameters, sample size, and resampling strategy. 
FLAML demonstrated remarkable performance, outperforming other 
leading libraries of AutoML (e.g., Auto-sklearn, H2O AutoML, and 
Alpine Meadow) (Wang et al., 2021b). Studies from environmental 
sciences, atmospheric sciences, and climate sciences are benefited from 
this package (Dong et al., 2025; Xia et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023). As 
tree-based models are suitable for tabular data, they were selected as 
base candidates for the FLAML model selection process, including the 
random forests (RF) (Breiman, 2001), extremely randomized trees 
(Geurts et al., 2006), XGBoost (a scalable end-to-end tree boosting sys­
tem) (Chen and Guestrin, 2016), LightBGM (a highly efficient gradient 
boosting decision tree) (Ke et al., 2017), and Catboost (gradient boosting 
with categorical features support) (Dorogush et al., 2018). FLAML im­
plements a Cost-Frugal Optimization (CFO) strategy, which starts with a 
low-cost configuration (e.g., small model size) and iteratively moves to 
more complex configurations only when the performance gain justifies 
the additional computational cost (Wu et al., 2021). Thus, this method 
can control cost during the optimization process compared to traditional 
hyperparameters optimization processes. Further, BlendSearch was 
applied by default, which is a hierarchical search algorithm that com­
bines global exploration with local optimization to minimize the total 
cost spent in finding good configurations (Wang et al., 2021a). The 
optimization process was constrained by a global time budget of 300 s. 
The optimization will be terminated when time budget is exhausted. To 
prevent overfitting, we adopted a 5-fold cross-validation strategy with a 
mean squared error (MSE) criterion during each AutoML optimization. 
Notably, the search spaces for the select learners above are dynamically 
included the number of estimators, number of leaves, learning rate, and 
regularization parameters. The specific search ranges were then deter­
mined by CFO strategy based on the training dataset size. The optimal 
sets of hyperparameters and settings of all models are provided in 
Supporting Information.

While error metrics (e.g., RMSE) assess the overall predictive per­
formance, they do not explicitly quantify the agreement between the 
predicted values and the ground truth (Martin Bland and Altman, 1986). 
Therefore, to evaluate the concordance between the predicted COD 
removal efficiency and the ground truth, the Bland-Altman analysis was 
performed (Martin Bland and Altman, 1986). The Bland-Altman method 
analyses the difference between the two methods against their average. 
In this study, the difference is calculated as the ground truth subtracting 
the prediction. A non-zero difference reveals that the model generally 
overestimates (difference <0) or underestimates (difference >0) the 
measurement. If the limits of agreement did not exceed the profession­
ally acceptable value range and 95% of the differences lie within 95% 
limits of agreement, then the consistency of the two methods was 
considered to be good (Li et al., 2022). The 95% limits of agreement 
were determined by adding and subtracting 1.96 standard deviations to 
the mean difference.
2.4. Ablation studies
We conducted ablation studies to evaluate the contributions of 
different features to the performances of the ML models. An ablation 
study is a technique used to assess the impact of specific components (e. 
g., features, parameters, or data) by removing or modifying them and 
observing the resulting changes in model performance. Specifically, we 
assessed the impact of OD, ORP, HRT, MLSS, and MLVSS on model 
performance. By comparing the performance of models with and 
without specific features, we can infer how these features influence 
modeling. Additionally, given that we collected more data than the 
previous study, we investigated the effect of this extended dataset on 
modeling performance. Throughout the ablation studies, the training 
and testing data, as well as the model training parameters, were main­
tained consistently with the previous setup.
2.5. Feature ranking score
We developed an ensemble framework using ranking score tailored 
for AutoML tasks. This framework combines tree-based feature impor­
tance, permutation feature importance, and shapley additive explana­
tions (SHAP) values (Schreck et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2025; Zheng et al., 
2023), and produces an ensemble score with feature importance 
ranking. This approach provides a more balanced assessment by 
combining multiple perspectives, rather than relying on a single 
method.
In tree-based models, feature importance is derived directly from the 
structure of the models. For example, in RF, importance is calculated 
using the Gini importance, which measures the normalized reduction in 
the Gini criterion contributed by each feature. In this paper, this method 
is referred to as a tree-based method.
The permutation method calculates feature importance by evalu­
ating the change in model performance when the values of a feature are 
randomly shuffled (Breiman, 2001), which can be applied to any model 
using tabular data. Specifically, the importance of feature j is defined as

principles. To address this, we introduced a “ranking score” metric to 
standardize the comparison of feature importance across the methods 
(Zheng et al., 2023). Feature importance values obtained from each 
method were sorted in ascending order, and each feature received a 
ranking corresponding to its position in the order-list. Specifically, the 
least important feature has a lowest score (1), and the second least 
important is 2, with higher scores given to increasingly important fea­
tures. This process ensured that the ranking scores ranged from one 
(indicating the least important feature) to the total number of features 
(indicating the most important feature). By normalizing the feature 
importance values in this manner, the feature importance from the 
different methods was consistent, facilitating direct comparison.
3. Results and discussion
3.1. AutoML for efficient AnMBR modeling
We constructed the AutoML model on the same dataset (including 
features and targets) as Li et al. (2022) and benchmarked its perfor­
mance against their results. The results showed that AutoML achieves 
competitive performance compared to the reported results of previous 
deep learning models (FCN, CNN, and DenseNet) (Table 1 and Fig. 2). 
The negative R2 values observed for previously deep learning architec­
tures may originate from overfitting, limited dataset size, lack of rele­
vant features, and model suitability for the task (Jiang et al., 2025). By 
using AutoML, the R2 value increased from negative to positive, high­
lighting a marked improvement in model performance. Further, we 
evaluated the AutoML using 10-fold cross-validation. The narrow RMSE 
confidence interval (±0.33%) validates the robustness of AutoML. 
Notably, tree-based models were used as base learners for the AutoML 
tasks. These models exhibit notable differences from neural networks, 
particularly in handling tabular data (Grinsztajn et al., 2022; 
Shwartz-Ziv and Armon, 2022). Existing evidence also suggests that 
tree-based algorithms often outperform deep learning methods when 
applied to structured tabular data and do not require extensive data 
preprocessing (McElfresh et al., 2023). Consequently, the adoption of

tree-based AutoML not only enhances the model accuracy but also im­

Furthermore, Bland-Altman analysis was employed to evaluate the 
consistency between predictions and observations (the ground truth), 
providing insights into the predictive accuracy and stability of the 
models. It shows the average difference (bias) and calculates the limits 
of agreement, which capture the expected range for most differences. A 
wider limit of agreement boundary indicates poorer consistency be­
tween the model and the observations. The AutoML results were 
distributed in a lower limit of agreement range than those of the deep 
learning models, with a more uniform error distribution (Fig. 3). This 
suggests that the AutoML approach yields more consistent and stable 
predictions. In our analysis, the majority of data points were within the 
95% confidence interval, demonstrating a strong agreement between 
the observed values and model predictions.
For the deep learning models, a trend was observed in which the 
difference between the predicted and observed values decreased as the 
mean value increased. By contrast, the AutoML results displayed no 
discernible pattern, with differences evenly distributed across the range 
of mean values, highlighting the stability and robustness of AutoML. It is 
noteworthy that all the analysis results show a positive mean difference 
(Fig. 3). This indicates a systematic tendency to underestimate the COD 
removal rate using these models. This underestimation may be attrib­
uted to the imbalanced data distribution between the training and 
testing sets. Additionally, data-driven models tend to smooth out the 
noise introduced by measurement uncertainty and the dynamic nature 
of the biological process. Despite this, the AutoML achieved an RMSE of 
3.09(%), which is lower than the standard deviation of the experimental 
COD removal efficiency reported in long-term AnMBR operations 
(Robles et al., 2022; Uman et al., 2021). This result indicates that the 
model's overall accuracy is sufficient to describe the AnMBR dynamics. 
However, as we discussed, overfitting, limited dataset size, model suit­
ability for the task, and testing methods affect the performance, thus the 
comparison results are indicative rather than definitive.
3.2. Improved ML performance with operation time as a feature
The treatment efficiency of the AnMBR is not only affected by T-R, T- 
in, T-env, pH-in, COD-in, and flux but also by the reactor operation 
conditions. For instance, the microbial community structure and activity 
in an AnMBR are key factors that affect its efficiency (Hu et al., 2017; Xie 
et al., 2014). However, measuring microbial community structure is a 
difficult task because it is time-consuming and costly, which restricts the 
availability of community structure data. Consequently, this limitation 
may also hinder data-driven AnMBR modeling from effectively uncov­
ering the underlying mechanisms.
Based on the hypothesis that additional information may correlate 
with operation time, we incorporated reactor OD into the modeling 
process, assuming that OD may be a potential proxy for the microbial 
dynamics. The inclusion of OD resulted in a substantial improvement in 
model performance compared to the baseline feature set (T-R, T-in, T- 
env, pH-in, COD-in, and flux) (Fig. 4 and Fig. S1), with the mean of R2 
value increasing from 0.44 to 0.55 and mean of RMSE decreasing from 
2.47 to 2.20 (Fig. 4). Spearman correlation analysis indicates a weak 
association between OD and the target variable, suggesting a low like­
lihood of data leakage (see supplementary material). Thus, such 
enhancement suggests that the additional information carried by OD 
may have been effectively learned by the ML model. It is important to 
note that rather than being a direct causal parameter, OD acts as a 
surrogate variable capturing unmeasured temporal dynamics inherent 
to the AnMBRs process. This includes the gradual acclimatization of the 
microbial community (e.g., biofilm development) and the cumulative

ment. The limited contribution of typically parameters (e.g., ORP, HRT, 
MLSS, and MLVSS) likely reflects the specific constraints of the data to 
this study, such as measurement frequency and signal-to-noise ratio, 
rather than a lack of physical and biological relevance. Additionally, 
feature collinearity may have masked their importance, as the model 
could derive sufficient information from other correlated predictors. 
These results highlight the importance of feature selection for modeling.

2022; Liu et al., 2023). However, when applied to experimental datasets, 
ML has inherent challenges in terms of data availability and reliability.

Laboratory experiments often yield small sample sizes because of con­
straints such as time, cost, and space availability. Moreover, the exper­
imental data are susceptible to various sources of error, including 
human and instrumental errors. These limitations increase the difficulty 
of applying ML-based modeling approaches effectively. Furthermore, 
the ability of a small, high-quality dataset to yield better inferences than 
a larger, low-quality one is often debatable (Faraway and Augustin, 
2018; Kokol et al., 2022). To explore this, we examined the impact of 
increasing the amount of experimental data on AnMBR modeling. We 
incorporated additional data and compared the results using the same 
testing sets to ensure uniform evaluation. The additional data originated 
from the same AnMBR reactors, but with a different operational time 
range. We utilized the original dataset (M1, 50–414 d) and the extended 
dataset (M2, 7–546 d) to establish three validation schemes based on 
10-fold cross-validation (Fig. 6). First, M1T1 represents the baseline 
performance using standard cross-validation on M1, in which T1 in­
dicates the testing data were split from M1. Second, for M2T1, the 
additional data was integrated into the training folds only, allowing the 
model to learn from a broader range (M2) while being tested on the T1. 
Finally, M2T2 involves standard cross-validation on the full M2 dataset, 
in which T2 was split from M2.
A comparison between the M1T1 and M2T1 groups indicated that 
adding more data decreased the R2 value and increased RMSE, MAE and 
MAPE (Fig. 6). A comparison between M1T1 and M2T2 also showed that 
adding more data increased the prediction error, with M2T2 showing 
worse modeling performance (Fig. 6). These findings suggest that the 
addition of more data is not necessarily beneficial for modeling. A likely 
explanation is that data collected under varying experimental conditions

3.4. Ensemble feature-based importance analysis
Using the ranking score approach (Section 2.4) to evaluate feature 
importance, the results of the three methods indicated that COD-in was 
the most important feature of all models (Fig. 7). For the model without 
OD and trained without additional data, T-env appeared to be the sec­
ond most important feature (Fig. 7 (a)), whereas for the model without 
OD but trained with additional data, pH-in became the second most 
important feature (Fig. 7 (e)). When the OD was included, it became the 
second most important feature, highlighting its important role in the 
modeling process (Fig. 7(c–g)). Overall, the COD-in is reasonable for the 
most important feature for predicting the COD removal of AnMBR as it 
can be served as an important indicator of reactor loading. In contrast, 
other features, such as ambient temperature, influent temperature, and 
pH, exhibited lower importance in the model, suggesting that these 
environmental parameters have a limited impact on the AnMBR 
performance.
According to Fig. 7(b–d, f, h), significant differences exist in the 
feature importance rankings among the interpretation methods. For 
instance, the results of the model trained with additional data showed 
notable discrepancies for T-env, flux, and pH-in across the different 
interpretation methods. Because determining the optimal interpretation 
method is difficult, we recommend employing a ranking score that in­
tegrates the results of multiple methods to ensure robustness. In addi­
tion, we examined the changes in feature importance when additional 
data were included. The results indicated substantial shifts in feature 
importance rankings, deviating significantly from the results of model 
trained with additional data. The interpretation results of the model 
trained with the additional data (Fig. 7 (h)) were not identical to those of 
the original model (Fig. 7 (f)). The methods selected for interpretation 
were based on the structure and prediction of the models. Thus, the 
deviations between them can be attributed to the incorporation of 
additional data that may introduce noise into the model, potentially 
reducing the predictive performance. This result supports the findings in 
Section 3.3 indicating that the additional data used in this study did not 
contribute to developing a robust AnMBR ML model.
The variability of feature importance reflects the uncertainty asso­
ciated with different mathematical methods for calculating the feature 
importance. However, despite these fluctuations, the top-tier features 
(COD-in and/or OD) consistently ranked highest across methods and 
models. This consistently suggests that while the lower-ranked variables 
carry uncertainty, the identification of the primary features is robust. 
The dominance of COD-in highlights the sensitivity to COD loadings, 
suggesting that COD-in can be a warning signal for COD-re prediction. 
For instance, when a high COD load is detected, the system can auto­
matically reduce the inflow rate to give the biological process more time 
to treat the water. The importance of OD underscores the non-stationary 
nature of the AnMBR process (e.g., membrane fouling and biomass 
maturation). This implies that process monitoring should not rely solely 
on instantaneous sensor readings but must also incorporate time- 
dependent maintenance schedules.
3.5. Limitations and future perspectives
While this study indicates AutoML as a potential tool for AnMBR 
modeling with limited data, several limitations warrant attention. First, 
although AutoML outperformed previous models under the identical 
fixed data split strategy, these results should be interpreted as indicative 
within this specific experimental setup rather than definitively superior 
across all models and scenarios. Second, the observation that certain 
features (e.g., MLSS and MLVSS) contributed less to the model should 
not be interpreted as the physical or biological aspect, but reflects 
modeling results. Thus, these findings represent statistical feature 
importance, not mechanistic causality. Third, the performance failed to 
improve with a larger dataset does not imply data volume being useless. 
Instead, it underscores the critical importance of data consistency and 
quality over mere quantity, highlighting the impact of distribution shifts 
in dynamic processes of AnMBR treating wastewater. Finally, this study 
provides deterministic point estimates. To further enhance operational 
decision-making, future works could integrate probabilistic approaches. 
Such approaches can provide prediction intervals and quantify

4. Conclusions
Modeling wastewater treatment processes deepens mechanisms 
insight and reduces experimental resources. However, practitioners 
without data-driven expertise often struggle with model selection and 
tuning, limiting the practical application of machine learning. To 
address this challenge, this study applied AutoML to AnMBR modeling 
in wastewater treatment. The results demonstrated that AutoML 
improved modeling performance compared to the previous study. An in- 
depth investigation of input features and data volume revealed that the 
features selected through modeling significantly improved the capa­
bility of ML to model the AnMBR process. By incorporating reactor OD, 
the R2 value further increased. However, increasing data volume did not 
consistently enhance model performance, underscoring the importance 
of data quality in wastewater treatment modeling. Based on these 
findings, using AnMBR modeling as a case study, this work demonstrates 
the efficiency and convenience of AutoML in small-sample modeling and 
ablation analysis. Furthermore, an integrated feature importance anal­
ysis method was proposed to evaluate the feature importance for 
AutoML model applications. Overall, this study provides new perspec­
tives and references for advancing the application of AutoML in waste­
water treatment, especially for modeling in data-scarce scenarios such as 
laboratory-scale and pilot-scale experiments.
