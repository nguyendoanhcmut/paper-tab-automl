A B S T R A C T
This study introduces an interpretable machine learning framework to predict nitrogen removal in membrane 
bioreactor (MBR) treating high-salinity wastewater. By integrating Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) with 
Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), we address the critical gap in linking predictive accuracy to operational 
decision-making for saline systems. CatBoost achieved the best performance, with an coefficient of determination 
(R2) of 0.88 and root mean square error (RMSE) of 4.27 for the effluent ammonia nitrogen (NH4
+-Nout), and an R2 
of 0.91 and RMSE of 4.35 for the effluent total nitrogen (TNout). SHAP analysis uniquely revealed salinity’s dual 
role in inhibiting nitrifying enzymes and disrupting carbon metabolism, with dissolved oxygen, pH and chemical 
oxygen demand removal efficiency as key regulators. Temperature and carbon-to-nitrogen ratio further modu­
lated total nitrogen dynamics through electron donor availability and microbial activity. The proposed SHAP- 
CatBoost model in high salinity MBR combines predictive modelling with mechanical process control.

1. Introduction
With the rapid advancement of urbanization and industrialization, 
water scarcity and pollution issues have intensified (Bao and Fang, 
2012). Chemical manufacturing, food processing, pharmaceuticals, and 
seawater desalination commonly produce high-salinity wastewater (Guo 
et al., 2023). Elevated salt concentrations pose severe environmental 
threats, including soil permeability degradation and aquatic life toxicity 
(Linari´c et al., 2013). Membrane bioreactors (MBRs) combine mem­
brane separation with biological treatment, making them an ideal 
technology for treating high-salinity wastewater (Neoh et al., 2016).
In MBR operations, wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) face sig­
nificant salinity-driven challenges. High NaCl (>1%) suppresses 
ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB) activity, necessitating costly chem­
ical supplementation to maintain nitrogen compliance (Zhang and Liu, 
2023). Salinity-stressed microbes prioritize osmoprotectant synthesis 
over denitrification, but existing models fail to quantify these carbon 
flux trade-offs (Jeon et al., 2022). Additionally, traditional sensors 
cannot detect salinity-induced microbial shifts in real time, leading to 
delayed aeration adjustments and excessive energy consumption (Waki 
et al., 2018). These issues inflate operational costs by 18–25 % in 
WWTPs, highlighting the need for interpretable models linking pre­
dictions to actionable process controls (Borzooei et al., 2019).
Traditional MBR approaches like mechanistic models, empirical re­
gressions, and hybrid frameworks have succeeded in low-salinity 
wastewater scenarios. For example, Park et al. (2015) developed a 
transmembrane pressure-driven flux model predicting fouling rates, 
while Waki et al. (2018) optimized aeration cycles using activated 
sludge models (ASM), reducing energy consumption by 18 %. However, 
these models struggle with high-salinity wastewater. Mechanistic 
models suffer from kinetic parameter drift when applied to saline con­
ditions (Huang et al., 2020), and hybrid models like ASM coupled with 
computational fluid dynamics (ASM-CFD) coupling overpredict nitrifi­
cation rates at elevated salinity (Xu et al., 2024). Empirical models fail to 
generalize across varying salinity gradients, as osmotic stress disrupts 
carbon-to-nitrogen (C/N) metabolic pathways (Zhang et al., 2015).
Recent advances in machine learning (ML) have enhanced predictive 
capabilities in wastewater treatment. Neural networks, such as artificial 
neural networks (ANN), have been used to predict total nitrogen (TN) 
removal (Jeon et al., 2022), while long short-term memory (LSTM) 
models handle dynamic water quality data (Yaqub et al., 2020). Tree- 
based models like XGBoost optimize chemical oxygen demand (COD) 
removal (Yaqub and Lee, 2022), and random forests (RF) identify heavy 
metal toxicity thresholds (Gaur et al., 2024). Hybrid models, such as 
ASM-ANN coupling, demonstrate the potential of machine learning in 
wastewater treatment (Xu et al., 2024).
Research on high-salinity and heavy metal wastewater has pre­
dominantly focused on membrane fouling, with limited attention on 
microbial metabolic processes (Borzooei et al., 2019). Studies on heavy 
metal wastewater have emphasized feature selection (e.g., pH, ion 
strength) yet often overlook process control integration (Ar´evalo et al., 
2013). Notably, no study has employed SHAP’s force plots or partial 
dependence plots (PDPs) to explore the salinity-induced trade-offs be­
tween nitrification enzyme activities and carbon allocation in MBRs. 
This gap limits the practical application of ML models in hypersaline 
environments, where threshold-driven control strategies are crucial for 
optimal performance.
This study bridges these gaps by developing an interpretable ML 
framework that quantifies and prioritizes salinity-impacted control 
variables in MBRs. The objectives are: 1) to establish an interpretable 
machine learning framework that decodes salinity-driven nitrogen 
removal bottlenecks; 2) to quantify multiscale interactions between 
operational parameters and microbial nitrification–denitrification 
functions under high-salt stress; and 3) to develop decision-centric vi­
sual tools that translate model predictions into actionable control stra­
tegies for WWTPs. This approach advances beyond conventional ML 
applications by embedding biological mechanism transparency, offering 
a paradigm shift toward intelligent, cost-effective MBR optimization.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Membrane reactor equipment
This study aims to simulate real-world conditions in MBRs treating 
high-salinity wastewater, focusing on salinity-induced metabolic stress 
and its impact on nitrogen removal efficiency. The experimental con­
ditions were selected to reflect typical operational challenges in high- 
salt environments. The synthetic wastewater was prepared using 
glucose (C6H12O6), potassium dihydrogen phosphate (KH2PO4), 
ammonium chloride (NH4Cl), sodium chloride (NaCl), and trace ele­
ments based on previous studies (Khan et al., 2011; Zhong et al., 2022). 
Five batches of reactors, each with an adequate volume of 10 L, were 
cultivated at salinity levels ranging from 0 to 5 %. Regulate the tem­
perature between 5 and 35 ◦C using a water bath heating system, 
maintaining suspended solids (SS) concentrations between 3 and 8 g/L 
and pH values ranging from 7.0 to 8.5. The peristaltic pump achieves 
continuous water inlet and outlet, the hydraulic retention time (HRT) is 
between 19 and 30 h, and the dissolved oxygen (DO) level is between 0.1 
and 3.5 mg/L. The influent total nitrogen (TNin) consisted of ammonia 
nitrogen (NH4
+-Nin), ranging from 20 to 90 mg/L. While the initial car­
bon source concentration of chemical oxygen demand (COD) equivalent 
varied between 180 and 300 mg/L. Hollow fibre membrane components 
were employed, and membrane fouling was monitored using trans­
membrane pressure gauges. The treatment procedure in each reactor 
module, whereby wastewater was introduced into the MBR using a 
peristaltic pump, agitated by a stirring and aeration system. Organic 
matter was biodegraded by denitrification and activated sludge process. 
Discharge the treated effluent via the membrane modules (Fig. 1).
2.2. Data collection and processing
Evaluate the quality of the treated water following standard pro­
cedures for measuring various nitrogen compounds and organic content. 
Monitor key parameters such as DO and pH using a portable meter 
designed for field use (HI98191, Hanna®, Italy). Adjustments to HRT 
were made by controlling the peristaltic pumping device’s flow rate. 
Systematically organized and analyzed the collected monitoring data 
resulted in 15 key feature parameters, including salinity, oxygen levels, 
retention time, acidity, temperature, and input and output concentra­
tions of key contaminants. Specifically, the output two parameters were 
the effluent of ammonia nitrogen (NH4
+-Nout) and total nitrogen (TNout), 
while the remaining 13 parameters served as input variables: salinity, 
DO, HRT, pH, temperature (Temp), chemical oxygen demand in the 
influent (CODin), chemical oxygen demand in the effluent (CODout), 
ammonia nitrogen in the influent (NH4
+-Nin), nitrite nitrogen in the 
effluent (NO2
−-Nout), nitrate nitrogen in the effluent (NO3
−-Nout), carbon-

MBR.
The pre-processing steps include removing duplicate samples and 
refining the data set to ensure the accuracy of the model and prevent 
data leakage. Input features were optimized using standardization

Additionally, winsorization was applied to cap values outside the 1st 
and 99th percentiles, preserving the data’s overall distribution. The 
dataset was then randomly split, with 80 % allocated for training and 20 
% reserved for testing. The final processed dataset consisted of 570 
samples (see supplementary materials). Fig. 2a shows a box plot of all 
the data, giving the mean and standard deviation. All machine learning 
models were validated using this testing set, allowing for a compre­

2.3. Model construction and optimization
2.3.1. Machine learning algorithms
This study applies six machine learning models to simulate effluent 
water quality. Gradient boosting decision trees (GBDT) is an ensemble 
learning method that sequentially builds multiple weak decision trees, 
with each tree correcting the errors of the previous one (Niu et al., 
2023). Light gradient boosting machine (LightGBM) is a variant of 
GBDT, using a histogram-based approach to speed up training and 
improve efficiency (Chang et al., 2022). Random forest (RF) is an inte­
grated approach to building multiple decision trees, each trained on a 
random subset of data and features (Lai et al., 2025). XGBoost is another 
gradient-boosting algorithm that optimizes performance and computa­
tional efficiency using techniques like regularization and parallelization 
(Yaqub and Lee, 2022). Adaptive boosting (AdaBoost) is an ensemble 
method that combines weak classifiers in a weighted manner to form a 
strong classifier, giving more weight to incorrectly classified instances in 
subsequent rounds (Zhuang et al., 2021). Categorical boosting (Cat­
Boost) is a gradient-boosting algorithm that efficiently handles cate­
gorical features by converting them into numerical representations 
during training (Ibrar et al., 2022). These models were applied using 13 
experimental input variables to predict effluent NH4
+-Nout and TNout in 
the MBR.
2.3.2. Bayesian optimization
Fine-tuning the hyperparameters of each model using Bayesian 
optimization improves performance while reducing computational 
costs. It builds a probabilistic model, typically a Gaussian process, to 
predict the performance of different hyperparameter configurations, 
guiding the selection of optimal values through a balance of exploration 
and exploitation (Shi et al., 2021; Yin and Li, 2022). The search space for 
hyperparameters, such as learning rate, tree depth, and iteration num­
ber, was defined based on prior knowledge and preliminary results. 
Compared to grid and random search, Bayesian optimization explores 
the parameter space more efficiently, achieving better results with fewer 
evaluations. Comparative experiments with stochastic and grid search 
confirmed that Bayesian optimization outperformed these traditional 
methods in terms of both accuracy and computational efficiency, mak­
ing it the most suitable choice for our specific application.

2.3.3. K-fold cross-validation
For model validation, K-fold cross-validation was employed to 
reduce bias and ensure stability across the datasets. This approach splits 
the data into k (k = 10) equally sized portions. Designate one portion as 
the test set for each iteration while using the remaining portions for 
training. This procedure is repeated k times, ensuring each subset is 
precisely set once as the test (Fushiki, 2011; Zhang and Liu, 2023). The 
average performance across all folds provides a robust measure of the 
model’s generalization ability, reducing overfitting and variance in our 
results.
2.3.4. Implementation of machine learning models
The models were implemented using Python 3.11.4 and scikit-learn 
libraries. The datasets were preprocessed to ensure compatibility with 
each algorithm, including scaling and encoding as necessary. Each 
model was trained using optimized hyperparameters obtained from 
Bayesian optimization, and cross-validated results were used to ensure 
consistency in model performance evaluation.

Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis was employed to 
enhance the interpretability of all machine learning models. SHAP 
values offer a game-theoretic method for explaining model outputs, 
highlighting the contribution of each feature to the predictions. These 
values are derived from Shapley values, where the contribution of each 
feature is determined by considering all possible feature combinations.

SHAP summary plot displays the overall importance of features 
across all predictions. It ranks features based on their contribution to the 
model’s output, providing a global view of the most influential param­
eters. Force plot visual explains individual predictions by showing the 
force each feature exerts on the prediction, pushing it higher or lower 
than the average prediction (He et al., 2023). The waterfall plot visu­
alizes how each feature contributes to a specific prediction by showing a 
cumulative breakdown of SHAP values. It allows us to understand the 
direction and magnitude of feature influence on a particular instance. 
The PDPs help illustrate the relationship between one or two features 
and the target variable. It shows how the expected prediction changes as 
a feature varies, holding other features constant, and provides deeper 
insights into feature behaviour (Kovacs et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2024). By 
combining these visual tools, global and local insights were gained into 
the machine learning models, improving the overall interpretability of 
the predictions.
3. Results and discussions
3.1. Model training and optimization
3.1.1. Hyperparameter optimization
Bayesian optimization with Gaussian processes was applied to tune 
hyperparameters across six models, utilizing a search space defined by 
prior experimental knowledge and pilot trials. For each algorithm, key 
parameters were bounded within physiochemical and computational 
constraints: learning rates (0.01–0.30) to balance convergence speed 
and stability, tree depths (3–20) to prevent overfitting, and regulariza­
tion terms (L2: 1–10) to control model complexity. The optimization 
process iteratively evaluated different parameter combinations per 
model via a lower confidence bound (LCB) acquisition function, maxi­
mizing the expected improvement in 10-fold cross-validation RMSE. 
CatBoost’s depth (4–10) and L2 regularization (1–10) were optimized to 
mitigate overfitting under high-salinity noise, converging at depth = 8 
and L2 = 3. LightGBM’s leaf count (20–50) and RF’s tree depth (5–20) 
addressed feature interaction capture in nonlinear salinity-response 
systems. XGBoost’s subsampling (0.50–1.00) enhanced robustness 
against ionic strength-induced data sparsity (see supplementary mate­
rials). The algorithm-specific ranges ensured physiochemical relevance 
with known microbial metabolic hierarchies. Optimal configurations 
reduced validation RMSE by 12–18 % compared to default settings, 
confirming Bayesian optimization’s efficacy in hypersaline wastewater 
prediction tasks.

Evaluate the results of the 10-fold cross-validation for the six ma­
chine learning algorithms applied to model NH4
+-Nout and TNout con­
centrations. The performance metrics, including R2 and RMSE, for NH4
+-

3.5. Underlying mechanisms and processes
In high-salinity environments, NH4
+-Nout removal is primarily hin­
dered by the inhibition of nitrification. The activity of ammonia- 
oxidizing bacteria (AOB) and nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (NOB) is sensi­
tive to changes in osmotic pressure caused by salt (Shen et al., 2014). 
This osmotic stress disrupts cellular functions, including the proton 
gradient required for the activity of AMO and HAO, enzymes critical for 
NH4
+ oxidation to NO2
−/NO3
−. Consequently, the nitrification process is 
slowed, leading to higher NH4
+ concentrations in effluent. Additionally, 
high salt levels may favour the growth of halophilic bacteria (e.g., 
Halomonas), which lack efficient nitrifying capabilities, further 
reducing nitrogen removal efficiency. The competition for carbon 
sources under saline stress leads to synthesizing compatible solutes like 
glycine betaine, diverting carbon away from nitrogen removal functions, 
thus promoting ammonia accumulation (Vyrides and Stuckey, 2017). 
Interactions between salt, pH, DO, and other parameters, such as the C/ 
N ratio and HRT, exacerbate the inefficiency of ammonium removal. 
Specifically, a higher C/N ratio can disrupt the balance between nitri­
fying and denitrifying bacteria. Reduced DO in high salinity can create 
hypoxic zones, inhibiting aerobic nitrification (Huang et al., 2024).
For TNout, salinity inhibits the activity of key enzymes involved in 
denitrification, such as Nar and Nir activity. It alters the structure of 
cytochrome c in the electron transport chain, reducing electron transfer 
efficiency (Luo et al., 2021). As a result, NO3
−/NO2
−
reduction is hin­
dered, and TN removal is impaired. Additionally, high salt concentra­
tions increase the competition for carbon sources, creating an imbalance 
in the C/N ratio (Ruan et al., 2020). This issue disrupts the availability of 
electron donors for denitrification, leaving nitrogen oxides (NOx
−) in the 
effluent. The accumulation of NO2
−
due to inhibited nitrification can 
feedback to suppress AOB activity, creating a vicious cycle of reduced 
nitrogen removal. Interactions with other parameters, such as DO, pH 
and temperature, further amplify these effects. Specifically, lower DO 
can hinder aerobic processes, while an optimal pH and temperature are 
crucial for maintaining effective nitrogen removal (Mishra et al., 2022).
3.6. Implications and outlook
This study used interpretable machine learning to predict nitrogen 
removal in MBR systems treating high-salinity wastewater. CatBoost 
model showed significant improvements in R2 (9 %) and RMSE (14 %) 
for NH4
+-Nout, and similar gains for TNout. SHAP analysis highlighted 
salinity as a key factor, disrupting nitrification and denitrification, while 
CODeff, DO and C/N ratio influenced microbial activity. Prior studies 
have consistently demonstrated the inhibitory effect of high salinity on 
ammonia oxidation by ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB) (Shi et al., 
2021; Xu et al., 2024). The SHAP analysis corroborates this but adds 
depth by revealing how salinity interacts with other key parameters, 
such as COD removal efficiency. Specifically, we found that high COD 
removal exacerbates carbon competition and starving nitrifiers of 
essential substrates, a nuanced finding not emphasized in prior work. 
Research on denitrification under saline conditions has shown that 
salinity impairs Nar activity (Li et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2015). Salinity 
inhibits Nar and alters the microbial community structure, further 
impairing carbon transfer to anoxic zones. Previous studies have 
explored this interplay less, often focusing on single stressors.
The temperature-related findings also align with previous research 
but provide more operationally relevant insights. Luo et al. (2019) 
examined temperature effects on nitrification in non-saline systems but 
did not account for the complex interactions with salinity. The SHAP 
waterfall plots indicate that below 20 ◦C, Nar activity decays signifi­
cantly due to Arrhenius-type behaviour, but this effect diminishes above 
25 ◦C due to thermal inertia. This insight provides more specific guid­
ance for operational strategies in saline wastewater treatment. 
Furthermore, while studies on aeration optimization have focused on 
improving DO for nitrogen removal (Zhang et al., 2019), this research 
highlights specific DO thresholds (0.5–1.2 mg/L) for optimal perfor­
mance under salinity stress. This result contrasts with prior work that 
provided general aeration guidelines, offering a more precise opera­
tional range.
This study’s model has some limitations for direct application in real- 
world environments. Extensive calibration is required when applying 
the model to wastewater with different compositions and for potential 
variations in plant-specific operational conditions (e.g., fluctuations in 
salinity levels). Continuous monitoring of environmental parameters is 
also necessary to ensure reliable model predictions. Additionally, the 
study focuses solely on high-salinity wastewater, and further adapta­
tions may be needed for other types of influent. Future research could 
explore the combined effects of salinity with other stressors like heavy 
metals (Ar´evalo et al., 2013) or organic pollutants (Holloway et al., 
2014), as these could compound the challenges of nitrogen removal. 
Factors such as adsorbents and membrane fouling could be incorporated 
into future models to refine operational strategies (Xu et al., 2024). 
Further validation under real-world conditions and incorporation of 
additional variables will strengthen model applicability and robustness.
4. Conclusion
This study leveraged interpretable machine learning to predict ni­
trogen removal in a MBR treating high-salinity wastewater. CatBoost 
achieved the best performance with R2of 0.88–0.91 and RMSE of 
4.27–4.35 for NH4
+-Nout and TNout. Compared to the second-best model, 
it improved R2 by 9 %-10 % and reducing RMSE by 14 %-16 %, 
respectively. SHAP analysis identified salinity as the dominant factor, 
disrupting nitrification via enzyme inhibition and denitrification 
through carbon metabolic competition. CODeff and DO governed mi­
crobial activity, while temperature modulated denitrifier enzyme ki­
netics. PDPs further revealed nonlinear thresholds in salinity and C/N 
ratio effects. These findings demonstrate how multi-faceted explain­
ability tools can decode complex interactions in biological systems. 
Targeted operational adjustments, such as salinity control and carbon 
supplementation, can also be guided to optimize nitrogen removal. 
Interpretable machine learning bridges predictive modelling and 
mechanistic understanding, offering actionable strategies for enhancing 
MBR performance in high-salinity wastewater treatment.
