A B S T R A C T
Accurate prediction of coagulant dosing is critical to ensure the efficiency and cost-effectiveness of the water 
treatment process. This study developed an accurate prediction model for coagulant dosing in drinking water 
treatment plants using automated machine learning (AutoML) and enhanced its transparency with the SHAP 
(Shapley Additive explanation) method. Results showed that the Random Forest (RF) model developed by 
AutoML outperformed the best single Gradient Boosting Tree model, with a 37 % lower RMSE of 0.89, a 52 % 
lower MAE of 0.47, and a 5 % higher R2 of 0.96. The RF model has the potential to save 10.25 % of coagulant 
dosage per year, specifically, 222 kg/d of PACl at a cost of 180.7 yuan/d for Yangtze River water, and 225 kg/ 
d at a cost of 183.2 yuan/d for Luan River water, while ensuring a more stable water quality in the treated water. 
SHAP analysis identified conductivity, ammonia nitrogen, chemical oxygen demand, and temperature of raw 
water as key factors affecting PACl dosage, which significantly impacts treated water pH. The combination of 
AutoML and SHAP method applied to intelligent water management, can bolster water treatment efficiency and 
management practices, resulting in noteworthy environmental, economic, and social benefits.
1. Introduction
With the rapid development of water treatment technologies, the 
issues of energy consumption and carbon emissions are becoming 
increasingly prominent [1,2]. In order to achieve the goal of green and 
low-carbon, traditional processes have been continuously optimized, 
but they are still subject to technical and economic constraints: 
adsorption technology is difficult to regenerate; photocatalytic deep 
mineralization is limited by the efficiency; and microwave catalytic ef­
ficiency is high, but the cost of the equipment is high [3–6]. Meanwhile, 
the complexity of the water treatment process places higher demands on 
the chemical dosing accuracy, especially in coagulation, whose oper­
ating cost accounts for a high proportion of the total cost of water plants 
[7,8], and multivariate (water temperature, pH, etc.), nonlinear dy­
namic response, and real-time disturbances make it difficult for tradi­
tional empirical models to balance dosing accuracy and energy 
efficiency [9,10]. Therefore, determining the optimal coagulant dosage 
based on raw water quality and established treated water quality criteria 
is critical to ensure water treatment effectiveness and to realize eco­
nomic and environmental benefits [11].
Traditional manual dosing is highly subjective, resulting in high drug 
consumption and difficult to adapt to large-scale production, resulting in 
insufficient stability of water control and regulatory lag [12]. Mecha­
nistic models, on the other hand, struggle to capture the complex re­
actions and nonlinear relationships within the coagulation process, 
thereby limiting their practical applicability [13]. Alternatively, data- 
driven models utilizing machine learning can construct black-box 
models for nonlinear, multivariate dynamic systems by learning from 
limited datasets [14]. However, current models have limitations in 
simulating and predicting anticoagulant dosing, particularly in terms of 
generalizability and convergence [15]. Among the existing methods for 
coagulation dosing prediction, DL-MV relies on parameter tuning for 
high-precision prediction [16]; PSO-SVR optimization requires manu­
ally preset parameters [17]; and GA-RF architectures are hardened and

have two limitations: first, they rely on manual intervention, making it 
difficult to automate the adjustment of complex nonlinear water quality 
relationships; second, they adhere to a single optimization paradigm and 
lack dynamic synergistic mechanisms, resulting in limited predictive 
efficiency and generalizability.
Automated Machine Learning (AutoML) builds efficient ML pipelines 
by automating the entire process [19], and its distributed frameworks (e. 
g., TPOT) enable fully automated model filtering to reduce development 
costs and labor dependency [20]. Studies have shown that AutoML ex­
cels in hyperparameter optimization and complex constraint solving 
[21–24]. TPOT improves accuracy by 15–20 % without feature reduc­
tion for 50-dimensional water quality data [25]. The H2O framework 
accurately predicts biological nutrient removal in wastewater [26], and 
TPOT outperforms traditional ML by 1.4 % in the lake water quality 
classification task [27]. Nevertheless, its exploration and application in 
coagulation agent dosing prediction have not yet been extensively 
pursued.
While some studies have used advanced ML techniques, their black- 
box nature limits interpretability and hinders practical application in 
water treatment plants, where operational transparency is critical. The 
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method proposed by Lundberg 
and Lee [28], which significantly improves model transparency, credi­
bility, and decision making by quantifying the marginal contribution of 
features to model predictions and identifying key influences and their 
interaction effects through global and local analysis [29]. The SHAP 
method has been applied in many fields [30–34] and has been used with 
high accuracy for surface water quality prediction [35–37]. However, 
this method has not been applied to coagulant dosage prediction.
Based on existing research, this study applies the interpretable 
AutoML framework to coagulation dose prediction for the first time, 
constructs the prediction system through automated model selection 
and hyper-parameter optimization, and integrates the SHAP method to 
achieve the global feature importance and local decision interpretation 
of the black-box model. By using real-time data and historical infor­
mation, it ensures that the coagulation process can be accurately 
controlled while maintaining treated water quality standards, facili­
tating future technological advances and upgrades in the water treat­
ment industry.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Data acquisition and preprocessing
The data utilized in this study were sourced from a water treatment 
plant located in Tianjin, China. This plant receives water from two pri­
mary sources: the Luanhe River (for December, January and February 
annually), diverted from Hebei Province, and the Yangtze River (from 
March to November annually), originating from the Danjiangkou 
Reservoir in Hubei Province. The water treatment process involves pre- 
treatment, enhanced conventional treatment, and a combination of ul­
traviolet and chlorine disinfection. The daily treatment capacity of the 
water plant is 17.5 wt/d, and the hydraulic retention time is relatively 
stable at 25.56 min. The plant uses two types of coagulants: ferric 
chloride and PACl, which are dosed during the pretreatment and 
blending stages, respectively. Water quality parameters and coagulant 
dosing data were collected daily from October 2022 to May 2024, 
totaling 1339 data points. These data were randomly divided into a 
training set (1071 entries) and a test set (268 entries) in an 8: 2 ratio. The 
feature variables consist of inflow rate (WTR) and water quality in­
dicators such as temperature (T), pH, turbidity (NTU), and electrical 
conductivity (EC), and chemical oxygen demand determined by potas­
sium permanganate (CODMn) for both raw water (RW) and treated water 
(TW), with ammonia nitrogen (NH3−N) being considered only for raw 
water. Based on practical plant operations and professional evaluations, 
the dosing amounts of the two coagulants are comparable, with a 
maximum discrepancy not exceeding 5 mg/L. Therefore, PACl was used 
as the only target variable for accurate simulation of coagulant dosing in 
this study.
During data collection, the occurrence of detection errors and dosing 
system malfunctions resulted in the raw data set containing problems 
such as missing values and noise. Therefore, preprocessing is essential to 
improve data reliability, model performance, and decision quality [38]. 
In this study, the K-nearest neighbor (KNN, k = 4) estimation model was 
used to deal with missing data, which balances interpolation stability 
and global representativeness, and provides better data retention for 
non-randomly missing key parameters (e.g., water temperature, 
ammonia nitrogen). The interpolation effect in the range of k = 1–10 
was searched through the grid and the mean square error (MSE) was 
used as the evaluation criterion, and the results showed that the MSE 
was minimized on the test set at k = 4. In addition, noise and random 
errors were mitigated using a moving average filter with a window size 
of n = 5, determined based on an autocorrelation function (ACF) anal­
ysis, a value that effectively captures short-term fluctuations in water 
quality parameters while avoiding excessive smoothing that leads to 
peak attenuation [39].
To investigate the correlations between various features such as 
WTR, raw water quality indicators, treated water quality indicators, and 
coagulant dosage, we used the Spearman rank correlation coefficient 
(rs) method, which is known for its low sensitivity to nonlinear re­
lationships and outliers. By excluding features with low correlations, we 
reduced redundant features to reduce the input dimensionality of the 
model, thereby improving both the operational efficiency and general­
ization capabilities of the model. A flowchart illustrating the modeling 
methodology is shown in Fig. 1.
2.2. Automated machine learning
AutoML is a technique for automating the machine learning process 
through algorithms, including feature engineering, algorithm selection, 
and hyperparameter optimization [40]. Its primary goal is to reduce the 
need for human intervention while improving model performance. In 
this study, the Tree-based Pipeline Optimization Tool (TPOT), an 
AutoML tool based on genetic programming, is used to automate model 
construction. TPOT is an open source Python-based AutoML library 
(version 0.11.7) with underlying dependencies on Scikit-Learn (version 
1.0.2), DEAP (Distributed Evolutionary Algorithms Framework), and 
extensions such as XGBoost [27]. The model runs in a Python 3.8 
environment, and the full configuration code for TPOT is open source.
In this study, the TPOT framework is adopted to automate the design 
of machine learning pipelines, whose core mechanisms are: iterative 
optimization based on tree-structured coding of genetic programming, 
integration of Scikit-Learn multi-algorithms with predefined hyper- 
parameter space to balance the search efficiency; and screening of the 
Pareto-optimal pipeline by repeating the 10-fold cross-validation (run 
independently for 3 times) to ensure the model generalization perfor­
mance [41].
2.3. SHAP interpretability method
The SHAP methodology encompasses Kernel SHAP, Deep SHAP, and 
Tree SHAP [42]. This study used the Tree SHAP package in Python 3.9 to 
analyze the importance, dependencies, and interactions of features. The 
modules and functions used in Python are listed in Table 1. The average 
absolute SHAP values were used to evaluate the importance of different 
factors in determining the dosage amount. By summing the average 
absolute SHAP values for all relevant environmental parameters, their 
combined impact on dosing strategies was quantified. Dependency plots 
of SHAP values revealed interactions between features and their col­
lective contribution to the prediction of dosing strategies for the coag­
ulation process [43]. Two typical samples were selected to generate 
local interpretability plots, waterfall plots, and decision plots in order to

2.4. Model evaluation
Root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and 
coefficient of determination (R2) were used as evaluation metrics to 
assess the reliability and accuracy of the predictive models [44]. RMSE 
assesses the degree of discrepancy between predicted and actual values 
within a sample set [45]. It has an increased sensitivity to outliers and 
acts as a metric of model accuracy by calculating the average size of 
prediction errors. MAE reflects the average discrepancy between pre­
dicted and actual values, with values closer to zero indicating superior 
model performance [10]. R2 quantifies the fit of model predictions to 
actual data, assessing the robustness and reliability of the model. A value 
of R2 approaching one indicates a higher correlation between the 
dependent and independent variables, which indicates a better model fit 
and consequently smaller prediction errors [46].
3. Results and discussion
3.1. Analysis of water quality data and feature selection
Fig. 2 illustrates the histogram distributions and statistical in­
dicators, including minimum (MIN), maximum (MAX), mean, standard 
deviation (SD), and coefficient of variation (CV) for each feature. CV 
serves as a metric to quantify the variability in datasets [47], with higher 
CV values indicating greater data variability. Notably, significant dif­
ferences are observed in T (SD = 9.37, CV = 62 %), NH3-N (SD = 0.07, 
CV = 59 %), NTU (SD = 2.58, CV = 57 %) for raw water, and T for 
treated water (SD = 8.77, CV = 54 %). Most other features remain 
relatively stable, with the pH fluctuation was minimal throughout the 
entire water treatment process, with a CV of only 3 % for the raw water 
pH and a CV of merely 2 % for the treated water pH.
The quality of raw water has a direct impact on the safety and reli­
ability of the final water supply, as well as coagulant requirements. Raw 
water quality indicators at this plant, such as CODMn and ammonia ni­
trogen, exhibit distinct seasonal trends. During summer, elevated tem­
peratures and increased rainfall stimulate algae growth in water sources, 
which raises the CODMn in water bodies and subsequently affects raw 
water quality. Additionally, rising temperatures reduce water viscosity, 
promoting ion movement in solutions and leading to decreased coagu­
lant requirements. In contrast, during winter, low temperatures decrease 
the solubility and diffusivity of coagulants, resulting in a deceleration of 
chemical reactions. Consequently, higher coagulant dosages become 
necessary to ensure the effectiveness of water treatment. Accurate 
calculation of coagulant dosage based on real-time raw water quality 
data and historical fluctuation information is crucial for maintaining 
process efficiency, stable water quality output, and prudent chemical 
use.

3.2. Model comparison and optimization
In this study, alongside TPOT AutoML, a range of typical algorithms 
were chosen to develop a prediction model of PACl dosage for com­
parison, including Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Regression (SVR), 
K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Linear Regression (LR), and Gradient 
Boosting Tree (GBT). Table 2 lists the hyperparameters of the models 
and Table 3 lists the performance metrics for each model. The optimal 
algorithm selected by AutoML was Random Forest (RF), which provided 
more robust and accurate predictions compared to the other algorithms. 
The R2 values of the RF model for the training and testing sets reached 
1.00 and 0.96, respectively, with the corresponding lowest RMSE: 0.95 
± 0.11 mg/L and MAE: 0.47 ± 0.83 mg/L. Kim et al. [48] predicted PAC 
dose using a convolutional neural network and gated recurrent units, 
and the model R2 exceeded 0.8 for both the training and test sets. This 
may be due to the fact that the model is typically used to extract 
spatiotemporal features, and the main drivers of PAC dose prediction are 
static water quality parameters (e.g., pH, turbidity, temperature, etc.) 
rather than time series or spatial patterns, which can introduce unnec­
essary complexity and lead to information redundancy. Lin et al. [39] 
used deep learning to predict PAC and its sulfate with a model RMSE of 
3.55 and an R2 of 0.94, while the error was even smaller when predicting 
coagulants using AutoML. Genetic programming for TPOT often favors 
integrated methods, which are inherently better for small- to medium- 
sized data with better bias-variance balance. The worst performance 
was observed for SVR, with R2 values of only 0.13 and 0.06 for the 
training and test sets, respectively. This is likely due to the high sensi­
tivity of SVR to hyperparameter selection and the lack of a precise so­
lution in defining the configuration parameters [49]. DT achieved an R2 
of 1.00 on the training set, but performed poorly on the testing set 
because it tends to build complex models on the training data that 
capture noise and details rather than the true data distribution patterns, 
resulting in poor predictive performance on new data.
Fig. 4 shows the training and testing results of the RF model devel­
oped using AutoML for predicting coagulant dosage. As shown in Fig. 4a 
and b, the model exhibits robust performance and high accuracy on both 
the training and testing sets. The low concentrations of suspended solids, 
colloids, and other contaminants in the raw water result in high quality 
water, and the minimal influence of pH-RW and NTU-RW on the coag­
ulation process means that the errors observed in the testing dataset are 
predominantly below 20 mg/L. From the scatter density plots in Fig. 4c 
and d, it can be observed that the actual and predicted PACl dosages on 
the training and testing set are mostly aligned along the diagonal, 
indicating a minimal potential bias in the model.

3.3. SHAP interpretation
Fig. 6 presents the SHAP feature importance and summary plots for 
the model evaluated on the testing set. Feature importance ranking from 
highest to lowest is as follows: EC-RW, pH-TW, NH3-N-RW, CODMn-RW, 
T-RW, NTU-RW, WTR, NTU-TW, and pH-RW, as shown in Fig. 6(a). 
Notably, the model in this study was constructed using monitoring data 
from the water treatment plant, adhering to the principle of continu­
ously adjusting dosage based on raw water quality to ensure stable and 
compliant treated water quality. Therefore, the relationship between 
dosage and treated water quality exhibits a direct causal link, despite 
dosage exceeding a certain threshold may have minimal impact on 
treated water quality. On the other hand, the relationship between 
dosage and raw water quality reflects practical expertise, with water 
plant operators relying on their experience and raw water quality to set 
chemical dosages that meet treated water standards. Because of the wide 
variation in raw water quality and the stability of treated water quality, 
raw water indicators are generally more important than treated water 
indicators in the model.
Fig. 6 (b) displays the contribution of input features to the prediction 
results, with features arranged in descending order along the vertical 
axis. Consistent SHAP values aligned with characteristic values for EC- 
RW, NH3-N-RW, CODMn-RW, and NTU-RW suggest a positive influ­
ence on coagulant dosing, while the opposite trend for pH-RW, T-RW, 
and WTR suggests a negative influence on dosing. For example, higher 
EC-RW corresponds to higher SHAP values, indicating an increase in 
dosing. Higher EC implies lower water purity, potentially containing 
higher concentrations of dissolved salts, organics, metal ions, and other 
contaminants. The cations in PACl can neutralize the surface charges of 
negatively charged suspended particles or colloidal substances in water, 
reducing the electrostatic repulsion between particles and promoting 
aggregation [50]. In contrast, higher WTR values correspond to lower 
SHAP values, indicating a decrease in dosage.
Fig. 7 illustrates the SHAP values for individual variables, which 
demonstrates the marginal effects of each feature on model predictions. 
For the raw water indicator, conductivity reflects the total concentration 
of dissolved ions in the water and shows a nearly linear enhancement 
relationship with dosage when it is between 300 and 550 μS/cm, while 
below 300 μS/cm or above 550 μS/cm this relationship shows a trend of 
diminishing returns. High conductivity compresses the bilayer and 
promotes coagulation, but low pH inhibits Al(OH)3 formation when 
incremental compensation is required. Ammonia is nitrogen in the form 
of free ammonia (NH3) and ammonium ions (NH4
+) regulated by pH and 
temperature. NH3-N-RW in the range of 0–0.2 mg/L also exerts a posi­
tive influence on coagulant dosage, with the relationship becoming less 
significant beyond this range. High temperature accelerates the hydro­
lysis, but low NH3-N can reduce the interference, at this time the PACl 
dosage can be dosed according to professional judgment.
CODMn reflects the total amount of organic matter and reducing 
inorganic matter in the water body, there is no obvious correlation with 
the dosage, if it is less than 4 mg/L, and more than 4 mg/L threshold, it 
will compete for the active site with the PACl, and the SHAP value at this 
time shows a sharp upward trend. Furthermore, NTU-RW generally has a 
positive promoting effect on dosing, as an appropriate increase in 
coagulant to promote particle aggregation and settlement, which is in 
line with the findings of Vunain et al. [51]. However, when NTU-RW is 
less than 2 NTU, the SHAP value shows an upward trend, as sparse and 
dispersed colloidal particles in low-turbidity water limit the ability of 
colloids formed after PACl hydrolysis to capture solid impurity particles. 
Therefore, the dosage of PACl must be increased to compensate for 
diffusion limitations and to ensure electroneutralization [52–54]. In 
high CODMn and high turbidity water, organic matter can encapsulate 
colloidal particles and additional amounts of PACl are needed to 
enhance charge neutralization and break down the organic matter 
barrier.
In addition, the required coagulant dosage decreases as the pH-RW 
increases within the range of 8.0 to 8.4, which is attributed to the 
opposite charges of PACl and contaminant particles in a weakly alkaline 
environment [55], allowing PACl to exhibit superior coagulation prop­
erties [56]. It is likely that temperature typically has a negative effect on 
dosing. The hydrolysis of PACl is an endothermic reaction, but the 
increased viscosity at low temperatures impedes the thermal motion of 
suspended particles in water, which is detrimental to the stability of 
colloidal aggregation [57]. Therefore, the dosage of PACl should be 
increased to achieve optimal coagulation efficacy [58].

hydrolysis reaction of PACl releases H+, resulting in a decrease in 
effluent pH [59]. However, over-dosing chemicals to meet the effluent 
pH standard will cause the colloids to re-stabilize, resulting in a decrease 
in flocculation efficiency. In addition, the increase in dissolved 
aluminum residue can affect the safety of the effluent [54]. In addition, 
no apparent linear relationship is observed between PACl dosage and 
NTU-TW after coagulation, contrary to the results reported by Chiavola 
[60]. NTU-TW is affected by both raw water quality and coagulant 
dosage. Specifically, elevated NTU-RW values require a manual increase 
in coagulant dosage to meet NTU-TW standards. Therefore, the NTU-TW 
values from an operating plant remain relatively low and stable with 
minimal variation, which has little impact on dosage prediction. In 
addition, the effectiveness of PACl in the coagulation process depends 
primarily on charge neutralization and adsorption bridging. An appro­
priate dosage of PACl hydrolyzes to produce positively charged ions that 
neutralize negatively charged colloids, thereby enhancing turbidity 
removal. However, excessive PACl triggers “colloidal protection,” where 
excess positively charged ions adsorb onto colloidal surfaces and 
hydrophobic colloids become hydrophilic, reducing floc settling and 
ultimately decreasing turbidity removal efficiency [56,61,62].
Although AutoML (TPOT) and SHAP perform well in optimizing 
machine learning models and improving interpretability, they still have 
several limitations. AutoML may face the risk of overfitting during 
model selection and hyperparameter optimization, and in this study, 
despite the training set R2 = 1.00, the high consistency of the repeated 
cross-validation and the independent test set (R2 = 0.96) suggests that 
the model was not overfitted. The SHAP method reveals complex action 
laws that cannot be captured by traditional linear methods, identifies 
the threshold response behavior of key parameters, and breaks through 
the limitations of linear statistics.

In summary, the model developed in this study optimizes coagulant 
dosing, not only enabling precise chemical addition in the coagulation 
process of water treatment plants—thereby significantly minimizing 
coagulant overuse—but also employing interpretable methods to reveal 
key factors and their impact relationships influencing coagulant con­
sumption. This empowers plant operators to make more scientific and 
rational decisions, avoiding overdosing while ensuring effluent quality. 
However, in practical water treatment applications, further efforts are 
required to mitigate the environmental impacts of coagulant use. This 
can be achieved by integrating the model with processes such as pre- 
chlorination and activated carbon adsorption to reduce coagulant de­
mand. Additionally, developing green and sustainable water treatment 
technologies, such as bio-based flocculants (e.g., chitosan, microbial 
flocculants), photocatalytic technologies[63,64], could reduce reliance 
on non-renewable resources and minimize environmental risks posed by 
chemical residues and byproducts (e.g., aluminum hydroxide sludge).
