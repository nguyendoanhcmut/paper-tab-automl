A B S T R A C T
This study presents a data-driven approach for the water management sector, utilizing a logistic 
regression (LR) framework to predict effluent ammonium nitrogen (NH4-Neff) concentrations and 
assess water reuse suitability. The system under investigation is an outdoor membrane photo­
bioreactor (MPBR) operating as a tertiary treatment step for nutrient removal from the effluent of 
an anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR), which is responsible for primary organic matter 
removal from municipal wastewater. The proposed framework estimates the likelihood of 
compliance with stringent Italian reuse regulations (NH4-Neff < 1.6 mg N/L). Utilizing 1.5 years 
of continuous outdoor operational data, eight binary LR models were developed. The input 
variables are explicitly defined and encompass operational parameters, solids retention time 
(SRT), hydraulic retention time (HRT), aeration flowrate (Fair), and nitrogen loading rate (NLR), 
as well as environmental and biological factors, including light availability, temperature, dis­
solved oxygen, and microalgal biomass indicators. An advanced control strategy was simulated to 
optimize the four operational parameters simultaneously. In-silico projections demonstrated 
significant practical advantages over fixed operating conditions: enhanced NH4-Neff removal ef­
ficiency (12–50% reduction), decreased aeration requirements (32–38% reduction in Fair), and 
increased production of compliant reclaimed water (up to 750 L per m3 of treated wastewater). 
The proposed framework could therefore support supervisory decision-making and potential 
integration with existing SCADA systems while retaining a transparent analytical structure.

1. Introduction
The microalgae sector holds significant potential within the emerging blue bioeconomy and can contribute to green objectives 
including decarbonisation, pollution reduction, circular economy, the protection of ecosystems, as well as supporting the UN Sus­
tainable Development Goals [1,2]. Despite this potential, the microalgae market remains limited. For instance, between 2016 and 
2020, the algae sector in the European Union contributed less than 0.01% to the EU's total Gross Domestic Product [3]. The devel­
opment of the sector is hampered by several issues, including insufficient market knowledge, uncertainty about the risks and envi­
ronmental impact of microalgae by-products, and a fragmented regulatory framework, which continues to result from limited 
knowledge about microalgae cultivation in the long term [4], which ultimately prevents the optimisation of large-scale processes. 
These knowledge gaps ultimately hinder the optimisation of large-scale processes, particularly when wastewater serves as a cultivation 
medium. Although wastewater-based cultivation enables nutrient recovery in the form of microalgal biomass that can be used to 
obtain a portfolio of bioproducts [5], prevention criteria impose additional constraints on this approach.
A key by-product in this facility is reclaimed water, i.e., treated wastewater meeting water-quality standards, which has the po­
tential to reduce hydric pressures in water-scarce regions and enhance circularity [6,7]. To be reused in Europe, reclaimed water must 
comply the European Regulation 2020/741 on water reuse [8]. This Regulation defines the maximum allowed concentrations of 
Escherichia coli, BOD5, TSS, turbidity, Legionella and helminth eggs across four quality classes (A–D). Nevertheless, some EU member 
states enforce more stringent national requirements that must also be met. In Italy, reclaimed water requirements are ruled by DM 
185-2003 [9], which entails compliance with 55 physicochemical and biological parameters, including generic macro-pollutants 
(COD, BOD5, TSS, nutrients), heavy metals, and selected organic pollutants. Among these, the ammonium concentration limit 
(NH4-Neff < 2 mg NH4/L, equivalent to <1.6 mg N-NH4/L) presents a major constraint for microalgae-based systems. This is partic­
ularly challenging when treating ammonium-rich streams such as raw sewage, primary effluent, or digestates from anaerobic digestion 
process, because ammonium serves as the primary nitrogen source for microalgae [10]. Depleting ammonium to such low levels can 
significantly restrict microalgal photosynthetic activity [11]. Consequently, ammonium concentration is arguably the most chal­
lenging parameter to control and satisfy the Italian legal limits for water reuse in microalgae-based systems. In fact, Hern´andez-Cuenca 
et al. [12] evaluated the potential health risks of reusing the reclaimed water treated by a membrane photobioreactor and reported that 
acceptable level of risks for most of the control parameters, except for the ammonium that did not meet the required water quality 
(<1.6 mg N/L) for approximately 50% of the operating period evaluated (55 days). This demonstrates that wastewater reuse from algal 
systems has the potential to be feasible both technically and legally, but the strict requirements on ammonium effluent concentrations 
warrants further evaluation.
So far, several strategies have been explored to reduce effluent ammonium concentrations in the long-term microalgal cultivation 
process. While indirect indicators (e.g., pH, dissolved oxygen) [13,14] or predictive weather modeling [15] have been utilized, 
advanced control primarily relies on mechanistic control models (MCM) [16–18] and fuzzy logic systems [19]. However, these existing 
approaches present distinct limitations regarding data requirements, mathematical complexity, practical implementation, and 
reproducibility. In terms of data, MCMs demand comprehensive, high-resolution datasets encompassing physicochemical and bio­
logical variables (e.g., specific autotrophic and heterotrophic bacteria concentrations) that are difficult and costly to measure 
continuously [20,21]. Regarding complexity, both MCMs and fuzzy logic systems require advanced solvers for non-linear differential 
equations or extensive, empirically defined rule bases. Consequently, their practical implementation into standard real-time process 
control platforms remains highly restricted [22] Implementation and reproducibility are further constrained by the need to calibrate 
numerous kinetic, stoichiometric, or rule-based parameters. Such calibration is commonly site-specific and may limit the trans­
ferability of a model to other facilities, operational conditions, or fluctuating outdoor environments. Consequently, models developed 
under short-term or simplified experimental conditions may not adequately represent the dynamics observed during long-term 
operation.
To address these limitations, the present study proposes a data-driven methodology based on logistic regression (LR). The origi­
nality of the proposed approach lies in applying an interpretable probabilistic classification framework to predict ammonium- 
compliance conditions in a long-term outdoor microalgal wastewater-treatment system. Unlike MCMs and fuzzy logic systems, the 
LR model does not require detailed biological measurements, extensive kinetic calibration, nonlinear numerical solvers, or a large set 
of empirically defined control rules. Instead, it produces a low-complexity analytical model that can be readily reproduced, validated, 
and implemented in standard process-control systems. Moreover, because LR directly estimates probabilities between 0 and 1, its 
output can be explicitly related to the binary regulatory criterion of compliance or non-compliance with the applicable effluent 
ammonium threshold.
Given the complexity of the processes occurring in microalgae consortia and the limitations of MCM models, machine learning 
models (ML) appear as an alternative for explaining the complex relationships between the microbial-based, operating and external 
(ambient) factors, which enables: i) the development of reliable models to develop efficient control strategies, ii) to optimize the 
cultivation process. ML models have been already used to predict algal biomass concentration and/or productivity [23–25], nutrient 
removal efficiency [18,26,27], as well as to identify microalgae strains in the reactor and their activity [28,29]. It has been shown that 
microalgae can form the basis of water reuse systems [28]. However, this aspect has not yet been widely discussed in the context of 
using ML models to optimize system performance. Currently, to model the performance of microalgal systems, neural networks [1,2,6,
9], multiple linear and non-linear regression [30–33], and other methodologies have been used (Table S1). However, a significant 
research gap remains: the majority of these advanced ML applications operate as ‘black-box’ algorithms (e.g., Artificial Neural Net­
works, Random Forests, XGBoost). While they offer high predictive accuracy, their lack of transparency hinders the extraction of 
physically interpretable operational rules, limiting their application for real-time control by plant operators [34].

Computational tools offering analytical mathematical forms provide a solution, yet traditional analytical models struggle to capture 
the complex, highly non-linear dynamics of microalgae cultivation processes [31]. Among the ML methods used in relation to 
microalgal systems, the logistic regression (LR) model, considered as a classification tool, has attracted considerable interest. One of 
the main advantages of LR is the analytical form of the model, in which the coefficients obtained had a physical interpretation, 
providing the possibility of implementing the model in technical conditions. This approach is particularly useful for predicting water 
reuse potential, as it yields probability estimates ranging from 0 to 1.
Another limitation in previous research relates to the scale of implementation. Simplified lab-scale studies on synthetic wastewater 
have provided limited insight into the interaction between relevant input parameters such as aeration rates, hydraulic (HRT) and solids 
retention times (SRT), nutrient loads and environmental factors (solar radiation, temperature, etc.). Consequently, existing models 
frequently omit crucial input variables. These highly simplified tools fail to explain parameter interactions, which is essential for 
understanding system dynamics and designing effective control strategies. Currently, control systems previously implemented under 
demonstrative conditions have primarily focused on manipulating (solely) SRT and HRT [19,20], obviating other relevant operational 
parameters such as air flowrate (Fair) and nitrogen loading rate (NLR). NLR is directly related to HRT in cultivation systems treating the 
single wastewater stream but can be decoupled by combining diverse streams to adapt the wastewater characteristics to the microalgae 
treatment capacity to optimize microalgal activity [12,35]. From a technological perspective, this uncoupling unlocks new possibilities 
for developing advanced, multidimensional control strategies in microalgae-based systems.
To address this gap, this study develops a transparent logistic regression (LR) framework for probabilistic assessment of effluent 
ammonium compliance in an outdoor MPBR treating the effluent of an Anaerobic Membrane Bioreactor (AnMBR) system fed by 
municipal wastewater. Unlike more complex mechanistic or less interpretable machine-learning approaches, LR provides an explicit 
analytical structure that directly estimates regulatory-compliance probability and facilitates interpretation and operational 
implementation.
The framework was developed using 1.5 years of continuous outdoor pilot-scale data and considers operational variables (solids 
retention time, SRT; hydraulic retention time, HRT; and aeration flow rate, Fair), influent nutrient-loading variables (nitrogen loading 
rate, NLR; and phosphorus loading rate, PLR), environmental conditions (photosynthetically active radiation, PAR; and temperature), 
and biological/process indicators (dissolved oxygen, DO; and optical density at 680 nm, OD680). The treatment capacity of this MPBR 
system was evaluated in previous studies [36,37], but the water reuse potential that could be achieved was not evaluated in the long 
term. The specific objectives were to: (i) develop and internally validate eight threshold-based LR models for predicting NH4-Neff 
ranges and regulatory-compliance probability; (ii) identify the most influential operational and environmental variables and evaluate 
their combined effects using local and global sensitivity analyses and multivariate cluster analysis; and (iii) evaluate, in silico, a su­
pervisory control strategy that simultaneously adjusts SRT, HRT, NLR, and Fair to reduce NH4-Neff and maximize the estimated pro­
duction of reclaimed water meeting the Italian reuse requirement [9].
The framework is limited by the discretization of the continuous NH4-Neff response and by the operating ranges represented in the 
training dataset. Further work should therefore include external validation under different wastewater characteristics, climates, and 
reactor configurations, together with prospective implementation and comparison or integration with mechanistic control models.
2. Methodology
The study followed a four-step framework (Fig. 1): Step 1, collection and pre-processing of long-term MPBR operational data; Step 
2, development and validation of the LR models for probabilistic classification of NH4-Neff; Step 3, local and global sensitivity analyses 
to identify influential variables and their interactions; and Step 4, in-silico evaluation of a multivariable supervisory control strategy to 
improve ammonium compliance and reclaimed-water production across four independent measurement campaigns (Table 1).
2.1. Measurement data
2.1.1. Experimental pilot plant
Experimental data were obtained from the 1.5-year continuous operation of a 470-L outdoor MPBR system comprising two flat- 
panel PBRs (1.25 × 2 × 0.1 m). These connected to a two-step harvesting system featuring 14-L tanks with 3.44 m2 hollow-fibre 
membranes (PURON® KMS PUR-PSH31, 0.03 μm). The PBRs were monitored using regularly calibrated on-line sensors for pH, 
temperature, dissolved oxygen, and vertically measured photosynthetically active radiation (PAR). A SCADA-controlled PLC managed 
data acquisition and regulated operational variables, including influent/effluent flows, HRT, SRT, and aeration [38]. This SCADA 
system also controlled the influent and effluent flows of the MPBR, allowing calculating the HRT and SRT of the system, as well as other 
operational variables such as Fair, simulating typical operations at large scale.
The MPBR system treated the effluent from a pilot-scale AnMBR treating municipal wastewater from Carraixet WWTP 
(39◦30′04.0″N 0◦20′00.1″W, Valencia, Spain) and operated continuously outdoors. While the AnMBR targeted organic matter and 
pathogens, the MPBR facilitated nutrient removal for water reclamation. The biomass obtained in both steps was treated in an 
anaerobic co-digestion (AcoD) process to maximize biogas production (Fig. S1). This combined system was evaluated in Seco et al. 
[39]. However, due to the relevance of the microalgal process in water reuse potential, detailed analysis of this second step was carried 
out in this study. The influent flow to the MPBR system was analysed by grab samples with the same frequency than the rest of MPBR 
parameters. This allowed evaluating the second stage of the process independently from the first step, so that biological performance of 
the AnMBR was not considered in this study, as the experimental dataset of this latter system was not sufficient to include it in the 
LR-framework.

2.1.2. Data pre-processing and input variables
The system yielded 230 samples across 10 experimental periods (2016–2018, 2–3 samples/week), excluding start-up and main­
tenance phases [40]. From 40 monitored variables, LR model inputs were selected based on previous multivariate (PCA, PLS) [40] and 
multiple linear regression analyses [31]. Selected inputs (Fig. 2) comprised: controllable operational parameters (HRT, SRT, Fair); 
inflow parameters (NLR, PLR); ambient factors (T, Tmax, PAR, PARmax); microalgal activity (DO, DOmax); and light distribution 
(OD680) [31].
Although NLR and PLR typically depend on HRT, they can be decoupled via stream blending to optimize biological activity and 
enable advanced control strategies [12,35]. Daily averages and maximums for PAR and T were incorporated, as their fluctuations 
critically dictate system performance [41,42]. Likewise, OD680 and DO serve as key indicators of light utilization and overall effi­
ciency [42]. Previous analyses of this dataset using multivariate projection methods (PCA and PLS) [40] and machine-learning 
techniques [31] identified the variables most strongly associated with process performance. Specifically, input variables for the LR 
model were selected based on correlation results and multiple linear regression (MLR) analysis, as detailed extensively in Szelag et al. 
[31]. Accordingly, the following inputs were considered (Fig. 2): (i) operational parameters (SRT, HRT, and Fair); (ii) influent con­
ditions (nitrogen (NLR) and phosphorus loading rates (PLR); (iii) photosynthetic activity indicators (DO and DOmax); (iv) ambient 
conditions (PAR and PARmax, and temperature T and Tmax); (v) light distribution, assessed by optical density at 680 nm (OD680); and 
(vi) effluent ammonium concentration (NH4–Neff). A Z-score pre-screening was performed to identify outliers and potential mea­
surement errors, with Zi = (xi −μi)/σi. All values were within the acceptance threshold (Zi = 0.01–2.95 < 3.0; Table S2), indicating no 
evidence of measurement errors in the analysed dataset.

Model performance was evaluated using accuracy (ACC), sensitivity/recall (SENS), specificity (SPEC), precision (Prec), the area 
under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC), and F1-score. Compliance with each selected NH4–N threshold was 
defined as the positive class. SENS therefore represents the proportion of compliant observations correctly classified, whereas spec­
ificity represents the proportion of non-compliant observations correctly classified. Precision quantifies the proportion of observations 
predicted as compliant that were experimentally compliant, while F1-score represents the harmonic mean of precision and recall. ROC- 
AUC evaluates the model's ability to discriminate between compliant and non-compliant observations across probability cut-offs. The 
equations used to calculate these metrics are provided in Supplementary Information, Section S1. 95% confidence intervals were also 
determined for all performance metrics.

the LR model, with 80% used for training and 20% for testing in each period (1–4), resulting in 210 episodes in which a wide range of 
NH4-Neff values was observed. Model training and validation were performed utilizing 10-fold cross-validation to reduce uncertainty

of the microbiota, whereas HRT values shorter than 1 d have shown to promote competing organisms such as heterotrophic and ni­

2-d HRT was the only period that the MHRAP system was able to remove nitrogen and phosphorus efficiently from municipal 
wastewater. In the other periods, the culture microbiome did not reach an equilibrium between microalgae and bacteria, thus reducing 
the performance of the system. Hence, knowing the appropriate SRT/HRT ranges of operation is essential for implementing this kind of 
systems.
From the predicted results, it was also observed that the lower the PARmax, the greater the influence of SRT on the probability of 
producing reclaimed water (Fig. 3a). This can be related to the fact that at low solar irradiances, the microalgae activity was probably 
photo-limited. This means that the amount of photos available was not enough to carry out the photosynthetic process efficiently, so 
that NH4 removal was below the optimal. Under these simulated conditions, the system would be expected to lengthen the SRT with 
the goal of providing more time (and subsequently more photons) to microalgae. As a consequence, PARmax below 1600 μmol m−2 s−1 
simulated NH4-Neff concentrations above the Preuse limit (Fig. 3b). On the contrary, at higher PARmax levels, photosynthetic activity 
may have approached a saturation threshold, where further increases in light intensity would not enhance ammonium uptake. 
Although excessive irradiance can lead to photoinhibition, such effects were not observed in this system, probably due to light 
attenuation from biomass shading. A similar trend was observed with OD680, but even with a greater influence than PARmax on the 
probability of reusing water (Fig. 3a and c).
As OD680 is an indirect measurement of the microalgal biomass [40], higher OD680 values are related to higher biomass pro­
duction, and subsequently, with higher NH4 consumption. From an operational perspective, the proposed approach offers several 
practical advantages for potential large-scale systems: i) it enables the estimation of NH4-Neff ranges without relying on costly NH4 
sensors, which require frequent maintenance and calibration [19]; ii) it supports the implementation of strategies for process opti­
misation (further details in Section 3.4); iii) the models could be integrated with weather forecasting tools to predict the treatment 
capacity of the system according to ambient conditions; iv) the curves determined by alternative logistic regression models do not 
require complex implementation unlike other MCM- or ML-based methodologies (Table S1). However, the accuracy of the results is 
limited as they are based on the probability of obtaining NH4-Neff within a certain range of values. The numerical NH4-Neff values used 
in this study are just approximations to the highest values of these ranges (as described in Section 2.2). As NH4-Neff concentration is the 
main limiting factor for reusing water, this approach could be enough for estimating the probability of not accomplishing reuse re­
quirements), which is highly relevant from a regulatory perspective.
3.3. Local sensitivity analysis (LSA)
LSA was performed using the sensitivity coefficient S to quantify the influence of each input on the predicted NH4–Neff, and 
therefore on the probability of water reuse compliance. Overall, the LSA indicated a strong influence of most of the evaluated envi­
ronmental and process variables (especially DO, DOmax, T, Tmax, PAR, PARmax, and OD680) on NH4–Neff (Fig. 4). This confirms that 
microalgae cultivation systems depend on multiple variables that need to be considered and evaluated simultaneously, implying that, 
even though control variables (SRT, HRT, Fair, NLR, PLR) are the only ones that could be managed by operators, the rest of input 
parameters need to be considered in the LR models. This was also confirmed by Szelag et al. [31], who carried out a multicriteria 
analysis of the same dataset of this study to develop multilayer prediction models (MLP). They showed relevant Shapley Indexes (>| 
0.1|) for most of the input parameters, although their relevance varied according to the output evaluated (biomass productivity, 
photosynthetic efficiency, nitrogen and phosphorus removal). Hence, to upscale the LR framework implemented in this study, not only 
the reactor design should be considered, but also it should contain similar automation and control system (described in Section 2.1).
Regarding specific groups of parameters, NLR exhibited a consistently higher influence on NH4– Neff (S = 0.26–0.45) than PLR 
(S = 0.03–0.25) (Fig. 4a). The relevance of nitrogen- and phosphorus-related variables for MPBR control is consistent with previous 
long-term observations. Zhang et al. [49] reported that varying the N/P ratio (3.9–9.7) affected performance during a 360-day 
lab-scale study (SRT = 20 d; HRT = 2.0–3.0 d; constant DO and temperature). Similarly, Gonz´alez-Camejo et al. [40] found 
nitrogen-related variables to be more influential than phosphorus in a PLS model of pollutant removal. This is related to the ‘luxury 
uptake’ capacity of microalgae. This means that microalgae are able to absorb large amounts of phosphorus (higher than stochiometric 
P) when this compound is in excess and store it intracellularly as polyphosphates. This intracellular P can then be used under con­
ditions of P-scarcity [50]. Consequently, P-removal (or P effluent concentration) are more difficult to correlate with input parameters

Regarding operational parameters, SRT was more influential (S = 0.04–0.21) than HRT (S = 0.04–0.11). The relevance of HRT and 
SRT for N and P removal has been widely reported in both lab-scale [49,51,52] and pilot-scale systems [53,54]. Nevertheless, their 
relationship with nutrient removal is only approximately linear within specific operating windows (Section 3.2). In this respect, the 
MPBR system of this study operated in the ranges HRT [0.9-3.2 d], and SRT [1.6-6 d]. Values out of these ranges should not be 
considered as operational under large-scale conditions, as they would produce sub-optimal results, as explained in Section 3.2. It must 
be also considered that HRT is inversely related to NLR (Eq. (5)), although NLR it can be partially controlled by blending different 
sewage streams such as primary effluent and centrate from anaerobic digestion [36]. In fact, Gonz´alez-Camejo et al. [55] reported 
influent water streams containing 20.2 ± 0.4 mgN-NH4/L when using primary effluent, whereas it increased to 35.1 ± 0.2 and 
134.2 ± 2.1 mgN-NH4/L when adding 10% and 30% of centrate, respectively. In this case, maximum N-removal was obtained when 
adding 10% of centrate, demonstrating that this approach can be useful to avoid nutrient limitation.
Aeration flow rate (Fair) is another key operational parameter because it governs culture mixing and, consequently, light–dark 
cycling, CO2 transfer, prevention of sedimentation, and limitation of biofilm formation on reactor walls, hence influencing in biomass 
concentration (measured as OD680), oxygen production and NH4-Neff concentrations [40]. Values around 0.22 vvm have been re­
ported as necessary to ensure adequate mixing in similar systems [56,57]. However, excessive mixing increases operating costs and 
may damage cells through shear stress [58,59]. In the studied MPBR, air mixing accounted for 78% of total energy use (0.288 kWh⋅ 
m−3 out of 0.369 kWh⋅ m−3) [35], supporting the need to minimise Fair while maintaining sufficient homogenisation. Consistently, LSA 
indicated that Fair had limited relevance at low NH4–Neff (1.6–8.0 mg N⋅ L−1; S = 0.10–0.13), probably because under favourable 
conditions, maintaining homogenisation in normal ranges could be enough to obtain efficient microalgae performance. However, its 
influence increased markedly at higher NH4–Neff (8.0–16.0 mg N⋅ L−1; up to S = 0.58) (Fig. 4b), indicating that this factor is more 
relevant when the system's performance decreases. Among ambient factors, temperature was the most influential variable in the 
NH4–Neff = 2–12 mg N⋅ L−1 range (ST = 0.35–0.50; R2 = 0.88). At higher NH4–Neff (>12 mg N⋅ L−1), the maximum temperature (Tmax) 
showed the highest influence (S = 0.34–0.68; R2 = 0.69). This pattern is consistent with evidence that exceeding the optimal tem­
perature by only 2–4 ◦C can accelerate microalgal deterioration, while sub-optimal temperatures tend to have a less severe effect [59]. 
In addition, sustained high temperatures (typically >30–32 ◦C) in mixed cultures can favour the proliferation of ammonia-oxidising 
bacteria (AOB), potentially reducing microalgal activity due to competition for ammonium uptake [20,21]. These results highlight 
the importance of temperature management in closed PBRs, particularly because internal reactor temperatures can exceed ambient 
conditions by 10–30 ◦C [60].
Light availability also influenced predictions, with PARmax and PAR showing relevant sensitivity (SPARmax = 0.25; SPAR = 0.22), 
confirming the central role of irradiance in microalgae-based processes, as reported in previous studies [31]. Actually, PAR is an 
indicator of the number of photons that are supplied to the microalgal system, which is not only related to light intensity, but also to 
light attenuation through the culture, i.e., the shadow effect [36,61]. Light irradiance is also related to indicators of photosynthetic 
activity, which also exhibited relevant influence on NH4–Neff (SDO = 0.25–0.83; SDOmax = 0.03–0.46; Fig. 4i and j). The high relevance 
of DO-related variables may also be linked to the use of closed PBRs, which can accumulate dissolved oxygen to higher levels than open 
systems [62].
The LSA evaluates the response to one predictor while holding the remaining variables constant and therefore does not quantify 
interactions among the model inputs. To address this limitation, a variance-based Sobol analysis was performed for the eight LR 
threshold models. The first-order index (Si) represents the independent contribution of each input to prediction variance, whereas the 
total-order index (STi) includes both its direct effect and its interactions with the remaining predictors. Consequently, the difference 
between STi and Si indicates the importance of interaction effects.
DO was particularly influential at the lower NH4–Neff thresholds of 1.6–4.0 mg N L−1, whereas temperature played a major role at 
intermediate thresholds of 4–12 mg N L−1. At thresholds above 8.0 mg N L−1, NLR became an increasingly important source of pre­
diction variability. The differences between Si and STi generally increased at higher ammonium thresholds, indicating stronger in­
teractions among nutrient loading, temperature, oxygen conditions, irradiance, and aeration. These results confirm that MPBR 
performance is governed by combined operational and environmental effects rather than by isolated predictors (Fig. S5).
The model sensitivities should be interpreted in the context of the coupled biological and engineering mechanisms governing the 
mixed microalgal–bacterial consortium. Although local sensitivity analysis evaluates predictors individually, MPBR performance 
emerges from interactions among biomass retention, hydraulic and nutrient loading, light availability, temperature, oxygen condi­
tions, and competition between functional microbial groups. Studies of these consortia confirm that ammonium removal can involve 
both microalgal assimilation and bacterial nitrogen transformations, with their relative contributions varying across operating con­
ditions [63,64].
SRT and HRT regulate complementary aspects of reactor performance. SRT determines biomass retention and microbial selection: 
an adequate SRT helps maintain an active microalgal–bacterial community, whereas a very long SRT can increase biomass concen­
tration and self-shading while permitting the retention of slow-growing nitrifiers. The resulting balance between microalgal assimi­
lation and bacterial nitrification is nevertheless system-specific; experimental evidence shows that changing SRT can substantially 
alter ammonium-removal pathways without establishing a universally monotonic response [63]. Long SRTs can also improve bio­
flocculation and membrane performance, further demonstrating that SRT affects more than nutrient conversion alone [65].
HRT determines hydraulic throughput, contact time, and volumetric nutrient loading. Because membrane filtration decouples 
hydraulic residence from biomass retention, shortening HRT primarily increases nutrient loading and biomass productivity but may

3.4. Development of MPBR control strategies
3.4.1. Interaction between operating parameters
For the control strategy based on the interaction among SRT⋅ HRT−1, Fair, and NLR, simulations indicated that increasing the SRT⋅ 
HRT−1 ratio (i.e., SRT/HRT) led to an approximately linear decrease in the required Fair to maintain preuse = 0.50 (Fig. 5a). As dis­
cussed above, higher Fair improves culture homogenisation, increasing the frequency of light–dark cycles (duty cycle) within the 
reactor. A higher duty cycle can enhance photosynthetic efficiency and reduce photoinhibition, as microalgae are exposed to high 
irradiance (when they are closed to the PBR's surface) for shorter periods of time, letting longer times for their photosynthetic 
apparatus to recover from excessive solar radiation when they are in darker zones of the reactor [63]. Higher duty cycles thus tend to 
support nutrient removal and thus increase water reuse potential. This implies that a balance may be found between the higher 
operating costs related to increasing Fair and the increase of reclaimed water volume. In this respect, water-energy nexus assessment is 
receiving increasing attention to evaluate in comprehensive manner the trade-offs between these two sectors that are often inversely 
correlated [68]. This is an issue to be evaluated in further studies.
In the present simulations, the increase of reclaimed water production (Preuse) with Fair was more pronounced at higher SRT⋅ HRT−1 
ratios. Although this combined ratio has been less frequently analysed than SRT and HRT separately, Xu et al. [69] also reported SRT⋅ 
HRT−1 as a relevant factor influencing performance in lab-scale microalgae reactors. However, from the best of authors’ knowledge, 
this fact has not been experimentally evaluated in large-scale systems, but the results of these simulations are promising.
For the relationship SRT = f (HRT, Fair), the simulations exhibited non-linear behaviour, which is consistent with outdoor 
microalgae-based systems where non-linear interactions among operating variables, environmental drivers and process outputs are 
common [31]. Comparable non-linear trends have been reported previously, including between SRT and biomass concentration at 
SRT = 10–30 d [52], and the broad operational variability of HRT in outdoor systems (2.7–14 d) [70]. While these non-linear re­
lationships demonstrate a robust statistical fit, their primary value lies in dictating practical MPBR operation. Operationally, the 
dependency established in Fig. 5a indicates that maintaining fixed SRT and HRT setpoints, a common practice in conventional 
wastewater treatment, is insufficient for outdoor microalgal systems subjected to high environmental variance.
From an engineering perspective, the models demonstrate that mechanical aeration (Fair) must be dynamically coupled with the 
combined SRT⋅HRT−1 ratio and the incoming nitrogen loading rate (NLR). Practically, this implies that during periods of elevated NLR 
or sub-optimal irradiance, operators cannot rely solely on the manipulation of hydraulic flows. Instead, an automated, preemptive 
increase in Fair is strictly required to satisfy the sudden oxygen demand exerted by competing nitrifying bacteria and to maintain 
suspension of the dense microalgal culture. Conversely, during periods of high photosynthetic activity and low influent loading, the 
model permits operators to safely down-regulate Fair. Given that aeration accounts for up to 78% of the total energy use in such MPBRs, 
translating these statistical thresholds into dynamic SCADA setpoints provides a direct mechanism to prevent biological process failure 
while simultaneously minimizing operational energy expenditures.

3.4.3. Advanced control strategy
A significant improvement in the simulation was achieved under the extended scenario, in which NLR and Fair were optimized 
alongside SRT and HRT (Table S10). In the extended in-silico scenario, NLR and F_air were optimized together with SRT and HRT 
(Table S10). The simulation projected reductions in NH4-Neff concentrations of 12–50% across the evaluated periods (Table 2). Ac­
cording to the model predictions, these reductions could increase the estimated volume of treated water meeting the reuse standard in 
5-67% compared to the baseline. These results represent simulation-based projections and require validation under controlled 
experimental or full-scale operating conditions.
This reduction in air flowrate would yield substantial energy savings for the MPBR system. A preliminary economic analysis of this 
specific MPBR setup by Gonz´alez-Camejo et al. [36] reported electricity requirements of 0.288 kWh⋅m−3 for aeration and 
0.369 kWh⋅m−3 for the entire system under fixed operating conditions. Based on the simulations, implementing the advanced control 
system could reduce the aeration energy requirement to 0.179– 0.196 kWh⋅m−3, bringing the total system energy consumption down 
to 0.260–0.277 kWh⋅m−3 (25-30% reduction of total energy consumption). Therefore, it is highly recommended that future control 
strategies do not rely solely on SRT and HRT, but instead simultaneously integrate Fair and NLR to optimize both biological perfor­
mance and resource efficiency (Fig. 7).
3.4.4. Advantages of the selected approach and future research
The approach employed in this study aligns with standard literature practices, wherein machine learning (ML) models are 
developed from experimental data, and the optimal model structure is identified during training and validation [68]. A primary 
advantage of the present approach is its ability to directly evaluate the dependencies between input variables and predicted outcomes, 
ensuring that the derived relationships are fundamentally sound from both a technological and biological perspective [72]. Conse­
quently, these ML models provide a robust foundation for the real-time optimisation and control of complex processes, tasks that 
traditionally require mechanistic control models (MCMs) [73]. However, calibrating MCMs is a highly complex endeavour; the 
resulting models are not always practical due to the intricate interactions and uncertainties among the calibrated kinetic and stoi­
chiometric parameters. In contrast, the logistic regression (LR) model developed in this study is highly interpretable and yields 
technologically correct operational dependencies.
Furthermore, this combination of logit models successfully mapped complex non-linear relationships, which frequently require 
complex “black-box” models such as MLP, SVM, or XGBoost [4,10]. Another advantage is the model's capacity to handle limited 
number measurement data, which typically poses a significant challenge when training multiclass ML models. The 1.5-year study 
period captured a wide range of wastewater quality fluctuations, encompassing episodes of both highly efficient system performance 
and significant process deterioration [37,40,47]. From a modelling perspective, this high variance was incredibly advantageous, 
enabling the creation of a robust tool capable of predicting NH4-Neff across widely varying conditions. In contrast, modelling 
microalgae-based systems at the pilot scale is often constrained by continuous high efficiency, which results in low output variance and 
limits the model's predictive scope. Despite a limited sampling frequency, all crucial factors governing the MPBR operation were 
captured [31].

Flow, DO, and temperature are commonly available as SCADA signals. In contrast, online TN, TP, PAR, and OD680 measurements

reagents, calibration, cleaning, and maintenance. Where such instruments are unavailable, laboratory measurements or validated 
virtual sensors may be used.
Because LR has low computational requirements, its equations can be implemented within SCADA software, programmable logic 
controllers, process historians, or external supervisory platforms. The model can calculate the probability of compliance with the 
ammonium threshold and provide operator warnings or recommendations for adjusting HRT and SRT.
Table 3 presents five illustrative implementation scenarios for facilities with different instrumentation levels. Practical deployment 
may initially occur in advisory mode. Following site-specific validation and the implementation of appropriate operational safeguards, 
the framework may be incorporated into automated supervisory control.
To further advance the predictive control and management of microalgae-based wastewater treatment and reuse systems, future 
research should focus on the following directions: 
i) Advanced Data Augmentation. To address the challenge of data scarcity common in long-term bioprocess monitoring, data 
augmentation techniques should be explored. Methods such as Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) or bootstrapping could 
be used to create larger, realistic synthetic datasets. This would not only improve the robustness and goodness-of-fit of the 
current models but also enable the training of more complex machine learning architectures that are currently unfeasible.
ii) Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs). Implementing PINNs based on fundamental differential equations (e.g., mass 
balances for substrate NH4-N and biomass) would seamlessly integrate physical and biological laws into the machine learning 
framework, ensuring predictions strictly adhere to physical constraints and enhancing the model's mechanistic validity and 
allowing for complex non-linear relationships and interactions between inputs.
iii) Sequential Hybrid Modelling (MCM þ ML). Developing a hybrid framework that integrates MCMs with ML models. MCMs 
could be used to simulate high-resolution internal biological state variables (e.g., specific biomass concentrations, inorganic 
nitrogen forms, dissolved oxygen, heterotrophic bacteria, ammonia-oxidising bacteria, nitrite-oxidising bacteria, and slowly 
biodegradable particulate organic matter). These simulated variables would then serve as inputs for the ML-based water reuse 
model. Additionally, ML algorithms could be deployed in series to dynamically correct the residual NH4-N prediction errors of 
the MCMs.
iv) Temporal Dynamics and Advanced Architectures. Incorporating the dynamic nature of input data on NH4-Neff estimates by 
moving beyond static models. High-resolution sensor data could be fed into advanced sequence models, such as Transformers or 
Liquid Neural Networks, which inherently account for temporal dependencies and operational delays.
v) Universal Model Development. Correlating the coefficients estimated via logistic regression with specific biological pa­
rameters (e.g., microalgae/bacteria ratios, activity of competing microorganisms) as well as physical and geographical con­
ditions, progressing toward the development of a universal predictive model that can be applied across different climates and 
reactor designs.

quantify water reuse potential in an outdoor membrane photobioreactor (MPBR) treating municipal wastewater. Based on 1.5 years of 
pilot-scale operational data, the following primary conclusions are drawn:

• Local sensitivity analysis indicated that photosynthetic activity (DO, DOmax) and ambient temperature exerted the strongest in­

episodes.
• Relying solely on hydraulic and solids retention times (HRT and SRT) is insufficient for maintaining optimal long-term performance 
under fluctuating outdoor conditions.
• In-silico simulations of an advanced control strategy—simultaneously optimising SRT, HRT, NLR, and Fair, projected a 12–50%

the estimated production of compliant reclaimed water.

simulations. Consequently, the broad applicability of this approach remains uncertain until subjected to further validation across 
diverse full-scale reactor designs, varying wastewater influent characteristics, and different climatic conditions.
