The air flow rate can be increased or decreased to maintain sustainable control without excessive energy use. Assumably, the 
system operates at ai, and nitrification is predicted at time ti (Fig. 5). If the model predicts complete nitrification ("True" label), the air 
flow rate remains at ai; otherwise, it is increased by A to ai +A Then, if operating the system through a measurement interval with air

predicted; if incomplete, it increases to ai + 2A. This process repeats at each subsequent interval (ti+2, ti+3, etc.), adjusting the air flow 
rate accordingly. As shown in Fig. 5, after determining whether the treatment system is fully nitrified, the air flow rate increases or 
decreases in units of A. The air flow rate can be reduced to the baseline ai minimally, but if it exceeds the aerator’s maximum capacity, 
an early warning system should trigger human intervention, such as discarding greywater or adding aeration equipment. Moreover, 
monitoring frequency can be increased during peak water usage to enhance treatment efficiency and reuse safety.
The binary classification framework demonstrates operational feasibility under data-constrained conditions. Extending the current 
framework to multiclass nitrification states—such as a four-level prediction scheme (25 %, 50 %, 75 %, and complete nitrification)— 
could enable proportional aeration control. This refinement would optimize energy use, reduce carbon footprint, and help ensure 
consistent effluent quality. Such models require expanded datasets from online sensors (Section 4.1) and specialized optimization 
techniques: multi-class threshold tuning addressing misclassification costs, and methods for imbalanced data (Davis and Goadrich, 
2006; Haibo He and Garcia, 2009; Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015). Threshold selection should account for the cost and risk of 
misclassification, beyond purely mathematical considerations. Additionally, class definitions should align with influent characteristics 
and reuse requirements.
Integrating the multiclassification model into the control system enables more precise adjustments, potentially eliminating the 
need for incremental changes (such as A in Fig. 5), but optimal aeration reduction strategies post-complete nitrification pre­
diction—gradual vs. immediate—require lab/pilot validation. Gradual reduction may be better for maintaining efficiency for strict 
reuse standards, necessitating risk-cost evaluations.
