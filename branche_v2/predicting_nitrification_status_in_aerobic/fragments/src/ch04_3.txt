3.3. Nitrification status prediction without biocarriers addition
Three classification models (LR, RF, XGB) were trained on datasets excluding biocarriers, with precision prioritized during 
hyperparameter optimization via stratified 5-fold cross-validation (SI 4). Table 1a summarizes prediction results using six selected 
input features, reporting accuracy, precision, TPR, and FPR for the test group, along with averaged results from cross-validation folds, 
illustrating the model’s predictive ability for nitrification status. Evaluation metrics for individual folds are provided in SI 5. Based on 
the limited data in the training (78) and test group (19), three key insights can be drawn from Table 1a: 
1) LR, RF, and XGB achieved comparable test group performance (accuracy: 0.79–0.84). The confusion matrices for the test group 
predictions by each algorithm are provided in SI 6. This good fit could be attributed to the mechanistic relationship between the 
inputs and the target output, involving nitrogen conversion in the nitrification process (Huang et al., 2022; Omar et al., 2024).
2) With considering the superior performance in validation groups (accuracy: 0.90–0.91), RF and XGB underperformed LR in the test 
group, indicating potential overfitting risks with limited data.
3) Additionally, the small data limited the ability to fully evaluate the performance difference among algorithms with different 
complexity, hard to assess the influence of model complexity on TPR and FPR.
These results preliminarily support the effectiveness of the ML algorithm in predicting the nitrification status through TMP, air flow 
rate, influent flow rate, and effluent COD, NO3
- -N, and NH4
+-N concentrations.

several factors. One primary reason is the issue of class imbalance. In the training set, negative label samples accounted for 64.10 %,

during testing, as models struggled to correctly classify underrepresented positive samples. This phenomenon was particularly evident 
in RF, where precision remained relatively high (0.8333) but TPR declined sharply to 0.6250 in the test set. The discrepancy between 
validation and test outcomes demonstrated that RF overfit to negative-class features during training, compromising its predictive

Feature importance analysis elucidated prediction mechanisms and guided data collection strategies. Table 1b shows importance 
scores with Kernel Density Estimates (KDE) for LR, RF, and XGB models. In LR, regression coefficients indicated feature-prediction 
directionality/magnitude. For RF and XGB, importance scores quantified feature contributions. KDE visualized probability density 
distributions for Predicted Positive (red) and Predicted Negative (blue) classes. Curve peaks revealed value concentration regions (e.g., 
Eff_COD peaked at ~30 for Positive class). Class discriminative ability was assessed through KDE overlap: minimal overlap indicated 
strong separability (e.g., Air Flow Rate showed moderate separation). Curve width reflected feature variance—narrow curves denoted 
consistent class values, while broader curves indicated higher variability.
Feature importance and KDE analysis confirmed effluent NH₄⁺-N and NO₃⁻-N as primary predictors of nitrification status. Although 
mechanistically relevant, influent flow rate (11.7–20.6 ML/min) and air flow rate (2–6 Lpm) showed limited contributions due to low 
operational variability. Effluent COD likewise had a minor impact, evidenced by its lower coefficient of variance (55.25 %) versus 
NH₄⁺-N (93.82 %) and NO₃⁻-N (101.72 %)—calculated as standard deviation relative to mean. This variability disparity explained 
differential feature importance.
SI 7 and 8 display pairwise plots across models and feature importance quantification across validation groups respectively, which 
confirmed effluent NO₃⁻-N/NH₄⁺-N as primary predictors, aligning with their biochemical centrality in nitrification. However, XGB 
validation groups showed unexpected prominence of stable influent and airflow rates over N-species. This stemmed from XGB’s 
boosting mechanism: sequential trees prioritize features correcting predecessor errors, dynamically weighting features during in­
cremental optimization (Bent´ejac et al., 2021; Nguyen et al., 2024). Such adaptability could enhance model flexibility across data 
groups but may inflate transient feature importance and overfitting risks.
Fig. 4 illustrates the SHAP analysis results for three predictive models—LR, RF, and XGB—including global importance (beeswarm 
plots, Fig. 4a–c) and instance-level decomposition (waterfall plots, Fig. 4d–f). The beeswarm plots revealed consistent feature 
importance rankings across all models: Effluent NH₄⁺-N and Effluent NO₃⁻-N dominate, followed by Effluent COD and TMP, suggesting 
robust, model-agnostic relevance of nitrogen and organic matter indicators in treatment performance prediction, consistent with the 
results of feature importance analysis.
More nuanced insights arose from the waterfall plots. In the LR model (Fig. 4d), the output was strongly driven by a single high 
NH₄⁺-N value (+2.84), counteracted by negative contributions from NO₃⁻-N and COD, reflecting the additive nature of linear models. 
Such strong reliance on a single dominant feature highlighted an overfitting risk: when NH₄⁺-N deviates from training patterns, the 
model struggles to generalize, explaining the decline in test TPR. In contrast, the RF model (Fig. 4e) showed a more balanced 
contribution from multiple features, with lower-magnitude but distributed impacts, consistent with ensemble decision averaging. The 
XGB model (Fig. 4f) exhibited stronger nonlinearity and interaction effects. Here, COD and NO₃⁻-N jointly exerted dominant negative 
influence, while NH₄⁺-N contributed positively but was insufficient to overcome process stress signals, resulting in a negative pre­
diction. Nevertheless, the complexity of these nonlinear feature interactions in XGB, combined with limited training samples, raised 
the likelihood of memorizing dataset-specific noise, another hallmark of overfitting. Notably, TMP and Air Flow Rate emerged as 
minor but non-negligible modulators, indicating sensitivity to operational states.

The SHAP analysis revealed that effluent NH₄⁺-N and NO₃⁻-N were the most influential predictors across all models, underscoring 
their critical role as indicators of nitrification performance in MBR systems. This suggested that real-time monitoring of these nitrogen 
species—rather than relying only on conventional parameters like TMP, DO in the reactor—could enhance operational control while 
monitoring essential effluent water quality parameters. Specifically, the identification of borderline cases (e.g., simultaneous NH₄⁺- 
N ≈2 mg/L and NO₃⁻-N ≈9 mg/L where false positives occur) highlighted process instability regions that were prone to misclassi­
fication. Such thresholds could inform early-warning systems: when nitrogen levels approach these critical values, the intelligent 
control system should proactively adjust aeration rates (the “Air Flow Rate” SHAP contributions support its modulating role) or check 
sludge retention time to prevent nitrification failure. Moreover, multi-parameter synergistic management is essential to maintain 
robust nitrification and avoid model overfitting to transient noise. These insights bridge data-driven predictions with actionable 
process strategies, promoting adaptive control in MBR operations.
