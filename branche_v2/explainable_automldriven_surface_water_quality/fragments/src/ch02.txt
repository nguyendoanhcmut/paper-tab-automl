Water quality monitoring and early warning systems play a crucial 
role in environmental protection and public health, particularly in 
developing countries facing rapid industrialization and urbanization 
(Michalak, 2016; Greenhill et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024). While the 
United Nations has emphasized water quality management in its Sus­
tainable Development Goals (SDGs 6 and 14), implementing effective 
monitoring systems remains challenging due to technical and resource 
constraints (Herrera, 2019; Byrne, 2024). Surface water quality classi­
fication based on monitoring indicators is fundamental to sustainable 
water environment management, yet achieving both cost-effectiveness 
and reliability in such classification systems remains a significant chal­
lenge in developing regions (Baccour et al., 2024).
Automatic water quality monitoring stations started to be deployed 
in major river basins in China since 1999, implementing classification 
based on the single-factor evaluation method (Table S1). This evaluation 
method requires monitoring of up to 24 water quality indicators. The list 
includes not only conventional parameters but also numerous indicators 
such as heavy metals (e.g., mercury, lead) and specific organic pollut­
ants (e.g., volatile phenols), which are notoriously difficult to monitor 
continuously in the field. This comprehensiveness poses significant 
practical challenges. Specifically, sensors for pollutants such as heavy 
metals and organics are expensive and less robust, leading to high

Traditional statistical approaches, such as principal component 
analysis and Pearson correlation analysis, have been widely used for 
water quality indicator selection (Tripathi and Singal, 2019; Uddin 
et al., 2021; Asadollah et al., 2021). With the advancement of artificial 
intelligence, machine learning (ML) methods have demonstrated supe­
rior capability in handling complex nonlinear relationships in water 
quality prediction and key indicator selection (Zhu et al., 2022; Ding 
et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). However, developing an optimal and 
robust predictive system using ML presents significant challenges. The 
process involves a complex, multi-stage workflow, including systemat­
ically selecting the most suitable algorithm, performing robust feature 
engineering, and conducting rigorous, computationally intensive 
hyperparameter optimization (HPO). While a single model might work 
efficiently with default parameters, the HPO process, which systemati­
cally evaluates hundreds or thousands of configurations to maximize 
predictive accuracy, is often computationally prohibitive, demanding 
substantial resources and specialized expertise (Peng et al., 2022; Xia 
and Zeng, 2022; Li et al., 2024). To address these specific workflow 
challenges, automated machine learning (AutoML) has emerged as a 
powerful paradigm. It is designed to automate this entire end-to-end 
pipeline, from data preprocessing and feature selection to model 
choice and HPO, thereby greatly reducing the demand for domain- 
specific expertise and computational oversight (Z¨oller and Huber, 
2021). Several AutoML methods have been developed, such as TPOT, 
H2O, and Auto-sklearn (Feurer et al., 2015; Olson et al., 2016; LeDell 
and Poirier, 2020). Among them, Auto-sklearn has demonstrated supe­
rior performance in multiple competitions through its integration of 
Bayesian optimization, meta-learning, and ensemble construction. Even 
in specially designed “Beat Auto-sklearn” challenges, manual parameter 
tuning by experienced data scientists could not surpass its automated 
optimization performance (Guyon et al., 2019).
However, the automated model selection and ensemble processes of 
AutoML intensify the “black box” nature of models, challenging the 
understanding and interpretation of model decision processes (Barredo 
Arrieta et al., 2020). This is particularly crucial in water quality man­
agement, where models must maintain both prediction accuracy and 
transparency in decision-making. The Kernel SHAP (SHapley Additive 
exPlanations) method offers a promising solution by combining LIME 
(Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) with Shapley values 
to construct a unified interpretation framework. Through quantitative 
feature importance attribution derived from cooperative game theory, 
SHAP effectively measures the contribution of features to model pre­
dictions (Lundberg and Lee, 2017a; Russo et al., 2023). While SHAP 
values show great potential, their application in water quality indicator 
screening remains unexplored, especially as an alternative to computa­
tionally prohibitive “best subset” selection methods. These brute-force 
approaches test every possible feature combination to guarantee 
finding the optimal set, but their computational cost grows exponen­
tially, making them impractical for larger feature sets (Chen et al., 
2020).
Herein, this study develops an automated and interpretable frame­
work for water quality classification using monitoring data from the 
China National Environmental Monitoring Center (CNEMC). The 
framework classifies surface water quality into a six-level system (Class 
I-V and worse than V) based on the nine core indicators reliably moni­
tored by the national online monitoring network of China. The specific 
objectives are to: (i) evaluate the performance of Auto-sklearn against 
conventional ML algorithms and modern ensemble methods through 
comprehensive metrics (Precision, Recall, Weighted F1, Macro F1, 
confusion matrix, and AUC-ROC curves); (ii) interpret the Auto-sklearn 
ensemble model using Kernel SHAP to quantify indicator contributions 
to model predictions; and (iii) identify key water quality indicators for 
optimizing monitoring schemes. Through this integrated approach, we 
aim to establish a cost-effective framework that can enhance water 
quality management and decision-making processes while simulta­
neously improving assessment reliability.
