To optimize performance of Auto-sklearn for water quality classifi­
cation, the impact of different training durations ranging from 0.5 to 12 
h was investigated while maintaining a constant data partitioning ratio 
of 0.8 (training dataset = 40,000, testing dataset = 10,000). Fig. 3
presents the trend of Weighted F1 and relative improvements across 
different runtime periods. The Weighted F1 was chosen as the primary 
evaluation metric because it provides a balanced assessment of model 
performance by combining both Precision and Recall, being particularly 
suitable for evaluating the overall effectiveness of the model (detailed 
results for Precision and Recall are provided in Table S5). The Weighted 
F1 exhibited a substantial and statistically significant improvement 
during the initial 4 h of training, increasing from 0.9537 ± 0.0002 (0.5 
h) to 0.9627 ± 0.0002 (4 h). A one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s 
post-hoc test confirmed that the performance at 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h 
were all significantly different from each other (p < 0.05). The high 
baseline performance reflects the strong, inherent relationship between 
the input indicators and the target classes, which are determined by 
these same indicators according to the national standard (Section 2.1).
With the increase in training time, a trend of diminishing returns was 
observed. Crucially, while the Weighted F1 increased to 0.9631 ±
0.0002 after 8 h, this improvement was not statistically significant 
compared to the 4-hour result (p > 0.05). The mean Weighted F1 in­
crease dropped from 0.0040 (1–2 h) to 0.0029 (2–4 h), and further

Through automated model selection and optimization, Auto-sklearn 
generated a final ensemble model consisting of 7 ML pipelines. The 
detailed configurations of this ensemble are now provided in the Sup­
plementary Materials (Table S6). In summary, the automated process 
consistently selected RF as the optimal classifier across all pipelines, 
applying various data preprocessing and distribution-based balancing 
strategies to optimize performance. This strong preference for RF clas­
sifiers suggests that tree-based ensemble methods with moderate 
complexity are particularly effective for this water quality classification 
task.
