A B S T R A C T
Surface water quality monitoring and classification face dual challenges: prohibitive costs of comprehensive 
indicators monitoring and the labor-intensive process of traditional machine learning (ML) implementation, 
which jointly constrain the development of efficient water quality assessment systems. This study presents an 
automated and interpretable framework: Auto-sklearn automates the machine learning workflow to address 
model selection complexity, while SHAP analysis quantifies feature contributions to reduce water quality indi­
cator requirements. Using monitoring data from major river basins of China, we evaluated Auto-sklearn against 
conventional ML algorithms and modern ensemble methods. Auto-sklearn achieved the highest mean perfor­
mance (Weighted F1: 0.9633 ± 0.0027) via an optimized ensemble. Crucially, this high performance proved to 
be robust and generalizable, as the framework maintained its superiority across rigorous temporal and spatial 
validation schemes. Through Kernel SHAP analysis, we identified CODMn, TP, and DO as the key indicators. A 3- 
indicator Auto-sklearn model achieved a high Weighted F1 (0.9205 ± 0.0097) and outperformed the rule-based 
baseline, with higher F1-scores across all classes and an 18.9 % relative improvement for Class WV water. This 
suggests that monitoring costs can be substantially reduced while simultaneously improving assessment reli­
ability. Further regional analysis revealed spatial variations, with most basins aligning with the nationwide set, 
but the Song-Liao and Yellow River Basins also requiring NH3-N monitoring. This framework provides a prac­
tical, cost-effective solution for water environment management, especially in developing regions.
1. Introduction
Water quality monitoring and early warning systems play a crucial 
role in environmental protection and public health, particularly in 
developing countries facing rapid industrialization and urbanization 
(Michalak, 2016; Greenhill et al., 2024; Qian et al., 2024). While the 
United Nations has emphasized water quality management in its Sus­
tainable Development Goals (SDGs 6 and 14), implementing effective 
monitoring systems remains challenging due to technical and resource 
constraints (Herrera, 2019; Byrne, 2024). Surface water quality classi­
fication based on monitoring indicators is fundamental to sustainable 
water environment management, yet achieving both cost-effectiveness 
and reliability in such classification systems remains a significant chal­
lenge in developing regions (Baccour et al., 2024).
Automatic water quality monitoring stations started to be deployed 
in major river basins in China since 1999, implementing classification 
based on the single-factor evaluation method (Table S1). This evaluation 
method requires monitoring of up to 24 water quality indicators. The list 
includes not only conventional parameters but also numerous indicators 
such as heavy metals (e.g., mercury, lead) and specific organic pollut­
ants (e.g., volatile phenols), which are notoriously difficult to monitor 
continuously in the field. This comprehensiveness poses significant 
practical challenges. Specifically, sensors for pollutants such as heavy 
metals and organics are expensive and less robust, leading to high

operational costs and frequent data interruptions (Durgun, 2024). As a 
direct consequence of these operational and financial constraints, the 
national online monitoring network of China pragmatically focuses on a 
core subset of more feasible indicators rather than the full 24 indicators. 
This reality highlights the urgent need to identify the most critical in­
dicators even within this reduced set to further enhance monitoring 
efficiency and cost-effectiveness (Othman et al., 2020; Wu et al., 2023).
Traditional statistical approaches, such as principal component 
analysis and Pearson correlation analysis, have been widely used for 
water quality indicator selection (Tripathi and Singal, 2019; Uddin 
et al., 2021; Asadollah et al., 2021). With the advancement of artificial 
intelligence, machine learning (ML) methods have demonstrated supe­
rior capability in handling complex nonlinear relationships in water 
quality prediction and key indicator selection (Zhu et al., 2022; Ding 
et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024). However, developing an optimal and 
robust predictive system using ML presents significant challenges. The 
process involves a complex, multi-stage workflow, including systemat­
ically selecting the most suitable algorithm, performing robust feature 
engineering, and conducting rigorous, computationally intensive 
hyperparameter optimization (HPO). While a single model might work 
efficiently with default parameters, the HPO process, which systemati­
cally evaluates hundreds or thousands of configurations to maximize 
predictive accuracy, is often computationally prohibitive, demanding 
substantial resources and specialized expertise (Peng et al., 2022; Xia 
and Zeng, 2022; Li et al., 2024). To address these specific workflow 
challenges, automated machine learning (AutoML) has emerged as a 
powerful paradigm. It is designed to automate this entire end-to-end 
pipeline, from data preprocessing and feature selection to model 
choice and HPO, thereby greatly reducing the demand for domain- 
specific expertise and computational oversight (Z¨oller and Huber, 
2021). Several AutoML methods have been developed, such as TPOT, 
H2O, and Auto-sklearn (Feurer et al., 2015; Olson et al., 2016; LeDell 
and Poirier, 2020). Among them, Auto-sklearn has demonstrated supe­
rior performance in multiple competitions through its integration of 
Bayesian optimization, meta-learning, and ensemble construction. Even 
in specially designed “Beat Auto-sklearn” challenges, manual parameter 
tuning by experienced data scientists could not surpass its automated 
optimization performance (Guyon et al., 2019).
However, the automated model selection and ensemble processes of 
AutoML intensify the “black box” nature of models, challenging the 
understanding and interpretation of model decision processes (Barredo 
Arrieta et al., 2020). This is particularly crucial in water quality man­
agement, where models must maintain both prediction accuracy and 
transparency in decision-making. The Kernel SHAP (SHapley Additive 
exPlanations) method offers a promising solution by combining LIME 
(Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) with Shapley values 
to construct a unified interpretation framework. Through quantitative 
feature importance attribution derived from cooperative game theory, 
SHAP effectively measures the contribution of features to model pre­
dictions (Lundberg and Lee, 2017a; Russo et al., 2023). While SHAP 
values show great potential, their application in water quality indicator 
screening remains unexplored, especially as an alternative to computa­
tionally prohibitive “best subset” selection methods. These brute-force 
approaches test every possible feature combination to guarantee 
finding the optimal set, but their computational cost grows exponen­
tially, making them impractical for larger feature sets (Chen et al., 
2020).
Herein, this study develops an automated and interpretable frame­
work for water quality classification using monitoring data from the 
China National Environmental Monitoring Center (CNEMC). The 
framework classifies surface water quality into a six-level system (Class 
I-V and worse than V) based on the nine core indicators reliably moni­
tored by the national online monitoring network of China. The specific 
objectives are to: (i) evaluate the performance of Auto-sklearn against 
conventional ML algorithms and modern ensemble methods through 
comprehensive metrics (Precision, Recall, Weighted F1, Macro F1, 
confusion matrix, and AUC-ROC curves); (ii) interpret the Auto-sklearn 
ensemble model using Kernel SHAP to quantify indicator contributions 
to model predictions; and (iii) identify key water quality indicators for 
optimizing monitoring schemes. Through this integrated approach, we 
aim to establish a cost-effective framework that can enhance water 
quality management and decision-making processes while simulta­
neously improving assessment reliability.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Study area and water quality dataset
Datasets of surface water quality were collected from the CNEMC, 
covering nine major river basins, including Continental, Hai, Huai, 
Pearl, Song-Liao, Southeast, Southwest, Yangtze, and Yellow rivers. The 
data were collected from approximately 1900 monitoring sites from 
January 2021 to June 2024, with a time step of 4 h (Fig. 1a). The na­
tional online monitoring system of CNEMC focuses on a core set of 11 
feasible indicators for real-time tracking. However, after assessing data 
quality, two of these indicators [chlorophyll-a (Chl-a, mg/L) and algal 
density (cells/L)] were excluded from our study due to severe data 
missingness (>70 %, Table S3). The final analysis was therefore con­
ducted on the remaining nine robustly-monitored indicators: water 
temperature (WT, ◦C), pH, dissolved oxygen (DO, mg/L), electrical 
conductivity (EC, μS/cm), chemical oxygen demand (CODMn, mg/L), 
ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N, mg/L), total phosphorus (TP, mg/L), total 
nitrogen (TN, mg/L), and turbidity (NTU).
According to the Environmental Quality Standards for Surface Water 
(MEEPRC, 2002), water quality was classified into six classes from best 
to worst (I-WV). This classification serves as the target variable for our 
supervised learning models. The classification for each data record was 
pre-determined by the CNEMC based on the “single-factor evaluation 
method”, where the final class is assigned based on the poorest- 
performing individual indicator relative to the standard limits as 
shown in Table S1. Our objective is therefore to predict this official 
classification using the nine selected water quality indicators as input 
features.
To ensure computational feasibility for the extensive automated 
model search and subsequent SHAP analysis, a subset of 50,000 samples 
was extracted from the cleaned dataset (n = 10,159,876) using stratified 
random sampling. This sampling approach was chosen to prioritize 
model stability, as our analysis showed that training on the full dataset 
produced unstable, high-variance results and required computationally 
prohibitive runtimes (Fig. S1). To rigorously evaluate the stability and 
robustness of our findings against sampling variability, this entire 
sampling process was repeated five times using different random seeds 
(42, 123, 777, 1024, and 2048).
This sampling methodology not only preserved the proportional 
distribution of the water quality classes (Fig. 1b) but also maintained a 
broadly consistent distribution across the major river basins. As detailed 
in Table S2, the proportional representation of each basin in the sampled 
subsets shows strong alignment with the full dataset, exhibiting only 
minor deviations, thereby confirming that the sampling process did not 
introduce significant regional bias. For the regional analysis, the same 
stratified sampling approach was applied to each major basin separately. 
Spearman correlation analysis was performed on the stratified dataset, 
and indicated no significant relationships among the selected water 
quality indicators (Fig. 1c), thus eliminating the need for removal of 
multicollinearity features.

2.3. Traditional and automated machine learning
Traditional ML pipelines require substantial human intervention 
across multiple stages, including data preprocessing, feature engineer­
ing, model selection, and hyperparameter tuning (Fig. 2a). For tradi­
tional ML models, each input feature was standardized using Z-score 
normalization (μ = 0, σ = 1) to ensure all variables had comparable 
scales, as illustrated in Fig. S2. And Gaussian process-based Bayesian 
optimization was employed for hyperparameter tuning in traditional ML 
approaches (Snoek et al., 2012).
In contrast, for AutoML implementation, raw data was used without 
any preprocessing, allowing the algorithm to automatically determine 
the optimal preprocessing methods (Fig. 2b). The Auto-sklearn (version 
0.15.0) framework combines Bayesian optimization, meta-learning, and 
ensemble construction for AutoML pipeline selection (Feurer et al., 
2015). This framework incorporates comprehensive ML tools, including 
classification algorithms, feature preprocessing methods, and data pre­
processing techniques. No manual adjustments were made to the model 
selection process, with Auto-sklearn automatically defining the search 
space for hyperparameter optimization. For the accurate assessment of 
model performance, due to the highly imbalanced distribution of water 
quality classes in the dataset, both traditional ML and AutoML utilized 5- 
fold stratified cross-validation during model training (Fig. S3).
For comprehensive evaluation, Auto-sklearn was compared with 
both conventional ML models implemented through scikit-learn [Lo­
gistic Regression (LR), k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Decision Tree (DT), 
Random Forest (RF)] and state-of-the-art ensemble learning models 
[CatBoost (CatB), LightGBM (LGBM), XGBoost (XGB)]. All imple­
mentations were conducted using Python 3.8.13. Detailed information 
about traditional ML methods is provided in the Supplementary Mate­
rials (Text S1).
2.4. Model evaluation metrics
The predictive performance of the models was assessed through a 
comprehensive suite of multi-dimensional evaluation metrics. These 
metrics, including their mathematical formulas and detailed statistical 
interpretations, are summarized in Table S4. For this multi-class clas­
sification task, we employed key metrics such as Precision, Recall, and 
the Weighted F1, which effectively accounts for class imbalance. Addi­
tionally, confusion matrices were used for a visual breakdown of clas­
sification performance across all classes, while Receiver Operating 
Characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding Area Under the 
Curve (AUC) values were used to evaluate the overall discriminative 
ability of the models.
2.5. Explanation of Auto-sklearn
To interpret the ensemble model generated by Auto-sklearn, we 
employed the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, which 
calculates feature importance through game theory principles (Kuhn, 
2020). In ML context, Shapley values measure the individual impact of 
each feature in the model, collectively contributing to the prediction. 
Various versions of SHAP have been developed by Lundberg and Lee, 
including DeepSHAP, Kernel SHAP, LinearSHAP, and TreeSHAP, each 
designed to approximate SHAP values for different types of ML models. 
A critical prerequisite for a meaningful SHAP analysis is the high pre­
dictive accuracy of the model being explained. The SHAP interpretation 
was therefore performed only after the high predictive performance of 
the Auto-sklearn ensemble had been established.
Auto-sklearn can produce heterogeneous ensembles of different al­
gorithm types. Therefore, the model-agnostic Kernel SHAP was selected 
for interpretation, as it is well-suited for such ensembles and requires no 
model-specific adjustments. It approximates SHAP values through a 
weighted local regression that bridges Model-agnostic Explanations 
(LIME) methods with Shapley values from cooperative game theory 
(Lundberg and Lee, 2017b). The SHAP value ∅i for feature i is calculated

3. Results
3.1. Runtime optimization and ensemble configuration of Auto-sklearn 
for water quality classification
To optimize performance of Auto-sklearn for water quality classifi­
cation, the impact of different training durations ranging from 0.5 to 12 
h was investigated while maintaining a constant data partitioning ratio 
of 0.8 (training dataset = 40,000, testing dataset = 10,000). Fig. 3
presents the trend of Weighted F1 and relative improvements across 
different runtime periods. The Weighted F1 was chosen as the primary 
evaluation metric because it provides a balanced assessment of model 
performance by combining both Precision and Recall, being particularly 
suitable for evaluating the overall effectiveness of the model (detailed 
results for Precision and Recall are provided in Table S5). The Weighted 
F1 exhibited a substantial and statistically significant improvement 
during the initial 4 h of training, increasing from 0.9537 ± 0.0002 (0.5 
h) to 0.9627 ± 0.0002 (4 h). A one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s 
post-hoc test confirmed that the performance at 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h 
were all significantly different from each other (p < 0.05). The high 
baseline performance reflects the strong, inherent relationship between 
the input indicators and the target classes, which are determined by 
these same indicators according to the national standard (Section 2.1).
With the increase in training time, a trend of diminishing returns was 
observed. Crucially, while the Weighted F1 increased to 0.9631 ±
0.0002 after 8 h, this improvement was not statistically significant 
compared to the 4-hour result (p > 0.05). The mean Weighted F1 in­
crease dropped from 0.0040 (1–2 h) to 0.0029 (2–4 h), and further

3.2. Water quality classification performance and generalization
3.2.1. Comparative performance of models
The performance of Auto-sklearn was comprehensively evaluated 
against seven machine learning models. Based on five independent runs 
using a stratified random split, a Tukey’s HSD statistical test (p < 0.05) 
grouped the models into distinct performance tiers. As shown in Table 1, 
Auto-sklearn, RF, and CatB constituted the top tier, with no statistically 
significant performance difference among them. Within this top group, 
however, Auto-sklearn consistently achieved the highest mean scores 
across all evaluation metrics, with a precision of 0.9636 ± 0.0027, recall 
of 0.9635 ± 0.0027, and a weighted F1-score of 0.9633 ± 0.0027. A 
detailed, per-class analysis presented in Table S7 further underscores 
this leading performance, showing that Auto-sklearn also attained the 
highest Macro F1-score. The remaining models were grouped into sub­
sequent performance tiers, with KNN and LR performing significantly 
poorer than all other methods.
The classification performance is further detailed in the confusion 
matrices (Fig. 4). Among the evaluated models, Auto-sklearn (Fig. 4a) 
emerged as the top-performing solution, demonstrating exceptional and 
well-balanced accuracy across all water quality classes. It achieved near- 
perfect scores for Class I (99.1 %) and Class II (98.4 %) and maintained 
high accuracy on the ambiguous intermediate classes: 96.1 % for Class 
III, 91.0 % for Class IV, and 84.4 % for Class V. This capability starkly 
contrasts with traditional models like LR (Fig. 4d), whose accuracy 
collapsed to just 53.3 % for Class III, 30.8 % for Class IV, and even 0 % 
for Class V, highlighting the severe limitations of linear models. The 
confusion matrices for other models are presented in Fig. S4.
The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and corre­
sponding Area Under the Curve (AUC) values in Fig. 5 further validate 
the models’ discriminative ability. Auto-sklearn (Fig. 5a) achieved the 
highest macro-average AUC (MA-AUC) of 0.9963, followed closely by 
other top-tier models like RF (MA-AUC = 0.9961) and CatB (MA-AUC =
0.9960). In contrast, LR (Fig. 5d) was substantially weaker with a MA- 
AUC of 0.9027, showing particular difficulty with Class III (AUC =
0.8257) and Class IV (AUC = 0.8762). Additional ROC analyses for the 
remaining models are available in Fig. S5.
This comprehensive comparison underscores the ability of the Auto- 
sklearn framework to automatically construct a model that achieves 
superior water quality classification performance.
3.2.2. Generalization assessment with temporal and spatial validation
To more rigorously assess model robustness and address potential 
data leakage from simple random splits, two additional validation 
schemes were implemented: a temporal hold-out (training on data 
before Jan 1, 2024, and testing on data after) and a spatial hold-out 
(leave-one-basin-out cross-validation). The results are summarized in 
Table 2.
The temporal validation results demonstrate the model’s robust 
classification performance, which remains stable across different time 
periods. The performance of models remained remarkably stable, with 
no statistically significant drop in the Weighted F1-score when pre­
dicting on future, unseen data. This indicates that the models effectively 
learned underlying patterns that are consistent over time.
The spatial validation provided critical insights into the geographic 
generalizability of models. While the average performance across the 
basin-level cross-validation remained high, the standard deviation 
increased significantly—for Auto-sklearn, from 0.0027 in the random 
split to 0.0154. This high variance is strong quantitative evidence of 
underlying regional heterogeneity, indicating that the model’s perfor­
mance, while strong overall, varies across basins with distinct charac­
teristics (Table S8). Crucially, despite this performance variability, the 
relative ranking of the models remained consistent. This stability in 
relative performance confirms that the Auto-sklearn framework is a 
robust and reliable choice for nationwide water quality classification.

was established (Fig. 7a). This was achieved by first calculating the 
mean absolute SHAP values for each indicator within each of the five

sum to one. The final importance scores shown are the average of these

was performed on these final scores to assess statistical significance (p <

CODMn and TP as the most influential indicators; their importances were 
not statistically different from each other but were significantly greater 
than all other indicators. DO ranked third, representing the next distinct 
level of statistical importance. A detailed visualization of the cumulative 
importance for each indicator, broken down by class, is provided in the 
supplementary materials (Fig. S6).

3.4. Regional variations in key water quality indicators across major 
basins
Building upon the identification of CODMn, TP, and DO as the key 
water quality indicators at the national scale, the SHAP analysis was 
extended to evaluate regional variations. To ensure the robustness of 
these regional findings, the analysis for each basin was performed using 
five independent model runs with different random seeds. The final 
importance scores, representing the mean of the five normalized SHAP 
values, are visualized in Fig. 8. The detailed results, including the mean 
and SD for each indicator in every basin, are available in the supple­
mentary material (Table S9).
The results revealed a high degree of consistency across most of 
China. Specifically, seven of the nine major river basins—the 
Continental, Hai, Huai, Pearl, Southeast, Southwest, and Yangtze River 
Basins—retained CODMn, TP, and DO as their three most critical in­
dicators, mirroring the national-level analysis. This widespread agree­
ment across diverse geographical regions further validates the 
fundamental significance of these three indicators in water quality 
assessment.
However, notable regional variations emerged in the two northern 
basins. In both the Song-Liao and the Yellow River Basins, NH3-N 
replaced DO as one of the top three key indicators (Table S9). These 
findings highlight that while CODMn, TP, and DO form an effective na­
tional minimum indicator set, regional hydro-environmental charac­
teristics may necessitate tailored monitoring approaches.

4.2. Rationality and practicality of key water quality indicators screening
4.2.1. A cost-effective nationwide indicator set
Water quality classification assessment traditionally relies on a 
comprehensive set of indicators, with national standards such as 
GB3838-2002 requiring monitoring of 24 fundamental indicators 
(MEEPRC, 2002). While this extensive monitoring approach ensures 
accuracy, it poses significant challenges in terms of cost and time effi­
ciency (Sun et al., 2021). Our SHAP-based analysis identified CODMn, 
TP, and DO as the most influential indicators for water quality classifi­
cation in China (Fig. 7a), offering a potential solution to this practical 
challenge.
The statistical effectiveness of this three-indicator set is robust. Our 
SHAP-based sequential analysis showed that these indicators alone 
could achieve a high Weighted F1 of 0.9205 ± 0.0097, capturing the 
majority of information necessary for accurate classification. To validate 
this further, we compared the Auto-sklearn trained on these three in­
dicators against a programmatic baseline applying the standard single- 
factor rule to the same limited set. As detailed in Table 3, the Auto- 
sklearn model achieved a statistically significantly higher Weighted 
F1-score (0.9205 ± 0.0097) compared to the rule-based baseline 
(0.8976 ± 0.0038). More importantly, the Auto-sklearn model demon­
strated superior performance across all classes, most critically for Class 
WV, where it achieved an F1-score of 0.8285—a substantial 18.9 % 
relative improvement over the baseline’s 0.6968. This superiority stems 
from the Auto-sklearn’s ability to capture complex, non-linear in­
teractions that the rigid rule inherently misses, confirming that the 
combination of our key indicators with an intelligent model provides a 
more accurate and practically valuable assessment, especially for man­
aging the most polluted water bodies.
The identification of these key indicators provides a practical solu­
tion to the prohibitive costs associated with comprehensive water 
quality monitoring. By focusing on just three indicators (CODMn, TP, and 
DO), monitoring programs can achieve reliable assessment while 
reducing both capital investment for equipment and operational ex­
penses for chemical analysis. This simplified approach is particularly 
valuable for developing regions with resource constraints, making sys­
tematic water quality monitoring more economically feasible and 
widely implementable.
4.2.2. Regional heterogeneity
While our reduced indicator set (CODMn, TP, and DO) performs well 
for general classification at the national scale, our regional analysis 
critically demonstrates that optimal indicator combinations vary in 
response to distinct, regional-scale anthropogenic pressures. As shown 
in Fig. 8 and Table S9, the Song-Liao and Yellow River Basins required a 
different key indicator set where NH3-N replaced DO. This shift is a 
direct reflection of the region-specific pollution drivers.
The exceptional severity of nitrogen pollution in these basins is 
quantitatively supported. The Song-Liao River Basin, for instance, has a 
Net Anthropogenic Nitrogen Input estimated at a staggering 9453 kg N 
km–2 yr–1, a value noted to be higher than that of southern basins (Cao 
et al., 2025). Similarly, the Yellow River Basin is the most severe region 
for agricultural non-point source pollution in China, with its provinces 
contributing a disproportionately high 42.67 % of the entire nation’s 
agricultural TN emissions (Tan et al., 2025). This extreme loading means 
that raw pollutants, rich in NH3-N, are the dominant feature of the 
pollution profile.
This immense pollution pressure is critically amplified by the re­
gion’s distinct hydrological conditions, which starkly contrast with 
other major basins. The northern basins are characterized by extremely 
low water availability. The annual average runoff of the Liaohe (36.7 
km3) and Yellow (52.1 km3) rivers is an order of magnitude lower than 
that of the southern Yangtze and Pearl rivers, which both exceed 300 
km3 (Gao et al., 2020). The Yellow River exemplifies this stress, irri­
gating 13 % of the nation’s cropland with only 3 % of its total river 
runoff (Ren et al., 2016). This “high load, low flow” dynamic means that 
the immense nitrogen inputs are concentrated in a minimal volume of 
water, leading to exceptionally high pollutant concentrations that are 
not seen in high-flow southern basins.
This comparative analysis confirms that the nitrogen pollution in the 
Song-Liao and Yellow River Basins is uniquely prominent. While 
southern basins like the Yangtze may have large total nitrogen loads, 
their vast dilution capacity mitigates the concentration and, thus, the 
immediate severity (Wang et al., 2020). In contrast, the combination of 
extreme nitrogen input intensity and severely limited dilution capacity 
in the Song-Liao and Yellow River Basins creates an environment where 
primary pollutants like NH3-N are a more direct and sensitive signal of 
degradation than secondary ecosystem responses like DO. This evidence 
validates the findings of our model and underscores the necessity of 
basin-specific indicator sets for effective water quality management.

4.3. Limitations and future perspectives
Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged from both 
technical and methodological perspectives. Firstly, the indicator opti­
mization in this study was confined to only nine commonly monitored 
indicators, which represents a subset of the comprehensive indicator 
system required by Chinese national standards (Table S1). Many 
important indicators specified in the standards, such as heavy metals, 
volatile phenols, and petroleum, were not included in our analysis. 
Moreover, biological indicators like Chlorophyll-a and algal density had 
to be excluded due to significant missing values in the monitoring data. 
These data availability constraints mean that our key indicator 
screening results might not fully reflect all aspects of water quality 
assessment required by national standards, particularly in terms of 
biological water quality and specific pollutants. To address this limita­
tion, future studies should expand the scope of indicator analysis to 
include all indicators specified in national standards, which would 
provide a more comprehensive basis for key indicator selection.
Secondly, while our current AutoML framework successfully reduces 
the dimensionality of water quality indicators, it can only perform 
classification when all key indicators (CODMn, TP, and DO) are avail­
able. The model becomes inoperable when any of these key indicators 
are missing due to monitoring equipment malfunction or data trans­
mission issues, which is a common scenario in environmental moni­
toring. To address this temporal missing data challenge, future research 
should incorporate time series models (e.g., RNNs, Transformers) to first 
predict missing key water quality indicators using their historical mea­
surements, and then feed these predictions into the AutoML classifica­
tion framework. This two-stage approach would enable continuous 
water quality classification even during periods of missing measure­
ments, further enhancing the practical applicability of the system.
Despite these limitations, the framework presented in this study of­
fers significant practical implications. Our work provides a data-driven 
pathway to design more cost-effective monitoring networks, first by 
identifying a generalizable set of core indicators (CODMn, TP, and DO) at 
the national scale. Crucially, the framework also demonstrates its ability 
to adapt to local conditions by identifying different key indicators (e.g., 
NH3-N) in basins with unique pollution pressures. This adaptability is 
particularly valuable for regions with limited financial or technical re­
sources. Moreover, the integration of an AutoML framework automates 
the otherwise complex and labor-intensive process of model selection 
and hyperparameter tuning, lowering the technical barrier for envi­
ronmental agencies to implement predictive analytics. Ultimately, this 
study provides a scalable and adaptable template for creating more 
efficient and intelligent water quality management systems, enabling a 
more strategic allocation of resources in environmental protection.
5. Conclusion
This study demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating Auto-sklearn 
with interpretable ML techniques for water quality monitoring and 
classification optimization. Auto-sklearn outperformed most traditional 
machine learning approaches in water quality classification, achieving 
the top-tier performance with a Weighted F1 of 0.9633 ± 0.0027. 
Crucially, the framework exhibited robust and generalizable perfor­
mance, maintaining its superiority across rigorous temporal and spatial 
validation schemes. Furthermore, SHAP analysis revealed CODMn, TP, 
and DO as the most influential indicators. Crucially, a model using only 
these three indicators achieved a high Weighted F1-score (0.9205 ±
0.0097) and outperformed the rule-based baseline, yielding higher F1- 
scores across all classes and a 18.9 % relative improvement for Class 
WV water, offering potential for streamlined monitoring strategies. 
Regional analysis demonstrated that while most river basins aligned 
with the nationwide key indicator set, spatial heterogeneity exists in 
Song-Liao and Yellow River Basin where NH3-N emerged as a key 
indicator.
The proposed framework addresses two critical challenges in water 
quality assessment: it eliminates the labor-intensive process of tradi­
tional ML implementation through automated workflow of Auto- 
sklearn, while SHAP-based indicator screening substantially reduces 
monitoring costs by identifying key indicators. This integration provides 
a practical solution for water environment management, particularly 
valuable for developing regions where both technical expertise and 
monitoring resources are limited.
