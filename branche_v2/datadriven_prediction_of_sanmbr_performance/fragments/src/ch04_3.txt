3.3.1  Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs)
PDPs are used to visualize the interaction between individ­
ual input features and their respective effect on the machine 
learning model’s output prediction, assuming all other fea­
tures remain constant. These plots illustrate the impact of 
changing a single feature on the prediction of the dependent 
variable, thereby making the results of the advanced SVR 
model more interpretable and understandable. Fig. 11 shows 
the PDPs for six key numerical input parameters employed 
in the current study.
The PDP for influent 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP) con­
centration reveals a non-linear response pattern. An initial 
increase in partial dependence values is observed within the 
lower concentration range (0–150 mg/L), indicating that the 
model predicts relatively stable or slightly improved COD 
removal performance in this interval. This behaviour sug­
gests that, within this range, the system is not immediately 
limited by toxicity effects. However, beyond 150 mg/L, the 
trend gradually plateaus and begins to decline, reflecting the 
onset of inhibitory effects on microbial activity. This tran­
sition highlights the balance between substrate availability 
and toxicity, where increasing pollutant concentration ini­
tially does not suppress system performance but eventually 
leads to inhibition, particularly affecting methanogenic pro­
cesses (Li et al. 2024).
For influent pH, the PDP showed a clear non-linear rela­
tionship, with the highest predicted COD removal efficiency 
observed in the pH range of approximately 8–9. Outside 
this optimal window, particularly under acidic (pH < 7) or 
strongly alkaline (pH > 9) conditions, the predicted perfor­
mance declines. This trend is consistent with the known sen­
sitivity of anaerobic microbial communities to pH, where 
deviations from the optimal range negatively affect enzy­
matic activity and metabolic stability (Meegoda et al. 2018).
Comparatively, influent COD (mg/L) and OLR (gCOD/
m³·day) exhibit relatively minor influence on the predicted 
COD removal efficiency within the studied ranges, indi­
cating that the system operated below its critical loading 
threshold. Similarly, influent turbidity (NTU) and TSS (g/L) 
show a slight negative trend, where increasing values corre­
spond to marginal decreases in predicted efficiency. This is 
consistent with the rate-limiting nature of particulate hydro­
lysis in anaerobic systems, as particulate matter requires 
additional processing steps compared to soluble substrates 
(Wang et al. 2022).
3.3.2  Sensitivity Analysis
This section quantifies the influence of individual input 
features on the optimal SVR model’s prediction of COD 
removal using univariate sensitivity analysis framework. 
Unlike model-specific explainability methods such as 
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which rely on 
internal model structures or game-theoretic formulations, 
the present approach is a model-agnostic sensitivity analy­
sis designed to evaluate the response of the trained model to 
controlled variations in input variables. This approach pro­
vides a direct and computationally efficient assessment of 
global feature influence, suitable for identifying dominant

process variables. In this approach, each input feature was 
varied independently across its observed range using a 
systematic discretization procedure, while all other input 
variables were held constant at their baseline values. Specif­

uniformly spaced points within its range was generated, and 
the trained SVR model was used to predict COD removal 
for each modified input set. The sensitivity index was then 
calculated as the mean absolute deviation between the pre­
dicted outputs obtained from the perturbed inputs and the 
baseline predictions. This metric quantifies the extent to 
which variations in each input feature influence the model 
output. Higher sensitivity values indicate stronger influence, 
while values approaching zero suggest minimal impact. The 
sensitivity analysis plot (Fig. 12) shows the relative impor­
tance of each input variable, with 2,4-DCP concentration 
and pH emerging as the main factors influencing predicted 
COD removal.
The analysis clearly establishes the crucial role of specific 
input parameters. Influent 2,4-dichlorophenol (mg/L) exhib­
its the highest sensitivity, confirming its status as the most 
influential feature in the SVR model. This finding is highly 
consistent with the partial dependence plot (PDP) results, 
which showed a strong negative impact on COD removal 
(a predicted decline from a stable 90% to below 70%) as 
its concentration increased. The high sensitivity to 2,4-DCP 
highlights the need to control inhibitory compounds, as this 
response is consistent with the known toxicity of chlorophe­
nols toward methanogenic archaea in anaerobic systems.
Influent pH is the second most influential parameter, 
which accurately reflects the SAnMBR system’s biologi­
cal nature and its dependence on a narrow optimal range. 
The PDPs demonstrate that removal efficiency is maxi­
mized within an optimal pH range of approximately 8–9, 
beyond which system performance progressively declines 
due to reduced microbial activity under non-optimal condi­
tions. Furthermore, parameters related to bulk organic load, 
such as influent COD and OLR, displayed lower sensitivity. 
Their limited influence is consistent with the PDPs showing 
largely flat impact curves, suggesting that the SAnMBR sys­
tem was highly robust and operated below its peak loading 
capacity throughout the dataset range. Influent turbidity and 
influent TSS had the lowest sensitivity scores, indicating 
that changes in these variables had only a minor effect on 
the SVR-predicted COD removal. This may be because par­
ticulate matter affects anaerobic treatment mainly through 
hydrolysis, which is typically slower than the degradation of 
soluble organic compounds. As a result, marginal changes 
in TSS or turbidity were less influential than soluble inhibi­
tory factors such as 2,4-DCP.
