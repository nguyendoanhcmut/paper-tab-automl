3  Results and Discussion
3.1  SAnMBR Experimental Performance
The 189-day time series (Fig. 4) shows that COD removal 
rose during an initial acclimation period (Days 1–50) 
from ~ 43% to roughly 85–90% and then remained mostly 
between 80% and 95% despite large, staged increases in 
OLR (from ~ 125 to ~ 800 g COD m⁻³·d⁻¹). Several non-
exclusive mechanisms can explain thisbehaviour.
A core advantage lies in membrane retention, which 
effectively decouples the Solids Retention Time (SRT) from 
hydraulic retention time (HRT). This separation enables 
the accumulation of slow-growing biomass, particularly 
methanogens, allowing the system to sustain efficient con­
version of soluble organic matter (COD) into methane. As 
a result, stable COD removal is maintained under varying 
loading conditions, and the impact of transient hydraulic 
and organic stress is significantly reduced. This critical SRT 
effect has been repeatedly observed as a primary advantage 
of SAnMBR configurations (Robles et al. 2018). Further­
more, the maintained mesophilic temperature (36 ± 1  °C) 
and high mixed liquor concentrations typical of long-term 
MBR systems enhance COD degradation kinetics by stabi­
lizing microbial activity and promoting enzyme-mediated 
reactions involved in hydrolysis and methanogenesis. Under 
mesophilic conditions, substrate utilization rates are more 
consistent and less sensitive to fluctuations, which supports 
efficient conversion of soluble organic matter and reduces 
the risk of kinetic limitations at elevated OLRs (Khan et 
al. 2024). As a result, higher apparent degradation rates 
and improved process stability are achieved under variable 
loading conditions. System-level control strategies are also 
key contributors to resilience. This includes controlled feed 
step-changes and proactive fouling management (such as 
gas sparging, regular backwash, and chemical cleaning), 
all of which limit the likelihood that transient high loads 
or membrane fouling translate into long-term performance 
loss.
Additionally, the observed stability is partially attribut­
able to microbial community adaptation. Under prolonged 
exposure to persistent substrates and phenolic compounds, a 
selective pressure is applied, which can increase the relative 
abundance of degraders and methanogens adapted to these 
recalcitrant substrates. Representative genera reported in 
similar anaerobic systems include syntrophic bacteria such 
as Syntrophus and Syntrophorhabdus, which are involved in 
the initial breakdown of aromatic compounds, and metha­
nogenic archaea such as Methanosaeta and Methanosar­
cina, responsible for the final conversion of intermediates to 
methane (Muñoz Sierra et al. 2020). These microbial func­
tions are consistent with those reported in comparable phe­
nolic wastewater treatment systems. Where such favourable 
adaptation occurs, reactors show improved stability, which 
is highly consistent with the high removal rates seen in 
this campaign. However, it is important to note that perfor­
mance can significantly decline past reactor-specific OLR 
thresholds.
Figure 5 illustrates the variation in COD, 2,4-DCP, 
turbidity, and TSS removal efficiencies dur-ing stepwise 
increases in the organic loading rate (OLR). 
The COD removal rate remained high throughout the 
experiment (85–95%), with short-term decreases of 5–8% 
following each increase in OLR, followed by rapid recov­
ery. There was a transient accumulation of soluble micro­
bial products (SMP) and extracellular polymeric substances 
(EPS) during stress conditions, which can cause these tem­
porary drops. The EPS produced and released by microor­
ganisms, together with SMP, can accumulate on membrane 
surfaces, causing fouling and limiting substrate diffusion. 
Comparable EPS and SMP accumulation has also been 
observed in AnMBRs operated under fluctuating organic 
loads and temperatures, where it was linked to reduced bio­
degradation performance(AbuKhadra et al. 2024; Medina et 
al. 2020).
Turbidity removal improved progressively from 60–65% 
in the early phases to approximately 75–80% in later stages, 
reflecting the gradual maturation of biomass and enhanced 
floc formation that improves colloid capture. TSS removal 
remained consistently high (> 90–95%) across all phases,

The removal of 2,4-DCP exhibited a more dynamic trend 
than bulk COD. During the initial period, removal efficiency 
was relatively low (< 20–30%), but after microbial acclima­
tion it increased steadily and stabilized at 80–95%. Minor 
reductions were observed after OLR increases, which were 
followed by rapid recovery. This lag compared to COD 
removal is typical for chlorinated phenolic compounds, 
where dechlorination and aromatic ring-cleavage steps 
are kinetically limiting and highly sensitive to substrate or 
toxicity shocks until dehalogenating microbial populations 
become fully established (Geng et al. 2020). Under longer 
hydraulic retention times (HRTs) and steady operating con­
ditions, AnMBRs have been shown to achieve parallel high 
removals of COD and chlorophenols, as shown by Zhu et 
al. (2020), who reported average COD and 2-chlorophenol 
removals of approximately 93% and 94%, respectively, at 
HRTs of 48–96 h for an influent 2-chlorophenol concentra­
tion of 5–200 mg/L (Zhu et al. 2020).
When compared with recent literature on SAnMBR 
and AnMBR systems, the steady-state removal efficien­
cies observed in this study fall within the upper range of 
reported values, and the transient fluctuations are consistent 
with expected operational dynamics. A 189-day SAnMBR 
experiment treating 2,4-DCP wastewater achieved almost 
complete removal at stable load (COD ≈ 99.6%, 2,4-
DCP ≈ 99.6%), confirming that once microbial communities 
are acclimated, phenolic degradation can reach efficiencies 
comparable to bulk organics. Other hybrid and submerged 
anaerobic membrane configurations have similarly demon­
strated strong solids capture and high COD removal, with 
transient performance variations primarily governed by 
OLR step changes, HRT, and fouling management strate­
gies (Wang et al. 2015). The slight differences between the 
present results and the plateau values reported in the litera­
ture may reflect differences in OLR ramping strategy, accli­
mation duration, phenolic compound type (2,4-DCP versus 
2-CP), and fouling behaviour driven by SMP and EPS 
accumulation(Chen et al. 2017).
The two-dimensional partial dependence analysis was 
performed to investigate the complex bivariate interaction 
between influent pH and 2,4-DCP concentration, which 
were identified as the most influential parameters based on 
the sensitivity analysis results (Fig. 6), where they exhibited 
the highest relative impact compared to other operational 
variables. This analysis extends the interpretation beyond 
single-factor effects by examining how interacting opera­
tional variables influence SAnMBR performance. As shown 
in Fig.  6, the highest predicted COD removal efficiency 
(92–95%) is achieved only under the strict combination of 
low 2,4-DCP concentrations (< 50 mg/L) and the optimal 
pH range (pH 8–9). This confirms the primary role of pH in 
maximizing microbial enzyme activity (Zhang et al. 2023).

Crucially, the plot reveals a non-linear, compounding 
inhibitory effect outside this optimal zone. The steepest 
decline in COD removal is observedalong the increasing 
2,4-DCP concentration axis. While performance remains 
relatively high when 2,4-DCP is low (regardless of minor 
pH changes), performance rapidly deteriorates as 2,4-DCP 
concentration increases above 150 mg/L. This deterioration 
is exacerbated outside the optimal pH zone. This behaviour 
is likely due to two factors: (1) Low pH (acidic conditions) 
increases the proportion of 2,4-DCP present in its undissoci­
ated, non-ionized form, which readily penetrates microbial 
cell membranes, dramatically increasing its toxicity; and 
(2) pH values outside the methanogenic ideal already stress 
the system, making the biomass less resilient to additional 
chemical inhibition (Fernandez et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 
2023). This finding has direct operational significance for 
the SAnMBR system, as it demonstrates that optimization 
is not merely about minimizing 2,4-DCP but is a dynamic 
trade-off. Maintaining rigorous pH control provides a criti­
cal buffering mechanism against moderate toxic spikes.
