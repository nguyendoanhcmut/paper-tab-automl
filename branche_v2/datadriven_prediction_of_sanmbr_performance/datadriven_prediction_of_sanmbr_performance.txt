Abstract
Treating phenolic wastewater remains a major challenge due to the toxicity and inhibitory effects of compounds such as 
2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP), which destabilize biological processes. This study investigates the performance of a pilot-
scale submerged anaerobic membrane bioreactor(SAnMBR) treating 2,4-DCP-contaminated wastewater over 189 days 
and develops a data-driven machine learning framework to predict COD removal efficiency. Based on 189 experimental 
observations and six key operational parameters, three algorithms, namely multiple linear regression, artificial neural 
networks, and support vector regression were trained. SVR outperformed the other models, achieving a test R² of 0.952, 
an RMSE of 2.96, and strong generalization performance. The results indicate that optimal COD removal occurred within 
a narrow pH range (8-9) and at low 2,4-DCP loads (< 50 mg/L), with steep performance declines observed under inhibi­
tory concentrations (> 150 mg/L). In addition to COD, SAnMBRs also achieved high removal efficiencies for turbidity 
(> 75%), TSS (> 90%), and 2,4-DCP (> 80%). These findings demonstrate that integrating long-term experimental data 
with interpretable machine learning enables reliable performance prediction and provides actionable insights for optimiz­
ing the SAnMBR operation in treating toxic industrial wastewater.

1  Introduction
Phenolic compounds, including phenol and its derivatives, 
represent a class of priority pollutants often found in indus­
trial effluents (e.g., petrochemical, pharmaceutical, dye, and 
pulp and paper industries). These compounds are character­
ized by aromatic ring structure bearing one or more hydroxyl 
groups, which contribute to their chemical stability and solu­
bility, allowing them to persist and disperse in aquatic envi­
ronment (Catherine et al. 2016). Their persistence, coupled 
with their bioaccumulative potential, raises severe environ­
mental and public health concerns. Ecotoxicological stud­
ies have demonstrated that phenolic compounds can exhibit 
carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, and endocrine-disrupting 
effects, even at relatively low environmental concentrations 
(Menon and Kalyanraman 2025). Quantitatively, phenolic 
compounds can exert acute toxicity at relatively low con­
centrations, with reported LC50 values for aquatic organ­
isms typically ranging from 5 to 25 mg/L, while sub-lethal 
effects such as enzymatic inhibition and oxidative stress 
have been observed at concentrations below 1 mg/L. More­
over, phenols and chlorophenols are often listed as priority 
pollutants by regulatory agencies due to their deleterious 
effects and recalcitrant behaviour. From a human health 
perspective, phenolic compounds pose a significant risk. 
For example, chronic exposure through ingestion or dermal 
contact can lead to neurological, hematological, and hepatic 
toxicity (Wang et al. 2019). Their removal from wastewa­
ter is therefore not only desirable but necessary to avoid 
environmental accumulation and potential health hazards. 
However, because of their chemical stability, conventional 
biological treatment processes sometimes fail to consis­
tently degrade phenols, especially when concentrationsare 
high or the influent composition is complex.
Biological treatment performance is highly dependent 
on phenolic concentration, as elevated levels can strongly 
inhibit microbial activity and destabilize reactor perfor­
mance. In addition, the speciation and toxicity of chlo­
rinated phenolic compounds such as 2,4-dichlorophenol 
(2,4-DCP) are strongly pH-dependent. Due to its weakly 
acidic nature (pKa ≈ 7.9), 2,4-DCP exists in both dissociated 
(ionized) and undissociated (non-ionized) forms depending 
on the solution pH. Azed form predominates, which is more 
hydrophobict lower pH values, the non-ioniand can readily 
diffuse through microbial cell membranes, thereby increas­
ing its inhibitory effects on biological processes, whereas 
under neutral to slightly alkaline conditions, the ionized 
form becomes dominant, reducing membrane permeability 
and overall toxicity. This pH-dependent behaviour plays a 
critical role in determining the bioavailability and inhibi­
tory impact of 2,4-DCP in anaerobic systems. Therefore, 
high-strength phenolic wastewater often requires dilution or 
appropriate pretreatment to reduce toxicity prior to biologi­
cal treatment, ensuring stable and efficient operation (Al-
Qodah et al. 2019).
Anaerobic membrane bioreactors (AnMBRs) have 
emerged as a compelling technology for treating industrial 
wastewaters, including those containing phenolic com­
pounds. Unlike conventional aerobic treatment, AnMBRs 
combine anaerobic biodegradation with membrane filtra­
tion, enabling high biomass retention, long solids reten­
tion time (SRT), and efficient recovery of biogas (Muñoz 
Sierra et al. 2020). Submerged configurations (SAnMBRs), 
in which the membrane module is immersed directly in the 
bioreactor, offer additional benefits such as reduced foot­
print, lower energy demand, and enhanced process stabil­
ity. In the context of phenolic wastewater, such systems are 
particularly attractive because the slow-growing anaerobic 
microbial communities (e.g., phenol-degrading syntrophic 
bacteria) can be retained within the reactor over long tim­
escales, maximizing the contact time necessary for degra­
dation (Rezakazemi et al. 2018). These microbial consortia 
typically include syntrophic bacteria such as Syntrophus and 
Syntrophorhabdus, which are involved in the initial degra­
dation of aromatic compounds, as well as methanogenic 
archaea such as Methanosaeta and Methanosarcina, which 
facilitate the final conversion of intermediates to methane 
(García Rea et al. 2020). Indeed, studies have demonstrated 
that submerged AnMBRs can achieve high removal effi­
ciencies: in one long-term experiment treating kraft evapo­
rator condensate, chemical oxygen demand (COD) removal 
exceeded 93–99% with stable methane production, and bio­
gas sparging was effective for controlling fouling (Muñoz 
Sierra et al. 2020). However, AnMBRs, especially sub­
merged ones, face nontrivial operational challenges. Mem­
brane fouling remains a primary limitation including cake 
formation, pore blocking, and the accumulation of extracel­
lular polymeric substances (EPS) or soluble microbial prod­
ucts (SMP) can degrade membrane performance, increase 
transmembrane pressure (TMP), and shorten membrane 
lifespan (Viet and Jang 2021). In addition, the complex and 
nonlinear interactions among operational variables, micro­
bial dynamics, and physicochemical properties make pre­
dictive modelling and control difficult.
In order to improve efficacy and parameter optimiza­
tion, machine learning focuses on developing algorithms 
that learn patterns from data and support prediction or 
decision-making.. In artificial intelligence (AI), machine 
learning focuses on developing computer algorithms to 
facilitate intelligent actions through data processing. AI 
also offers potential for real-time optimization and con­
trol, enabling dynamic operational strategies that adapt to 
changing conditions. However, in SAnMBR systems treat­
ing phenolic wastewater, the necessity of ML is not merely

While ML is widely used in MBRs, a systematic com­
parison of advanced non-linear techniques as well as inter­
pretability analyses remains limited, particularly when 
operating Submerged Anaerobic MBRs (SAnMBRs) under 
high phenolic shock loads for long periods of time. Stud­
ies often focus only on maximizing correlation coefficients, 
failing to investigate why the model works. Accordingly, 
the objective of this study is not limited to comparing model 
performance, but to link predictive capability with process 
understanding by identifying the key drivers and critical 
thresholds governing reactor stability. In this study, multiple 
linear regression (MLR), artificial neural networks (ANN), 
and support vector regression (SVR) are evaluated to predict 
COD removal efficiency in the treatment of 2,4-dichloro­
phenol wastewater using a pilot-scale SAnMBR, address­
ing a key gap in the existing literature. The selection of 
2,4-DCP as the target compound is specifically motivated 
by its well-documented inhibitory effects on anaerobic bio­
logical processes and its persistence in industrial effluents, 
making it a representative model pollutant for assessing 
system behaviour under phenolic shock loading conditions. 
Unlike readily biodegradable organics, 2,4-DCP introduces 
toxicity-driven non-linear responses that manifest at the 
system level, influencing overall reactor performance and 
stability rather than directly measured microbial activity 
(Li et al. 2024). Given the highly non-linear and coupled

the resulting framework can support the development of 
real-time control strategies (optimal pH ranges, safe 2,4-

operational expenditure, and advancing the practical digital 
deployment of SAnMBR technology.
2  Materials and Methods
2.1  Experimental Setup
The experimental setup of the SAnMBR is shown in Fig. 1. 
The reactor was made of Plexiglas with an effective vol­
ume of 8 L and a total volume of 8.733 L. The system was 
operated as a continuous-flow SAnMBR under long-term 
dynamic loading conditions (189 days), incorporating a full 
membrane module and process control strategy represen­
tative of practical operation. This SAnMBR was operated 
over a period of 189 days. The injection of 2,4-dichlorophe­
nol (2,4-DCP) commenced on day 118, after achieving sta­
ble baseline reactor performance, in order to systematically 
evaluate the system response to controlled phenolic shock 
loading. The organic loading rate (OLR) was gradually 
varied between 0.125 and 0.798 kg COD/m³·day to assess 
system performance under diverse operational conditions. 
Water-jacket compartments were installed on bothsides of 
the reactor and the reactor temperature was maintained at 
36 ± 1 °C by a circulating water bath. This controlled meso­
philic temperature was selected to maintain stable anaerobic 
conditions and eliminate external variability associated with 
ambient temperature fluctuations. The membrane module 
was composed of a submerged hollow fiber polypropylene 
membrane (Hydrol, UK) with an area of 0.1 m². Influent and 
effluent flows were regulated using two parallel peristaltic 
pumps (Shiva Amvaj, Iran), controlled via a digital multi-
timer (Shiva Amvaj, Isfahan, Iran). To minimize evapora­
tion and salt accumulation in the water bath, distilled water 
was used, and the bath surface was covered with plastic film.
2.2  Experimental Procedure
The reactor was seeded with 2000 mL of digested sludge 
obtained from the South Tehran municipal wastewater treat­
ment plant. Prior to seeding, the sludge was thoroughly 
washed with tap water to remove impurities and charac­
terized for total suspended solids (TSS: 21,082 mg/L) and 
volatile suspended solids (VSS: 12,937 mg/L).
Synthetic wastewater was freshly prepared daily, con­
taining glucose as the primary carbon source along with 
essential macro- and micronutrients sourced from Merck 
(KGaA, Germany). Nutrient concentrations were dosed 
per gram of COD as follows: NH₄Cl (76.45 mg/g COD), 
KH₂PO₄ (10.00  mg/g COD), and trace elements includ­
ing K₂HPO₄ (25.32), FeCl₃ (1.021), CaCl₂·2  H₂O (2.06), 
MgSO₄·7  H₂O (2.14), MnSO₄·H₂O (0.355), CoCl₂·6 H₂O 
(0.092), NiSO₄·6  H₂O (0.0763), ZnSO₄ (0.1055), (NH₄)
Mo₇O₂₄·4 H₂O (0.4198), CuSO₄·5 H₂O (0.0321), and H₃BO₃ 
(0.020). The reactor was operated continuously, with 2,4-
DCP concentrations gradually increased from 5  mg/L to 
300  mg/L following its initial injection. System perfor­
mance was assessed by monitoring COD and 2,4-DCP 
removal efficiencies under different loading regimes.
2.3  Analytical Methods
Physicochemical parameters including temperature, pH, 
COD, turbidity, TSS, TDS, VSS, 2,4-DCP concentration 
and electrical conductivity (EC) were monitored through­
out the study. COD and phenol concentrations were deter­
mined using the closed reflux colorimetric method (APHA 
5220D) and direct photometric method (APHA 5530D), 
respectively, with a DR 6000 spectrophotometer (Hach, 
Germany). Turbidity was measured using the nephelometric 
method with a Hach 2100AN turbidimeter..

2.4  Data Acquisition and Pre-processing
The predictive modelling analysis was based on an experi­
mental dataset collected throughout the operational period 
of the Submerged Anaerobic Membrane Bioreactor 
(SAnMBR). In total, 189 valid data points were obtained, 
with no missing entries. The selection of input features (X) 
was guided by their known relevance to biological waste­
water treatment and their operational importance in the 
SAnMBR process. Six independent variables were used in 
the modelling: influent COD (mg/L), organic loading rate 
(OLR, gCOD/m³·day), influent 2,4-dichlorophenol (mg/L), 
influent turbidity (NTU), influent TSS (g/L), and influent 
pH. The dependent variable (Y) was the COD removal per­
centage (%). To ensure reliable model training and evalua­
tion, the dataset was randomly divided into two parts: 70% 
(around 132 samples) for model training and the remain­
ing 30% (around 56 samples) for testing. This division 
allowed the trained models to be evaluated on data they had 
not previously encountered, providing a fair assessment of 
their generalization capability. Prior to normalization, the 
dataset was first split into training and testing subsets. The 
Min–Max scaling parameters were then fitted using only the 
training data and subsequently applied to both training and 
testing sets. All input features were normalized using the 
Min–Max scaling method to rescale their values to the [0,1] 
range. This normalization step avoided bias toward vari­
ables with larger numerical ranges and ensured that each 
input contributed equally during training.

2.5.2  Support Vector Regression (SVR)
In order to address the complex, non-linear nature of envi­
ronmental datasets, SVR was selected as the primary non-
linear predictive tool. This model was chosen because of 
its highgeneralization capacity, which is crucial when using 
complex, non-linear environmental datasets. This model 
uses a kernel function to map the six input features to a 
high-dimensional space, converting the non-linear regres­
sion problem into a linear one. As a result of rigorous 
optimization, the Radial Basis Function (RBF) kernel was 
selected, as it was most effective at capturing biological 
treatment dynamics. The model’s performance was fine-
tuned by optimizing the key hyperparameters, namely the 
penalty parameter (C) and the kernel width inline-eq-IEq7 (Najafza­

2.5.3  Artificial Neural Network (ANN)
Artificial neural network conceptsdate back to early artifi­
cial neuron models introduced in the 1940s is fundamentally 
based on simulating the structure and function of biologi­
cal nervous systems. As a result of the introduction of the 
backpropagation training algorithm, artificial neural net­
works can represent complex, non-linear functionality using 
fewer parameters. This capability has led to extensive use in 
a variety of fields, including water quality modelling, medi­
cine, and engineering (Reza et al. 2024). In order to balance 
the complexity against the risk of overfitting, the developed 
MLP network structure (6-N-1) was systematically tuned to 
determine the optimal number of neurons (N) in the hidden 
layer. The network architecture, which defines the flow of 
information through the system, is visually represented in 
Fig. 2. The architecture consists of: (1) an Input Layer with 
6 neurons, receiving the normalized input features; (2) one 
Hidden Layer using the tansig (tangent sigmoid) transfer 
function to introduce non-linearity; and (3) a single Output 
Layer neuron using the purelin (linear) function to yield the 
final COD Removal prediction. The network was trained in 
a standard computational environment (Python), replicat­
ing the desired output based on unseen inputs in accordance 
with the provided training data.

All machine learning models, including SVR, ANN, and 
MLR, as well as the interpretability analyses such as Par­

1.2.2) for SVR and MLR, while the ANN model was devel­
oped using the TensorFlow/Keras framework. Data prepro­
cessing, including normalization and dataset splitting, was 
carried out using the Pandas library, and visualization was 
performed using Matplotlib and Seaborn.

3  Results and Discussion
3.1  SAnMBR Experimental Performance
The 189-day time series (Fig. 4) shows that COD removal 
rose during an initial acclimation period (Days 1–50) 
from ~ 43% to roughly 85–90% and then remained mostly 
between 80% and 95% despite large, staged increases in 
OLR (from ~ 125 to ~ 800 g COD m⁻³·d⁻¹). Several non-
exclusive mechanisms can explain thisbehaviour.
A core advantage lies in membrane retention, which 
effectively decouples the Solids Retention Time (SRT) from 
hydraulic retention time (HRT). This separation enables 
the accumulation of slow-growing biomass, particularly 
methanogens, allowing the system to sustain efficient con­
version of soluble organic matter (COD) into methane. As 
a result, stable COD removal is maintained under varying 
loading conditions, and the impact of transient hydraulic 
and organic stress is significantly reduced. This critical SRT 
effect has been repeatedly observed as a primary advantage 
of SAnMBR configurations (Robles et al. 2018). Further­
more, the maintained mesophilic temperature (36 ± 1  °C) 
and high mixed liquor concentrations typical of long-term 
MBR systems enhance COD degradation kinetics by stabi­
lizing microbial activity and promoting enzyme-mediated 
reactions involved in hydrolysis and methanogenesis. Under 
mesophilic conditions, substrate utilization rates are more 
consistent and less sensitive to fluctuations, which supports 
efficient conversion of soluble organic matter and reduces 
the risk of kinetic limitations at elevated OLRs (Khan et 
al. 2024). As a result, higher apparent degradation rates 
and improved process stability are achieved under variable 
loading conditions. System-level control strategies are also 
key contributors to resilience. This includes controlled feed 
step-changes and proactive fouling management (such as 
gas sparging, regular backwash, and chemical cleaning), 
all of which limit the likelihood that transient high loads 
or membrane fouling translate into long-term performance 
loss.
Additionally, the observed stability is partially attribut­
able to microbial community adaptation. Under prolonged 
exposure to persistent substrates and phenolic compounds, a 
selective pressure is applied, which can increase the relative 
abundance of degraders and methanogens adapted to these 
recalcitrant substrates. Representative genera reported in 
similar anaerobic systems include syntrophic bacteria such 
as Syntrophus and Syntrophorhabdus, which are involved in 
the initial breakdown of aromatic compounds, and metha­
nogenic archaea such as Methanosaeta and Methanosar­
cina, responsible for the final conversion of intermediates to 
methane (Muñoz Sierra et al. 2020). These microbial func­
tions are consistent with those reported in comparable phe­
nolic wastewater treatment systems. Where such favourable 
adaptation occurs, reactors show improved stability, which 
is highly consistent with the high removal rates seen in 
this campaign. However, it is important to note that perfor­
mance can significantly decline past reactor-specific OLR 
thresholds.

present results and the plateau values reported in the litera­

3.3.1  Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs)
PDPs are used to visualize the interaction between individ­
ual input features and their respective effect on the machine 
learning model’s output prediction, assuming all other fea­
tures remain constant. These plots illustrate the impact of 
changing a single feature on the prediction of the dependent 
variable, thereby making the results of the advanced SVR 
model more interpretable and understandable. Fig. 11 shows 
the PDPs for six key numerical input parameters employed 
in the current study.
The PDP for influent 2,4-dichlorophenol (2,4-DCP) con­
centration reveals a non-linear response pattern. An initial 
increase in partial dependence values is observed within the 
lower concentration range (0–150 mg/L), indicating that the 
model predicts relatively stable or slightly improved COD 
removal performance in this interval. This behaviour sug­
gests that, within this range, the system is not immediately 
limited by toxicity effects. However, beyond 150 mg/L, the 
trend gradually plateaus and begins to decline, reflecting the 
onset of inhibitory effects on microbial activity. This tran­
sition highlights the balance between substrate availability 
and toxicity, where increasing pollutant concentration ini­
tially does not suppress system performance but eventually 
leads to inhibition, particularly affecting methanogenic pro­
cesses (Li et al. 2024).
For influent pH, the PDP showed a clear non-linear rela­
tionship, with the highest predicted COD removal efficiency 
observed in the pH range of approximately 8–9. Outside 
this optimal window, particularly under acidic (pH < 7) or 
strongly alkaline (pH > 9) conditions, the predicted perfor­
mance declines. This trend is consistent with the known sen­
sitivity of anaerobic microbial communities to pH, where 
deviations from the optimal range negatively affect enzy­
matic activity and metabolic stability (Meegoda et al. 2018).
Comparatively, influent COD (mg/L) and OLR (gCOD/
m³·day) exhibit relatively minor influence on the predicted 
COD removal efficiency within the studied ranges, indi­
cating that the system operated below its critical loading 
threshold. Similarly, influent turbidity (NTU) and TSS (g/L) 
show a slight negative trend, where increasing values corre­
spond to marginal decreases in predicted efficiency. This is 
consistent with the rate-limiting nature of particulate hydro­
lysis in anaerobic systems, as particulate matter requires 
additional processing steps compared to soluble substrates 
(Wang et al. 2022).
3.3.2  Sensitivity Analysis
This section quantifies the influence of individual input 
features on the optimal SVR model’s prediction of COD 
removal using univariate sensitivity analysis framework. 
Unlike model-specific explainability methods such as 
SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which rely on 
internal model structures or game-theoretic formulations, 
the present approach is a model-agnostic sensitivity analy­
sis designed to evaluate the response of the trained model to 
controlled variations in input variables. This approach pro­
vides a direct and computationally efficient assessment of 
global feature influence, suitable for identifying dominant

process variables. In this approach, each input feature was 
varied independently across its observed range using a 
systematic discretization procedure, while all other input 
variables were held constant at their baseline values. Specif­
ically, the baseline inputs were defined using the mean val­
ues of the originaldataset, and each variable was perturbed 
across its minimum–maximum range using a fixed number 
of uniformly spaced intervals. For each variable, a series of 
uniformly spaced points within its range was generated, and 
the trained SVR model was used to predict COD removal 
for each modified input set. The sensitivity index was then 
calculated as the mean absolute deviation between the pre­
dicted outputs obtained from the perturbed inputs and the 
baseline predictions. This metric quantifies the extent to 
which variations in each input feature influence the model 
output. Higher sensitivity values indicate stronger influence, 
while values approaching zero suggest minimal impact. The 
sensitivity analysis plot (Fig. 12) shows the relative impor­
tance of each input variable, with 2,4-DCP concentration 
and pH emerging as the main factors influencing predicted 
COD removal.
The analysis clearly establishes the crucial role of specific 
input parameters. Influent 2,4-dichlorophenol (mg/L) exhib­
its the highest sensitivity, confirming its status as the most 
influential feature in the SVR model. This finding is highly 
consistent with the partial dependence plot (PDP) results, 
which showed a strong negative impact on COD removal 
(a predicted decline from a stable 90% to below 70%) as 
its concentration increased. The high sensitivity to 2,4-DCP 
highlights the need to control inhibitory compounds, as this 
response is consistent with the known toxicity of chlorophe­
nols toward methanogenic archaea in anaerobic systems.
Influent pH is the second most influential parameter, 
which accurately reflects the SAnMBR system’s biologi­
cal nature and its dependence on a narrow optimal range. 
The PDPs demonstrate that removal efficiency is maxi­
mized within an optimal pH range of approximately 8–9, 
beyond which system performance progressively declines 
due to reduced microbial activity under non-optimal condi­
tions. Furthermore, parameters related to bulk organic load, 
such as influent COD and OLR, displayed lower sensitivity. 
Their limited influence is consistent with the PDPs showing 
largely flat impact curves, suggesting that the SAnMBR sys­
tem was highly robust and operated below its peak loading 
capacity throughout the dataset range. Influent turbidity and 
influent TSS had the lowest sensitivity scores, indicating 
that changes in these variables had only a minor effect on 
the SVR-predicted COD removal. This may be because par­
ticulate matter affects anaerobic treatment mainly through 
hydrolysis, which is typically slower than the degradation of 
soluble organic compounds. As a result, marginal changes 
in TSS or turbidity were less influential than soluble inhibi­
tory factors such as 2,4-DCP.
3.4  Operational Implications and Fouling Risk 
Analysis
The interpretation of the SVR model provides insight into 
both biological treatment performance and membrane-
related operation within the SAnMBR system. Although 
influent TSS and turbidity showed low sensitivity toward 
predicted COD removal, their role should be interpreted 
within the broader operating context rather than dismissed as 
irrelevant. Within the investigated range, the low sensitivity

of TSS and turbidity indicates that particulate-related vari­
ables had a limited effect on SVR-predicted COD removal 
compared with pH and 2,4-DCP concentration. This obser­
vation is specific to the operating conditions investigated in 
this study and should not be generalized across anaerobic 
treatment systems. COD comprises both soluble and par­
ticulate fractions, and the hydrolysis of particulate organic 
matter is often the rate-limiting step in anaerobic digestion. 
In SAnMBRs, however, extended solids retention and effec­
tive biomass retention can promote particulate hydrolysis, 
which may explain why TSS and turbidity showed limited 
influence on predicted COD removal under stable operating 
conditions(Sandoval-García et al. 2025; Turan 2025).
Despite their relatively minor contribution to predicted 
COD removal, particulate-related parameters remain criti­
cally important from an operational perspective, particularly 
in relation to membrane fouling behaviour. As illustrated 
in Fig.  13, membrane fouling in the SAnMBR system is 
governed by multiple interacting mechanisms rather than 
a single dominant process. Initially, the accumulation of 
suspended solids and colloidal matter near the membrane 
surface leads to concentration polarization, which increases 
local solute concentration and promotes further deposition. 
This is followed by the formation of an external bio-cake 
layer composed of biomass, TSS, and extracellular poly­
meric substances (EPS). Over time, EPS production and 
microbial activity facilitate the penetration of organic mat­
ter into membrane pores, resulting in pore blocking and, 
ultimately, internal pore clogging. These sequential and 
overlapping mechanisms contribute to increased hydraulic 
resistance and progressive decline in membrane perfor­
mance, leading to elevated transmembrane pressure and 
more frequent maintenance requirements (Du et al. 2020; 
Zhang and Hao 2022). 
These results highlight a fundamental decoupling 
between biological treatment efficiency and operational sus­
tainability. While the SVR model identifies COD removal 
as primarily governed by biochemical and inhibitory factors 
(e.g., pH and 2,4-DCP concentration), particulate matter 
exerts a dominant influence on membrane fouling dynam­
ics and long-term system stability. The low sensitivity of 
TSS in the SVR model should therefore not be interpreted 
as operational insignificance, but rather as an indication that 
fouling-related impacts are not captured by COD-based per­
formance metrics.
From an operational standpoint, this dual behaviour 
underscores the necessity of adopting a multi-objective 
optimization strategy. Optimization based solely on COD 
removal may lead to suboptimal operating conditions, where 
membrane fouling accelerates despite stable treatment per­
formance. Although increasing TSS may not significantly 
affect COD removal efficiency within the studied range, it 
directly contributes to more frequent cleaning, increased 
chemical consumption, and elevated operational costs (Ma 
et al. 2015). This suggests that, beyond a critical particu­
late threshold, the system transitions from a performance-
controlled regime to a fouling-controlled regime, even when 
apparent treatment efficiency remains unchanged. There­
fore, controlling particulate load is essential not only for 
fouling mitigation but also for maintaining overall system 
efficiency and cost-effectiveness. These findings emphasize 
that optimal SAnMBR operation cannot be defined solely 
by treatment performance indicators, but must incorporate 
fouling propensity, membrane resistance development, and 
operational resilience under variable process conditions.
4  Conclusion
This study shows that a data-driven modelling approach 
can capture the non-linear behaviour of SAnMBR systems 
under phenolic shock conditions, with SVR providing reli­
able prediction of COD removal in a biologically sensitive 
process. Model interpretation showed that performance was 
strongly affected by the interaction between pH and inhibi­
tory phenolic compounds, suggesting that stable operation 
depends not only on organic loading but also on keeping 
toxicity below critical thresholds. The decline in biologi­
cal performance at elevated 2,4-DCP concentrations fur­
ther indicates that high-strength phenolic wastewater may 
require dilution, pretreatment, or selective recovery of phe­
nolic compounds before biological treatment. This agrees 
with previous studies showing that detoxification can 
improve microbial activity and enhance subsequent anaero­
bic treatment. The results also show that biological removal 
and membrane operation are controlled by different factors. 
COD removal was mainly influenced by biochemical con­
ditions, whereas particulate matter remains important for 
membrane fouling and long-term process stability. Although 
phenolic compounds were treated as inhibitory pollutants in 
this study, their recovery before biological treatment is gain­
ing attention because of their potential value as antioxidants 
and industrial precursors. These findings support the use of 
interpretable machine-learning models for digital monitor­
ing and adaptive control of SAnMBR systems treating com­
plex industrial wastewater. Future work should integrate the 
developed SVR model into real-time control frameworks 
to support dynamic adjustment of key variables such as 
pH and influent 2,4-DCP concentration. Including fouling-
related indicators, such as EPS, SMP, and transmembrane 
pressure, would also allow multi-objective optimisation of 
biological performance and membrane operation.
