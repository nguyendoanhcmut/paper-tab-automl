3.2. Construction and evaluation of base models

3.2.1. Correlation-based feature selection
Feature–target (X–y) Pearson correlation analysis was further con­
ducted, and the ranking results are shown in Fig. S4. Overall, FLUX, 
EPSc, HRT, and SMPp showed relatively strong correlations with TMP, 
with | rx,y⃒⃒
values of 0.471, 0.462, 0.440, and 0.347, respectively. In 
contrast, SCOD, SMPc, and TCOD showed weak correlations with TMP, 
with | rx,y⃒⃒
values all below 0.1, and were therefore excluded from the 
reduced input set used for subsequent modeling. The remaining vari­
ables were kept for the subsequent collinearity screening step.
Feature–feature (X–X) Pearson correlation analysis was then per­
formed to further control inter-feature collinearity (Fig. 3(b)). HRT was 
strongly negatively correlated with FLUX (rxj,xk = -0.86), likely reflect­
ing their operational coupling: HRT is inversely related to treatment 
flow rate, whereas FLUX represents the permeate throughput per unit 
membrane area [8]. MLSS and MLVSS showed a very strong positive 
correlation (rxj,xk = 0.96), consistent with MLVSS representing the vol­
atile biomass fraction of MLSS [40]. Within these two collinear feature 
pairs, FLUX and MLVSS had higher | rx,y | values with TMP and were 
therefore retained. Accordingly, FLUX, SRT, MLVSS, EPSc, EPSp, and 
SMPp were retained as a reduced input set for subsequent base model 
development.

3.2.2. Performance evaluation of base models
The prediction scatter plots for both base models show that most 
training and testing points were distributed close to the line of equality, 
indicating good agreement between the predicted and observed TMP 
values in the source plants. XGBoost-base achieved an R2 of 0.86, an 
RMSE of 3.01 kPa, and an MAE of 1.94 kPa on the test set (Fig. 3(c)). 
LSTM-base showed slightly better performance, with an R2 of 0.87, an 
RMSE of 2.86 kPa, and an MAE of 1.68 kPa (Fig. 3(d)). Larger prediction 
deviations were observed at higher TMP values in both models, sug­
gesting that TMP prediction became more difficult under more severe 
fouling conditions, possibly due to intensified fouling-layer compression 
and nonlinear fouling resistance at elevated TMP levels [41]. Overall, 
both models captured the main TMP variations in the source datasets, 
with LSTM-base showing a small but consistent advantage over 
XGBoost-base.

3.2.3. SHAP interpretation of base models and physicochemical 
characterization
SHAP attribution patterns were further analyzed for both base 
models. ChatGPT-5.4 was used only as an auxiliary tool to support the 
summarization of these attribution patterns, while the final interpreta­
tion was performed by the authors based on the SHAP plots and corre­
sponding feature-attribution results (Fig. 6(d)). For LSTM-base, the 
SHAP summary plots showed that EPSc, MLVSS, EPSp, and SMPp made 
the largest contributions (Fig. 6(a); Fig. S5), whereas FLUX and SRT 
were comparatively less influential. XGBoost-base showed a much more 
concentrated attribution pattern, with FLUX dominating the SHAP 
attribution and contributing more than the other input variables (Fig. S6 
(a); Fig. S6(b)). These results indicate that the two base models achieved 
similar predictive performance but relied on different predictive struc­
tures during pretraining on the source plants. The broader dependence 
of LSTM-base on EPSc, MLVSS, EPSp, and SMPp may partly reflect the 
greater suitability of sequence-learning architectures for variables with 
stronger temporal continuity and gradual variation [42]. In contrast, the 
FLUX-dominated attribution pattern in XGBoost-base was consistent 
with the X–y correlation analysis and may partly reflect the tendency of 
tree-based models to rely heavily on a dominant predictive signal [43,
44]. These results were consistent with the subsequent transfer learning 
results, which showed that XGBoost-FT achieved only limited 
improvement compared with LSTM-FT (discussed in Section 3.3).
Given that LSTM-base achieved strong source-domain predictive 
performance and showed much more promise in transfer learning (dis­
cussed in Section 3.3), the physicochemical characteristics of biomass in 
the source plants were further examined to determine whether its 
learned attribution pattern was consistent with real fouling mechanisms. 
EPS and SMP were emphasized, because they were identified as 
important features in the SHAP analysis. Specifically, EPS is more 
closely associated with sludge flocs and membrane-associated fouling 
[45], whereas SMP mainly represents soluble organics [46].
Concretely, the three source plants showed a consistent EPS-related 
signature, including a shared aromatic protein-like peak in the EPS 
EEM spectra (Ex/Em ≈220–225/325–355 nm; Fig. 4(a)–(c)), and sludge 
particle-size distributions dominated by particles of 20–40 μm (Fig. 4
(d)). These features generally suggested a readily depositable sludge 
structure that could promote cake-layer formation in MBRs [47]. 
Moreover, the protein fraction represented by EPSp likely comprises 
aromatic protein-like components, which could be associated with 
sludge floc aggregation and the initial adhesion of foulants to the 
membrane surface [48]. By comparison, EPSc may be more closely 
related to the gel-forming and resistance-enhancing effects of the poly­
saccharide matrix, thereby further increasing the hydraulic resistance of 
the fouling layer [49]. The relatively high attribution of MLVSS 
(revealed by SHAP) was also physically reasonable, as MLVSS represents 
the biomass fraction that carries EPS and contributes to sludge deposi­
tion on the membrane surface [3].
Additionally, SMP from the three source plants exhibited similar 
EEM spectral features, with a shared aromatic protein-like fluorescence 
peak (Ex/Em ≈220–230/330–350 nm; Fig. S7(a)–(c)). These aromatic 
protein-like SMP components may contribute to fouling development 
through membrane-surface adsorption or retention within the devel­
oping fouling layer, particularly during the early stage of membrane 
fouling [50,51]. This interpretation was further supported by the 
LC-OCD results (Fig. 4(e) and (f); Table S9), which showed that SMP in 
the three source plants was dominated by hydrophilic DOC, with humics 
and biopolymers as the major fractions. For each LC-OCD fraction, 
rejection was calculated as the percentage decrease from its concen­
tration in SMP to that in the corresponding effluent, relative to its 
concentration in SMP. On this basis, biopolymers showed the highest 
rejection across the three source plants, ranging from 90.0 to 95.3%. 
These results suggest that SMPp may mainly reflect the aromatic 
protein-like component within the biopolymer pool that was preferen­
tially retained by the membrane and could therefore participate directly 
in organic membrane fouling [52].
Overall, the shared physicochemical characteristics of source plants 
provide clear mechanism-informed support for the EPS- and SMP- 
related attribution pattern learned by LSTM-base. This indicates that 
the learned structure was not merely statistical but grounded in real 
fouling-related properties. In turn, this supports the use of LSTM-base as 
a reliable pretrained model for subsequent transfer learning.
