A B S T R A C T
The application of machine learning (ML) offers significant advantages in predicting critical performance metrics 
during wastewater treatment, enabling optimized operational control and mitigating challenges such as mem­
brane fouling. This study employed ML methods to evaluate COD removal and membrane fouling in a mesophilic 
anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) for transfer station landfill leachate treatment operated for 120 days. 
The system achieved ≥80% COD removal at organic loading rate (OLR) of 6.4 kg COD/m³ ⋅d and hydraulic 
retention time (HRT) of 6.5 days. Artificial neural networks (ANN), random forest (RF), and extreme gradient 
boosting (XGBoost) were developed to predict effluent COD and transmembrane pressure (TMP). RF achieved the 
strongest predictive performance for both effluent COD (R² = 0.96; RMSE = 331.50 mg/L) and TMP (R² = 0.88; 
RMSE = 3.86 kPa). SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis identified OLR, HRT and oxidation reduction 
potential (ORP) as the main model-associated predictors for effluent COD, while effluent NH4
+-N, effluent pH and 
ORP contributed most strongly to TMP prediction. These results show that integrating AnMBR operation with 
interpretable ML-based predictive modelling can provide useful diagnostic insights for optimizing leachate 
treatment performance and membrane fouling management.
1. Introduction
Rapid urbanization and rising living standards have driven a sharp 
increase in municipal solid waste, placing significant strain on urban 
waste management systems. Urban waste management generally in­
volves three interconnected stages: front-end collection, mid-end 
transfer, and end-of-life treatment [1]. Waste transfer stations serve as 
a key link between waste collection and final disposal [2]. Leachate 
generated at these stations is compositionally complex, typically con­
taining high levels of organic matter, ammonia, soluble microbial 
products (SMP), suspended solids, and toxic substances [3,4]. Without 
proper treatment, such pollutants can severely impact soil, surface 
water, groundwater, and the atmosphere [5]. In practice, leachate 
management at transfer stations is frequently constrained by limited 
space [2], economic barriers to advanced treatment technologies, and, 
in some cases, insufficient regulatory enforcement. Consequently, 
transfer station landfill leachate is often stored inadequately or dis­
charged without minimal treatment, increasing environmental risks [1].
Anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) technology has received 
increased attention as a promising approach for the treatment and reuse 
of industrial and municipal high-strength wastewaters [3], owing to its 
high-quality effluent, compact footprint, and potential for energy re­
covery [6,7]. However, membrane fouling remains the most critical 
challenge limiting the stable and cost-effective operation of AnMBR [8, 
9]. Fouling leads to elevated transmembrane pressure (TMP), reduced 
permeate flux, and increased maintenance cost, ultimately affecting the 
long-term feasibility of AnMBR application [10,11]. To mitigate mem­
brane fouling, various control strategies have been explored, including 
influent pre-treatment, increased aeration intensity, and chemical con­
ditioning of mixed liquor [9,12]. Nevertheless, these approaches are 
often energy-intensive, economically unattractive, or insufficient to 
highly variable waste streams like landfill leachate [13].
Recent advances in Machine learning (ML) have demonstrated 
strong capabilities in modelling complex, nonlinear relationships 
inherent in environmental systems. ML methods, such as artificial neural 
networks (ANNs) [14], support vector regression (SVR) [15], extreme 
gradient boosting (XGBoost), and random forest (RF) [12,16], have been 
widely applied to monitor and optimize wastewater treatment processes

Despite these advances, most ML applications have focused on relatively 
stable municipal or industrial systems [3,20,8,21], with limited atten­
tion to highly variable, high-strength waste streams such as landfill 
leachates, where influent composition and loading conditions can fluc­
tuate sharply over time [18,13,14]. Moreover, existing models are often 
trained for interpolation within historical data ranges and may lack 
robustness under sudden loading fluctuations or operational distur­
bances [22]. These limitations are particularly critical in AnMBR sys­
tems, where treatment performance and membrane fouling evolution 
are strongly influenced by time-dependent interactions among influent 
quality, operating conditions, and microbial community dynamics [20, 
23]. Thus, there is a clear need for adaptive, dynamic modelling 
frameworks capable of integrating real-time sensor data and reliably 
predicting system behaviour under varying operating scenarios.
In response, this study integrates 120-day AnMBR operation for 
transfer-station leachate treatment with interpretable, data-driven ML 
models. The reactor was operated under systematically varied hydraulic 
retention time (HRT) and organic loading rate (OLR) conditions to 
evaluate COD removal, nitrogen transformation and membrane fouling 
behaviour. Fouling development was assessed through operational 
monitoring, antifouling performance evaluation, and characterization of 
foulant composition and particle size distribution. Microbial community 
structure and functional shifts were also analysed to better understand 
the linkages among microbial dynamics, reactor performance and 
fouling development. Three ML algorithms, namely RF, ANN and 
XGBoost, were developed to predict effluent COD and TMP using 
routinely monitored influent, effluent and operational parameters. 
Model hyperparameters were optimized using randomized search and 5- 
fold cross-validation. Model interpretation was performed using SHap­
ley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to identify the variables most strongly 
associated with COD and TMP predictions. The aim of this study is 
therefore to provide a quantitative operational boundary, a reversed 
foulant predictor, and an interpretable, sensor-lite diagnostic framework 
for understanding and predicting AnMBR performance during the 
treatment of high-strength and highly variable transfer-station landfill 
leachate. By integrating long-term reactor operation, microbial com­
munity analysis and interpretable ML models, this work provides useful 
diagnostic insights into the OLR-HRT operating window, COD removal 
behaviour and membrane fouling development, thereby supporting 
more sustainable and robust AnMBR-based leachate management at 
waste transfer facilities.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Leachate characteristics and inoculum
The leachate used in this study was obtained from a municipal solid 
waste transfer station in Changsha City, Hunan Province. The key 
physicochemical properties of leachate are summarized in Table S1
(Supplementary Material).
2.2. Reactor configuration and operating conditions
A laboratory-scale AnMBR with a total volume of 5.0 L (effective 
working volume 4 L) was fabricated from transparent acrylic. The sys­
tem was inoculated with anaerobic sludge accounting for 30% of the 
working capacity. The initial HRT was set at 15 d, corresponding to a 
feed flow rate of 0.33 L/d. The reactor was operated at mesophilic 
temperature (35 ± 2 ◦C). A polyvinylidene fluoride hollow fiber mem­
brane module with a surface area of 712 cm² and a pore size of 0.1 µm 
was employed for filtration, operated intermittently with a 4-min suc­
tion followed by a 1-min relaxation period, controlled by a peristaltic 
pump.
The operation of AnMBR spanned 120 days and was divided into 
three phases, each characterized by distinct HRT and OLR settings to 
simulate real operating scenarios. Phase I (start-up and adaptation, 1–28 
d) involved a gradual HRT reduction from 15 to 9.5 d, with influent 
concentration increased stepwise from 20% to 100% of the raw leachate. 
Consequently, OLR rose from a low level to approximately 4.5 kg COD/ 
m³ ⋅d. Phase II (stable operation, days 29–103) saw a further HRT 
decrease from 9.5 to 6.5 days, while the influent remained at 100% 
concentration; OLR was maintained between 5.0 and 6.0 kg COD/m³ ⋅d. 
Phase III (shock loading test, 104–120 d) was designed to evaluate 
system resilience under sudden organic overload. During this phase, 
HRT was held constant at 5 d, influent concentration was kept at 100%, 
and OLR was sustained within 7.5–8.0 kg COD/m³ ⋅d. Throughout the 
experiment, sludge was withdrawn daily and other parameters were 
monitored at different frequencies according to their analytical re­
quirements and operational relevance. COD, NH4
+-N, and pH were 
measured every 2 days, while ORP was measured daily. TMP was 
continuously monitored using an online pressure transducer, and daily 
representative TMP values were logged for model development. EPS and 
SMP were extracted and analyzed weekly, and microbial samples were 
collected at the end of each operational stage. The above parameters 
were measured in a single time, as the high sampling frequency offsets 
the lack of formal replication.
2.3. Analytical methods
COD analysis was conducted by the potassium dichromate oxidation 
method as per the requirements listed in GB11914–89 and APHA stan­
dard procedure. The concentration of NH4
+-N was measured by color­
imetry, utilizing Nessler’s reagent to determine NH4
+-N concentrations at 
all stages of reactor operation. pH and ORP were measured using a 
digital pH meter (PHS-3C) and ORP meter (Pe−20k), EPS and SMP were 
extracted following previous work [7]. Loosely bound EPS (Lb-EPS) was 
obtained by vortexing and centrifugation, whereas tightly bound EPS 
(Tb-EPS) was extracted by thermal treatment followed by centrifuga­
tion. Protein and polysaccharide concentrations were analyzed using the 
Lowry and anthracene-sulfuric acid methods, respectively. Fouling 
conditions were continuously recorded by TMP. The sludge properties 
were analyzed in terms of particle size distribution using the Mastersizer 
3000 and Zeta potential using the Zetasizer Nano ZS. Fluorescence 
excitation-emission matrix spectroscopy was used to analyze the dis­
solved organic matter content.
To study microbial dynamics in the AnMBR, sludge samples were 
collected at the end of each operational stage from the membrane- 
adhered sludge, representing the biofilm/cake-layer community 
directly involved in fouling development. DNA was extracted, and the 
V4 region of bacterial and archaeal 16S rRNA genes was amplified using 
universal primers 515 F (5′-GTGCCCAGCMGCCGCGG−3′) and 806 R (5′- 
GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT−3′). High-throughput sequencing was 
performed by Majorbio (Shanghai, China), and bioinformatic analysis 
was conducted on the Majorbio Bioinformatics Cloud Platform to obtain 
microbial composition and diversity indices. Sequencing data were then 
used to identify community shifts associated with operational phase 
transitions and membrane fouling behaviour [12].

2.5. Model development and selection
Three ML algorithms, ANN (multilayer perception), RF, and XGBoost 
were developed predictive models for effluent COD and TMP in the

operational variables and system performance in wastewater treatment

2.6. SHAP interpretation method
Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is an interpretable ML 
approach derived from cooperative game theory that quantifies the 
contribution of each input feature to a model prediction. SHAP repre­
sents a complex model as an additive explanation model while satisfying 
key properties such as local accuracy, consistency, and missingness. The 
contribution of each feature is expressed through its SHAP value, which 
reflects the marginal impact of that feature on the prediction across all 
possible feature combinations [19]. This enables a transparent and 
theoretically grounded interpretation of feature importance. The SHAP

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Organic removal efficiency in AnMBR systems
The AnMBR system demonstrated consistent performance across all 
operational stages despite variations in HRT and OLR. During the start- 
up and adaptation phase (days 1–28, Fig. 1a), influent COD concentra­
tion increased while HRT decreased from 15 to 9.5 days. Effluent COD 
remained below 900 mg/L, with COD removal efficiency rising from 
90% to 99%, indicating effective microbial adaptation. Over this period, 
OLR increased from 0.42 to 4.65 kg/m³d. During the stable operation 
phase (days 29–103), HRT was reduced further to 6.5 days with 100% 
raw leachate as the influent. Despite intermittent fluctuations in influent 
COD concentration, removal efficiency remained remarkably high, 
suggesting that the reactor was approaching its treatment capacity. At 
this phase, the AnMBR exhibited robust and stable performance. Under 
the shock loading phase (days 104–120), the system experienced highly 
stressful conditions, with effluent COD peaking at approximately 
5500 mg/L and OLR ranging from 7.5 to 8.0 kg COD/m³ ⋅d. COD 
removal efficiency declined to 84–88%, which can be attributed to 
organic overload and potential leachate toxicity [19].
As presented in Fig. 1b, effluent NH4
+-N concentrations remained 
persistently high throughout the experiment, averaging 1300 mg/L. 
This implies that under strictly anaerobic conditions, the AnMBR 
exhibited very limited capacity for NH4
+-N removal [25], despite 
achieving effective organic matter removal [26]. Similar behaviour has 
been reported for various anaerobic membrane systems treating 
high-strength wastewater [27]. To overcome this limitation, integrating 
AnMBR with aerobic post treatment units, such as nitrification or partial 
ammonia oxidation processes, was thus recommended by previous 
studies [28,29] to enhance overall nitrogen removal performance.
3.2. Membrane fouling behaviour and particle characteristics
Fig. 2a shows TMP dynamics under varying HRT conditions. At the 
end of each operating phase, the membrane was cleaned to restore 
permeability before the next phase was started. Physical cleaning was 
applied after Phase I, sodium hypochlorite cleaning was applied after 
Phase II, and citric acid cleaning was applied after Phase III. These 
cleaning steps reduced the accumulated hydraulic resistance and 
returned TMP close to the initial baseline level. At an HRT of 9.5 d, TMP 
remained stable at around 1–2 kPa, indicating minimal fouling during 
the system adaptation phase. However, when HRT was reduced to 7.5 d, 
TMP increased sharply to approximately 35 kPa, suggesting pronounced

chemical cleaning in restoring membrane functionality. In summary, 
TMP effectively captures fouling phenomena in AnMBRs, with particular 
emphasis on HRT optimization to mitigate fouling and maintain supe­

3.3. Composition and functional roles of microbial communities
The microbial community in the AnMBR showed significant shifts 
over the operational period, closely following reactor performance and 
environmental variation. The microbial community analysis, including 
phylum/class-level composition and functional predictions, is shown in 
Fig. S3. Firmicutes remained abundant facilitating efficient decomposi­
tion of organic compounds. In contrast, the proportion of Proteobacteria 
decreased, reflecting reduced degradation of refractory organics and 
increased production of humic-like substances under shorter HRT. 
Meanwhile, the relative abundance of Chloroflexi rose from 6.6% to 
37.5%, while groups related to Anaerolineaceae became more abundant, 
which might indicate their involvement in sludge structure, EPS pro­
duction, biofilm formation and development of cake layers. These 
compositional changes in microbial community clearly indicate a strong 
interplay between microbial community structure and reactor operation 
in the AnMBR system [12,30]. Enrichment of functionally relevant mi­
crobial groups supported stable process performance even under 
elevated loading conditions. In relation to membrane fouling, these 
microbial shifts were interpreted together with TMP, EPS/SMP, EEM, 
particle-size, SEM, and cleaning-response results Fig. 2(b-e). The com­
bined evidence thus establishes a consistent pathway: operational stress 
(HRT reduction) → selective enrichment of fouling-associated taxa in 
the membrane-adhered sludge → enhanced EPS/SMP secretion → 
aggravated cake-layer formation and pore blocking → TMP rise.

3.4.2. Hyperparameter optimization results
To maximize predictive accuracy while preventing overfitting, 
hyperparameter optimization was performed using randomized search 
with 5-fold cross-validation (CV). This approach systematically explores 
the hyperparameter space to identify configurations that optimize 
generalization performance. Table 1 summarizes the optimized hyper­
parameters and corresponding mean cross-validation scores for both 
prediction targets.
For effluent COD prediction, RF demonstrated superior stability 
during CV, achieving the highest R² (0.94) and lowest RMSE 
(396.65 mg/L). XGBoost performed comparably (CV R² = 0.92), while 
ANN showed greater prediction variability (CV R² = 0.87). XGBoost 
exhibited comparable performance (cross-validation R² =
0.92), 
whereas the artificial neural network (ANN) showed relatively lower 
predictive stability (cross-validation R² = 0.87). For TMP prediction, 
XGBoost attained the highest cross-validation performance in the 
transformed target space, with R² = 0.69 and RMSE = 0.54 kPa, fol­
lowed by RF (R² = 0.62, RMSE = 0.59 kPa), while the ANN yielded 
weaker cross-validation results (R² = 0.47, RMSE = 0.73 kPa). However, 
the performance of XGBoost raises concerns regarding potential over­
fitting due to its model complexity compared to the small size of the TMP 
dataset. Thus, although XGBoost had slightly superior cross-validation 
performance, RF provided the strongest generalization for TMP 
prediction.

involving biological activity, EPS accumulation, cake layer formation, 
and physicochemical interactions (Fig. 4d–f and Table 2). After model 
optimization, RF achieved the best TMP prediction performance (R² =
0.88, RMSE = 3.86 kPa), while XGBoost and ANN yielded weaker 
generalization, with R² values of 0.56 and 0.54 and RMSE values of 7.50 
and 7.59 kPa, respectively. The predicted versus observed plots showed 
that RF predictions aligned more closely with the 1:1 line than those 
from XGBoost and ANN. XGBoost closely fitted the training data but 
exhibited larger deviations on the test set, indicating overfitting and 
reduced transferability to unseen observations. ANN predictions were 
more dispersed, suggesting a limited capacity to capture the nonlinear 
TMP response under the available dataset.
Residual analysis confirmed these patterns (Fig. S4d–f). RF produced 
the most compact residual distribution for TMP among the three models, 
although some deviations remained at higher TMP magnitudes, indi­
cating increased uncertainty under more severe fouling conditions. 
XGBoost displayed larger residual errors despite its strong training 
performance, supporting the interpretation that the model captured 
training-specific patterns rather than stable fouling dynamics. ANN re­
siduals were also widely scattered, reflecting weaker generalization for 
TMP prediction. Overall, the residual spread was greater for TMP than 
for effluent COD, confirming that fouling prediction is more uncertain 
than COD prediction. This behaviour is consistent with previous mem­
brane fouling studies, where data-driven models may perform well 
under normal operating conditions but become less reliable during 
abrupt or severe fouling development [8,31].
Overall, effluent COD (R² > 0.90) proved more predictable than TMP 
(R² = 0.80–0.50), consistent with COD being governed by relatively 
deterministic, load-dependent kinetics, whereas TMP reflects highly 
nonlinear and stochastic fouling mechanisms influenced by biological 
activity, EPS accumulation, and physicochemical conditions [8]. RF is 
therefore best suited for COD monitoring due to its stability and accu­
racy, while ANN provides superior robustness for TMP prediction under 
variable loading. XGBoost poor TMP generalization underscores the 
importance of independent test-set validation for complex models 
applied to limited environmental datasets, a point emphasized in recent 
guidance on secure and accurate modelling of anaerobic membrane 
bioreactors. The combined use of predicted-versus-observed plots and 
residual analysis offers a comprehensive assessment of both accuracy 
and reliability.
Additionally, we evaluated extrapolation under unseen operating 
conditions, the chronological data splitting was applied. Data from 
Phases I–II were used for model training, while Phase III data served as 
an independent test set. After data cleaning and alignment, the training 
set contained 103 samples and the Phase III test set contained 16 sam­
ples. This test was used only to assess extrapolation under shock loading 
and was reported separately from the main random 80:20 split results. 
The Phase III shock-loading extrapolation test showed a marked decline 
in model performance compared with the random 80:20 split (Table S3). 
Effluent COD and TMP prediction by RF, ANN, and XGBoost produced 
Phase III R² negative values, indicating that the models could not reli­
ably extrapolate to unseen shock-loading conditions and performed

3.5. SHAP feature importance analysis
Since RF provided the strongest independent test-set performance for 
both effluent COD and TMP, SHAP analysis was applied to the final RF 
models to explain model-level predictive associations [19]. The mean 
absolute SHAP values are shown in Fig. S5, while the SHAP summary 
plots are presented in Fig. 5. For Effluent COD prediction, Fig. 5a, the 
important features in the prediction were ranked in the following order 
of importance: OLR > HRT > ORP > influent NH₄⁺-N > influent COD 
> influent pH. OLR showed the largest contribution to the model output, 
with high OLR values generally associated with positive SHAP values 
and higher predicted Effluent COD. Low OLR values mainly contributed 
negative SHAP values, indicating lower predicted Effluent COD. This 
pattern is consistent with the operational role of organic loading, where 
higher loading can increase the treatment burden on the reactor and 
reduce COD removal efficiency. HRT showed an opposite tendency, 
where longer HRT values were generally associated with negative SHAP 
values and lower predicted COD, consistent with longer contact time 
and improved biodegradation [20]. ORP also contributed to COD pre­
diction, with more reducing conditions generally associated with lower 
predicted COD, reflecting a more favourable anaerobic environment for 
organic matter degradation. Since OLR, HRT and influent COD are 
operationally related, these SHAP rankings were interpreted as 
model-learned associations rather than independent causal effects.
For TMP prediction, SHAP interpretation of the RF model also 
indicated a distinct hierarchy of feature importance (Fig. 5b). The var­
iables were ordered as follows: effluent NH4
+-N > effluent pH > ORP 
> influent pH > influent COD > influent NH4
+-N. The effluent NH4
+-N 
values were generally associated with positive SHAP values, indicating 
higher predicted TMP, while lower effluent NH4
+-N values contributed to 
lower predicted TMP. This association was interpreted cautiously as a 
diagnostic signal of the reactor’s biological and chemical state rather 
than direct evidence that NH4
+-N caused fouling. Ammonia and free 
ammonia have been reported to inhibit anaerobic microbial activity and 
affect sludge properties. Free-ammonia pretreatment can promote 
sludge solubilization and hydrolysis [32], while free-ammonia concen­
tration affects EPS production and sludge physicochemical properties 
[33]. In nitrogen-removal MBR systems, EPS and microbial metabolites, 
including proteins, polysaccharides and humic substances, contributed 
to more severe membrane fouling [34]. It is also reported that EPS 
composition and microbial community shifts were closely related to 
nitrogen-removal stability and sludge aggregation [35].
Effluent pH and ORP also contributed to TMP prediction, suggesting 
that acid-base conditions and redox status were associated with the 
fouling state captured by the model. pH reflects several indirect but 
operationally relevant fouling-related processes in high-strength 
AnMBR systems, including free ammonia equilibrium that may inhibit 
methanogenic activity and influence the release of SMP, surface charge/ 
gelation propensity of EPS that affect cake-layer compactness and 
filtration resistance, and inorganic precipitation that participate in 
composite organic-inorganic fouling layers. Since effluent NH4
+-N and 
effluent pH are downstream or synchronously measured variables, the 
TMP model should be interpreted as a diagnostic soft-sensing model 
rather than a strict forward-looking early-warning model. The SHAP 
results therefore provide interpretable associations between routinely 
monitored process variables and TMP behaviour, but they do not 
establish independent mechanistic causality.
While the experimental and ML based predictions yield encouraging 
results, several considerations warrant mention regarding the scope of 
this study. The models were developed using data from a single 
laboratory-scale reactor (4 L working volume) treating leachate from 
one transfer station thus, direct extrapolation to full-scale systems or 
different leachate compositions should be undertaken with caution. The 
120-day operational period, although informative, covers a narrower 
range of conditions than typical long-term industrial applications. 
Furthermore, the SHAP analysis is confined to the available input vari­
ables and does not encompass other biologically or physicochemical 
relevant parameters that may influence process behaviour. Another 
limitation is the presence of collinearity especially OLR, HRT and 
influent COD. This may affect the interpretation of feature importance 
therefore, SHAP results were used to support model interpretation 
rather than to establish causal mechanisms. Additionally, the current 
models predict effluent COD and TMP without directly incorporating 
microbial community dynamics or fouling composition data. Future 
efforts should prioritize data collection from diverse AnMBR configu­
rations to enhance model generalizability and enable transfer learning. 
Integrating multi-frequency data streams, online monitoring, and bio­
logical indicators would further improve the capacity to predict rapid 
performance transitions and membrane fouling characteristics.
4. Conclusion
This study integrated experimental analysis with ML modelling to 
elucidate organic matter transformation and membrane fouling 
behavior in AnMBR systems treating high-strength wastewater (transfer 
station landfill leachate). The results demonstrated that operational 
parameters, particularly HRT and OLR, strongly influenced COD 
removal efficiency (>80% across all phases), while elevated loading 
periods triggered rapid TMP increases from 2 kPa to 32.5 kPa, under­
scoring the acute sensitivity of fouling dynamics to operational distur­
bances. RF achieved the strongest overall predictive performance for
