3.4. Microbial community structure analyses 
To better understand the microbial responses to the stepwise in­
crease salinity in AnMBR, the microbial consortium in the UASB reactor 
at the end of each phase were comprehensively examined (Table 3 and 
Fig. 6). Alpha diversity indices were used to determine the microbial 
community richness (observed species, ACE and Chao 1) and diversity 
(Shannon and Simpson). ACE, Chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices 
reduced dramatically to a minimum in Phase III and then slightly 
recovered in Phase IV. This result indicated that high salinity adversely 
affects the microbial community richness and diversity, but the further 
increase of salinity to 20 g/L can re-establish a more tolerant microbial 
community. Similarly, previous studies also reported that high salinity 
(>20 g/L) could have an unfavorable impact on microbial richness and 
diversity (Chen et al., 2019). Interestingly, Phase II showed slightly 
higher Shannon and Simpson indices than that in Phase I and it was 
speculated that low salinity (5 g/L) may provide a manageable selective 
pressure to promote population diversity.

Hierarchical clustering analysis (heat map) was also performed 
(Fig. 6b) and indicated that the microbial consortium structure in Phase 
I was similar to Phase II, which is significantly different from the mi­
crobial population in Phases III and IV owing to the salinity exposure. 
Higher salinity has a greater adverse effect on bacteria, such as Tricho­
coccus, Aminiphilus, Caldatribacterium and Mesotoga (Diaz et al., 2007; Li 
et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018b), which is related to the hydrolysis and 
acidogenesis. For example, Aminiphilus related to the amino acid 
degradation and its relative abundance reduced from 6.19 to 7.42% to 
undetectable as the salinity rose to 10–20 g/L in Phase III and Phase IV 
(Diaz et al., 2007). Mesotoga was responsible for the complex carbohy­
drate degradation and the reduced relative abundance at high salinity 
stress might explain the reduced COD removal efficiency (Li et al., 
2022). Additionally, the relative abundance of methanogenic archaea 
including Methanobrevibacter, Methanosaeta and Methnobacterium 
showed a slight increase from Phase I to Phase II and significantly 
reduced in Phase III and IV. For example, Methanosaeta and Meth­
anobacterium increased from 2.81% to 1.67%–9.22% and 6.14% from 
Phase I to Phase II with the introduction of 5 g/L salinity, but both 
decreased to below 0.1% in the following two phases. This observation 
suggested that low salinity can enrich some methanogens, but high 
salinity (>10 g/L) severely jeopardized the aforementioned metha­
nogens, which is in good agreement with the SMA results. On the con­
trary, Longilinea, Ignavibacterium, Rhodovarius, Bosea and Flexilinea 
become preponderant under high salinity stress. Longilinea is a strict 
anaerobe that degrades carbohydrates to produce volatile fatty acid (Liu 
et al., 2017), which is also salinity tolerate increasing from 0.3% without 
salinity to 22.4% under 10 g/L salinity (Guo et al., 2018). Ignavibacte­
rium and Bosea was reported to be responsible for benzene ring opening 
and azo dye degradation, respectively (Song et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 
2023). Their higher relative abundance in Phase III and IV can be used to 
explain the relatively stable MO removal efficiency. 
To further assess the relationship between environmental factors 
such as salinity and microbial community, principal component analysis 
(PCA) was performed on microbial genera (Fig. 7). Clustered sample 
dots of Phase I and II were observed, indicating similar microbial com­
munity structures in these two phases. The projection of Phase III and IV 
suggests they are positively related to the salinity. It must be pointed out 
that the Phase III dot deviated more from the salinity vector than the 
other sample dots (Phase I, II and IV), which indicated that the microbes 
in Phase III are less sensitive to salinity change. This may suggest that 
Phase III is a transition period from salinity-sensitive microbes to a 
salinity-tolerant community. The relative abundance of bacteria (Ami­
niphilus, Caldatribacterium, Mesotoga) and archaea (Methanobrevibacter, 
Methanobacterium, Methanosaeta) at the genus level showed a negative 
correlation with the salinity. On the other hand, the Longlinea, Ignavi­
bacterium, Rhodovarius, Bosea and Flexilinea at the genus level indicated a 
positive correlation with the salinity. This observation is consistent with 
the specific genera variations under salinity in the previous discussion 
and further confirmed the microbial community transition from salinity- 
sensitive microbes to salinity-tolerant microbes. Interestingly, the vector 
of Ercella overlaps with the salinity vector, which suggests that it may be 
halophilic and highly sensitive to salinity (Li et al., 2021).
