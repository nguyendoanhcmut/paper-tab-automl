3.1. Impact of salinity on AnMBR treatment performance 
Fig. 2 and Table 2 showed both COD and MO removal efficiencies 
with the stepwise increment of NaCl concentration (0, 5, 10 and 20 g/L). 
In Phase I (NaCl 0 g/L), 88.4% of COD removal efficiency was achieved. 
When the AnMBR was first subjected to salt stress (5 g/L), the COD 
removal efficiency rapidly reduced to 80.5% and gradually recovered to 
reach 88.9%. Luo et al. (2016) also observed the initial reduction and 
subsequent increase of the treatment performance of MBR as the influent 
salinity increased. This suggested that the biological performance of 
AnMBR can be restored when the microbial consortium adapts to the salt 
environment and the AnMBR system has a certain shock resistance to 
salinity (Luo et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020a). Further increase of NaCl

96% to less than 78% with the NaCl content increase from 0 to 40 g/L in

pressure difference over the cell wall, disrupted metabolic functions and 
caused the disintegration of cells due to cellular water loss and cyto­
plasm recession, thus resulting in microbial activity loss and cell dehy­
dration (Abou-Elela et al., 2010; He et al., 2017). 
The variations of MO removal efficiency under stepwise salinities 
were different from that of COD removal efficiency (Fig. 2b). The 
average MO removal efficiency in Phase I was over 96%. Although a 
slight decrease was observed during the transition period of salinity 
variations, the overall MO removal efficiency can maintain about 
90–96% even if the salinity increased to 20 g/L. Previous studies also 
indicated that although COD removal efficiencies were significantly 
inhibited, dye removal was not affected (Dafale et al., 2008; Is¸ik, 2004). 
This result indicated that salinity has a limited effect on MO removal, 
which can be ascribed to the enrichment of several genera related to 
aromatic compound degradation such as Acidovorax, Bosea, and Long­
ilinea at higher salinity (Zhu et al., 2018). Additionally, the azo dye 
decolorization was closely related to the enzymes including lignin 
peroxidase, azo-reductase and laccase, and their activities were only 
inhibited when the salinity reached 15% and 20% (corresponding to 
150–200 g/L) (Guo et al., 2021). 
To assess the impact of salinity on biogas production, the modified 
SMA tests under different salinities were conducted (Table 2, Fig. S1). In 
Phase I, the SMA of anaerobic sludge was 0.046 g COD g VSS−1 d−1, 
followed by an increase to 0.077 g COD g VSS−1 d−1 in Phase II. Further 
increase of the salinity to 10 and 20 g/L can remarkably reduce the SMA 
to 0.042 and 0.010 g COD g VSS−1 d−1, respectively. This result sug­
gested that low NaCl concentration boosted the biogas production whilst 
high NaCl concentration had an inhibiting effect. Zhang et al. (2020b) 
also reported that biogas production from anaerobic digestion (AD) for 
kitchen waste treatment increased from 4.25 L to 5.28 L when the 
salinity increased from 1.0 to 2.0 g/L and decreased as the salinity 
further increased. The initial biogas production increase under low 
salinity indicated that methanogens could potentially adapted to salinity 
environments after acclimation (Zhang et al., 2020b). The decrease of 
methane production under high salinity can be ascribed to the con­
sumption of substrate for anaerobic microbes to generate compatible 
solutes to confront the high salinity, and low enzymatic activities 
because of cell intracellular water loss owing to the high osmotic pres­
sure (Aslan and S¸ekerda˘g, 2016; Oh et al., 2008). 
Overall, low salinity (5 g/L) may have a limited effect on organic 
degradation and biogas production due to the anaerobic biomass accli­
mation to salinity. High salinity (>10 g/L) showed a significantly 
negative effect on the COD removal and biogas production mainly due to 
the reduced microbial activity owing to the high osmosis pressure and 
cell intracellular water loss. In terms of the MO, salinity had a marginal 
effect on MO removal in AnMBR, which can be due to the enrichment of 
specific microbes and high salinity tolerance of the color degradation- 
related enzymes. Some researchers have investigated the addition of 
inorganic ions (such as K+, Ca2+ and Mg2+) or compatible solutes (such 
as glycine betaine) to balance the high osmotic pressure (Li et al., 2018b; 
Zhang et al., 2016). Li et al. (2018b) evaluated the impact of KCl addi­
tion on the anaerobic treatment of salinity wastewater with 2.0% NaCl 
and results indicated that KCl addition can significantly increase the 
COD removal efficiency by 115.4%. Zhang et al. (2016) reported that 
glycine betaine addition can effectively mitigate the high salinity effect 
on the AD process, especially for acetic acid producers, acetoclastic 
methanogens and n-butyrate utilizers. Therefore, the addition of inor­
ganic ions or compatible solutes seems to be feasible for AnMBR treat­
ment performance but further studies are still warranted focusing on 
their effects on the membrane fouling. Additionally, the operating 
conditions such as SRT and HRT should also be optimized to confront the 
salinity in AnMBR. In the future, the AnMBR with ceramic membrane 
has the potential to be applied to treat textile wastewater with various
