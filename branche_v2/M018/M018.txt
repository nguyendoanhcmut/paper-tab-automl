A B S T R A C T  
Anaerobic membrane bioreactor (AnMBR) shows great potential for textile wastewater treatment, but high 
salinity in the influent may undermine its performance. This study evaluated the impact of salinity on the 
treatment performance of an upflow anaerobic sludge blanket (UASB) configured AnMBR using a flat sheet 
ceramic membrane. The salinity was stepwise increased (0, 5, 10 and 20 g/L) in four phases of the AnMBR 
operation. Results indicated that increased salinity jeopardized the COD removal efficiency of AnMBR from 92% 
to 73%, but had a marginal effect on dye removal efficacy (90–96%). Low salinity (5 g/L) boosted the biogas 
production whilst high salinity (>10 g/L) had a negative impact. Additionally, the increase of salinity resulted in 
the soluble microbial production (SMP) concentration soar and membrane fouling rate increase, peaking at a 
salinity of 10 g/L (Phase III) and recovering back to a lower level at a salinity of 20 g/L (Phase IV). This indicated 
a transition occurrence at a salinity of 10 g/L (Phase III). The microbial diversity analyses further suggested a 
transition from salinity-sensitive microbes (Aminiphilus, Caldatribacterium, Mesotoga, Methanobrevibacter, Meth­
anobacterium, Methanosaeta) to salinity-tolerant microbes (Longilinea, Ignavibacterium, Rhodovarius, Bosea and 
Flexilinea). This transition can be associated with the increase SMP concentration and more severe membrane 
fouling in Phase III, which were mitigated after a new equilibrium was reached when the microbial consortium 
acclimatized to the high salinity. Finally, a machine learning model of the Adaboost algorithm was established to 
predict COD removal under different salinities. Importantly, this study revealed that AnMBR process perfor­
mance and membrane operation can be maintained for high salinity textile wastewater treatment with a halo­
philic microbial community growth under high-salinity selection pressure.   
1. Introduction 
The textile industry has been growing extensively and is becoming an 
important contributor to the national economic development of many 
countries (Carvalho et al., 2020; Li et al., 2018a). Additionally, the 
textile industry tends to consume a large quantity of water and over 90% 
of the water consumption becomes wastewater containing a high con­
centration of salinity with 5–6% NaCl, color, solids, chemical oxygen 
demand (COD) and heavy metals (Lin et al., 2015; Sari Erkan et al., 
2020). The discharge of effluent from the textile industry into natural 
water bodies posed a great potential hazard to aquatic organisms and 
public health because of its high toxicity and mutagenicity (Ozmen 
et al., 2008). Textile wastewater treatment can be treated by aerobic and 
anaerobic

Aivasidis, 2006; Yu et al., 2015).

biological treatment process with membrane separation technology,

exhibiting advantages of high color removal, energy saving, biogas re­
covery and low sludge production in textile wastewater treatment 
(Moideen et al., 2023; Sari Erkan et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020). 
Yurtsever et al. (2015) studied the AnMBR performance for synthetic 
textile wastewater and indicated that complete color removal and about 
94% COD removal can be achieved. Berkessa et al. (2020) evaluated the 
feasibility of using an anaerobic dynamic membrane bioreactor for 
anthraquinone dye textile wastewater treatment and indicated that 
98.5% of soluble COD and over 97.5% of color can be removed. How­
ever, textile wastewater not only contains dyes but also several auxiliary 
chemicals and inhibitory compounds which may affect the treatment 
performance (Yurtsever et al., 2020). Yurtsever et al. (2020) investi­
gated the treatment performance of AnMBR for real textile wastewater 
treatment and only 84% COD removal efficiency can be obtained. 
The high salinity in textile wastewater would also inhibit the treat­
ment performance which can be ascribed to the negative effect on the 
metabolic activity of microorganisms thus reducing the pollutants 
degradation efficiency (Chen et al., 2019). Chen et al. (2019) evaluated 
the impact of salinity (NaCl 0–40 g/L) on AnMBR and suggested that 
COD removal efficiency was inhibited from 96.4% to 77.7%. The high 
salinity not only affect the treatment performance but also leads to more 
severe membrane fouling, which might be due to the increase of soluble 
microbial products (SMP) content under high salt stress (Yurtsever et al., 
2016). However, previous studies which evaluated the impact of salinity 
on AnMBR treatment performance mainly focused on the continuous 
stirred tank reactor (CSTR) configuration (Chen et al., 2019; Mu˜noz 
Sierra et al., 2018, 2019; Yurtsever et al., 2020). The upflow anaerobic 
sludge blanket (UASB) configured AnMBR appears to mitigate mem­
brane fouling compared with AnMBR in CSTR configuration due to the 
less fouling layer deposition (Liao et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2018a). 
Furthermore, UASB as a typical high-rate anaerobic reactor is commonly 
used for textile wastewater treatment and the usage of UASB configured 
AnMBR is conducive to the refurbishment of existing treatment plants 
(Liao et al., 2006). However, limited study was conducted in terms of the 
impact of salinity on UASB configured AnMBR for textile wastewater 
treatment. 
The biological process in the AnMBR is complicated and treatment 
performance is influenced by many factors (Huang et al., 2018). How­
ever, the relationship between the variables and pollutant removal has 
not been deeply clarified (Giri et al., 2011; Huang et al., 2018). Machine 
learning (ML) is a classification and regression technique based on past 
experience or data, which is widely used in wastewater treatment 
(Yaqub and Lee, 2022; Ye et al., 2020). Several models have been 
established to predict the AnMBR (Benyahia et al., 2013; Huang et al., 
2018). Huang et al. (2018) used a back propagation neural network 
(BPNN) model to predict the AnMBR treatment performance (COD and 
antibiotics removal) for pharmaceutical wastewater treatment. How­
ever, limited studies considered to include the impact of salinity in the 
AnMBR treatment performance prediction models. Adaboost, decision 
trees and random forests are typical machine learning algorithms. 
Adaboost takes multiple weak classifiers and combines them into a 
strong classifier (Freund and Schapire, 1997), which has a strong clas­
sification performance and is good at predicting pollutant concentration 
(Huang et al., 2023). Additionally, compared with decision trees and 
random forests, adaboost tends to have higher accuracy with less 
occurrence of overfitting, resulting in more realistic predictions (Chan 
and Paelinckx, 2008; Miao and Heaton, 2010). 
Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the impact of salinity on UASB 
configured AnMBR using ceramic membrane for synthetic textile 
wastewater treatment. The impact of stepwise increase of salinity (0–20 
g/L NaCl) on decolorization, organic degradation, anaerobic process 
and membrane fouling of an UASB configured ceramic AnMBR for 
textile wastewater treatment was evaluated. Then, the microbial com­
munity structure was analyzed to investigate the variations of microbial 
population under different salt stress. Finally, the effluent COD predic­
tion model based on Adaboost algorithm as a machine learning model 
was established to build the relationship between effluent COD and 
environmental factors in AnMBR. 
2. Materials and methods 
2.1. UASB-configured AnMBR setup and operational conditions 
The AnMBR was composed of a 6.4 L UASB reactor (0.045 m 
diameter × 0.6 m height) and an external membrane tank (Fig. 1). The 
UASB reactor was inoculated with 1.6 L anaerobic flocculent sludge (46 
g TS L−1, 26 g VS L−1) obtained locally from an anaerobic digester for 
municipal sludge treatment (Ningbo, Zhejiang, China). Synthetic textile 
wastewater containing 1000 mg/L glucose, 100 mg/L of methyl orange 
and some trace elements (Yurtsever et al., 2016) was pumped into the 
UASB reactor using a peristaltic pump (YZ1515X, Longer, Baoding, 
China). The amount of sodium chloride (NaCl) varied in each phase. The 
hydraulic retention time (HRT) and solids retention time (SRT) of the 
AnMBR was about 21 h and about 100 d, respectively. The reactor 
temperature was kept by water bathing at 35 ± 1 ◦C. The biogas was 
monitored using a gas flow meter (LML-1, Senlod, Nanjing, China). 
UASB effluent overflowed to a 5.3 L membrane tank (0.3 × 0.1 × 0.2 
m) and the retentate was circulated to the UASB reactor to maintain the 
upflow velocity at 0.8–1.0 m/h. A flat sheet Al2O3 ceramic membrane 
(0.1 μm, 0.065 m2, Tianjian Water Company, Hangzhou, China) was 
used. Nitrogen gas was sparged as an alternative to biogas for fouling 
control due to the health and safety regulation in the laboratory. The 
nitrogen gas was produced from a nitrogen generator (TN-10, Tainuo, 
Shandong, China). A flow meter (LZB-6WB, Shengzhiyuan, Changzhou, 
China) was utilized to regulate the nitrogen flow rate. The specific gas 
demand per unit membrane area (SGDm) was maintained as 2.0 m3 m−2 
h−1. Future studies about the impact of biogas compositions on the bulk 
sludge characteristics and membrane fouling would be warranted. The 
membrane flux was maintained at 5 L m−2 h−1 with 9 min filtration and 
1 min relaxation. Transmembrane pressure (TMP) data was collected by 
a pressure gauge (−0.1–0.15 MPa, Yichuan, Shanghai, China) and 
recorded in a data logger. Membrane fouling rate with dTMP/dt values 
in slow and sudden TMP increase stages were also calculated (Yoon, 
2015). 
The sequence of the experiment and a summary of the conditions at 
each phase were presented in Table 1. The experimental trial can be 
separated into four phases according to different influent NaCl contents 
(0 g/L, 5 g/L, 10 g/L, 20 g/L) and each phase lasted for about 20–30 d. 
The AnMBR effluent was sampled to measure COD and azo dye, and 
membrane tank mixed liquor was taken for mixed liquor suspended 
solids (MLSS) and SMP concentrations determination at a regular in­
terval of 2–3 days. When the membrane was fouled with TMP of 30 kPa, 
the membrane module was first physically cleaned and then immersed 
in 0.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaClO) for 24 h. 
2.2. Microbial community analysis 
High-throughput sequencing technology was applied to analyze the 
microbial population of anaerobic flocculent sludge from the UASB 
reactor after each phase. The collected samples were saved at −20 ◦C 
before DNA extraction, which was done with the MagBind® Soil DNA 
Kit (Omega, USA). The universal primers of 515FmodF and 806RmodR 
were utilized for the amplification of the microbial 16 S rDNA gene 
sequence for both bacteria and archaea. The microbial diversity was 
analyzed by Shanghai Majorbio Biopharm Technology Co., Ltd 
(Shanghai, China).

2.4. Analytical methods 
Mixed liquor suspended solids (MLSS) and COD were determined in 
accordance with the Standard Methods (2017). In order to remove the 
interference of excessive chloride ions on COD determination, the 
effluent water was diluted by 10–20 times and then measured. The 
concentration of methyl orange (MO) was measured by ultra-violet 
spectrophotometer (Persee T6, Beijing, China) at 464 nm (Liu et al., 
2018a). The specific methanogenic activity (SMA) of the UASB sludge at 
the end of each phase was conducted in order to evaluate the impact of 
salinity on the methane production (Ozgun et al., 2015; van Loosdrecht 
et al., 2016). Acetate was used as the substrate with COD of 2 g/L and 
the VSSinoculum and CODsubstrate ratio was set at 2. In order to maintain 
the consistent salt inhibition level, the SMA test was modified by adding 
the same NaCl concentration in the anaerobic serum bottles as the 
reactor operational phase. 
The membrane tank mixture was filtered via 0.45 μm membrane for 
the SMP concentration determination. The modified Lowry’s method 
(Lowry et al., 1951) and phenol-sulfuric acid method (Dubois et al., 
1956) were introduced for the protein and polysaccharide determina­
tion. The membrane tank mixture was also divided into different particle 
sizes (1.2 μm, 0.45 μm, 100 kDa, 10 kDa, 1 kDa) using ultrafiltration 
cups (MSC300/MSC050, Shanghai, China) to determine the SMP mo­
lecular weight distribution. Luminescence spectrometry (Horiba 
FluoroMax-4, Kyoto, Japan) was used to measure all the EEM fluores­
cence spectra of the membrane tank mixture. 
3. Results and discussion 
3.1. Impact of salinity on AnMBR treatment performance 
Fig. 2 and Table 2 showed both COD and MO removal efficiencies 
with the stepwise increment of NaCl concentration (0, 5, 10 and 20 g/L). 
In Phase I (NaCl 0 g/L), 88.4% of COD removal efficiency was achieved. 
When the AnMBR was first subjected to salt stress (5 g/L), the COD 
removal efficiency rapidly reduced to 80.5% and gradually recovered to 
reach 88.9%. Luo et al. (2016) also observed the initial reduction and 
subsequent increase of the treatment performance of MBR as the influent 
salinity increased. This suggested that the biological performance of 
AnMBR can be restored when the microbial consortium adapts to the salt 
environment and the AnMBR system has a certain shock resistance to 
salinity (Luo et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2020a). Further increase of NaCl

COD removal rate to 85.2% and 78.3%, respectively. Chen et al. (2019) 
also reported that COD removal was significantly inhibited from over 
96% to less than 78% with the NaCl content increase from 0 to 40 g/L in 
AnMBR. Aslan and S¸ekerda˘g (2015) also reported that salinity has an 
adverse effect on UASB treatment and COD removal efficiency signifi­

of 10 g/L and further decreased to 20–30% with a salt concentration of 
50 g/L. This might be because high salinity increased the osmotic 
pressure difference over the cell wall, disrupted metabolic functions and 
caused the disintegration of cells due to cellular water loss and cyto­
plasm recession, thus resulting in microbial activity loss and cell dehy­
dration (Abou-Elela et al., 2010; He et al., 2017). 
The variations of MO removal efficiency under stepwise salinities 
were different from that of COD removal efficiency (Fig. 2b). The 
average MO removal efficiency in Phase I was over 96%. Although a 
slight decrease was observed during the transition period of salinity 
variations, the overall MO removal efficiency can maintain about 
90–96% even if the salinity increased to 20 g/L. Previous studies also 
indicated that although COD removal efficiencies were significantly 
inhibited, dye removal was not affected (Dafale et al., 2008; Is¸ik, 2004). 
This result indicated that salinity has a limited effect on MO removal, 
which can be ascribed to the enrichment of several genera related to 
aromatic compound degradation such as Acidovorax, Bosea, and Long­
ilinea at higher salinity (Zhu et al., 2018). Additionally, the azo dye 
decolorization was closely related to the enzymes including lignin 
peroxidase, azo-reductase and laccase, and their activities were only 
inhibited when the salinity reached 15% and 20% (corresponding to 
150–200 g/L) (Guo et al., 2021). 
To assess the impact of salinity on biogas production, the modified 
SMA tests under different salinities were conducted (Table 2, Fig. S1). In 
Phase I, the SMA of anaerobic sludge was 0.046 g COD g VSS−1 d−1, 
followed by an increase to 0.077 g COD g VSS−1 d−1 in Phase II. Further 
increase of the salinity to 10 and 20 g/L can remarkably reduce the SMA 
to 0.042 and 0.010 g COD g VSS−1 d−1, respectively. This result sug­
gested that low NaCl concentration boosted the biogas production whilst 
high NaCl concentration had an inhibiting effect. Zhang et al. (2020b) 
also reported that biogas production from anaerobic digestion (AD) for 
kitchen waste treatment increased from 4.25 L to 5.28 L when the 
salinity increased from 1.0 to 2.0 g/L and decreased as the salinity 
further increased. The initial biogas production increase under low 
salinity indicated that methanogens could potentially adapted to salinity 
environments after acclimation (Zhang et al., 2020b). The decrease of 
methane production under high salinity can be ascribed to the con­
sumption of substrate for anaerobic microbes to generate compatible 
solutes to confront the high salinity, and low enzymatic activities 
because of cell intracellular water loss owing to the high osmotic pres­
sure (Aslan and S¸ekerda˘g, 2016; Oh et al., 2008). 
Overall, low salinity (5 g/L) may have a limited effect on organic 
degradation and biogas production due to the anaerobic biomass accli­
mation to salinity. High salinity (>10 g/L) showed a significantly 
negative effect on the COD removal and biogas production mainly due to 
the reduced microbial activity owing to the high osmosis pressure and 
cell intracellular water loss. In terms of the MO, salinity had a marginal 
effect on MO removal in AnMBR, which can be due to the enrichment of 
specific microbes and high salinity tolerance of the color degradation- 
related enzymes. Some researchers have investigated the addition of 
inorganic ions (such as K+, Ca2+ and Mg2+) or compatible solutes (such 
as glycine betaine) to balance the high osmotic pressure (Li et al., 2018b; 
Zhang et al., 2016). Li et al. (2018b) evaluated the impact of KCl addi­
tion on the anaerobic treatment of salinity wastewater with 2.0% NaCl 
and results indicated that KCl addition can significantly increase the 
COD removal efficiency by 115.4%. Zhang et al. (2016) reported that 
glycine betaine addition can effectively mitigate the high salinity effect 
on the AD process, especially for acetic acid producers, acetoclastic 
methanogens and n-butyrate utilizers. Therefore, the addition of inor­
ganic ions or compatible solutes seems to be feasible for AnMBR treat­
ment performance but further studies are still warranted focusing on 
their effects on the membrane fouling. Additionally, the operating 
conditions such as SRT and HRT should also be optimized to confront the 
salinity in AnMBR. In the future, the AnMBR with ceramic membrane 
has the potential to be applied to treat textile wastewater with various

3.2. Impact of salinity on AnMBR fouling profiles 
TMP profiles were monitored to characterize the ceramic membrane 
fouling under different salinity stress (Fig. 3). During Phase I and Phase 
II, a relatively similar membrane fouling profile was observed with 
operational time about 6.9–13.5 d. The dTMP/dt in slow and sudden 
TMP increase were 0.67 and 11.8 kPa/d, respectively. Further increase 
the salinity to 10 g/L in Phase III resulted in the fastest membrane 
fouling rate, showing dTMP/dt in slow and sudden TMP increase of 2.0 
and 24.4 kPa/d. Interestingly, as the salinity reached 20 g/L in Phase IV, 
the membrane fouling cycle was prolonged to 9.3–10.7 d with a much 
lower membrane fouling rate (dTMP/dt) of 0.36 kPa/d in the slow TMP 
increase stage. The highest TMP increase occurred in Phase III with 10 
g/L NaCl can be ascribed to the significant SMP secretion from microbes 
to confront the high salinity (Aquino and Stuckey, 2004). The TMP 
profile also indicated that Phase III was during a transition stage, which 
might be due to the transformation of the microbial community struc­
ture from salinity sensitive to salt-tolerant microbes under salinity se­
lective pressure (Luo et al., 2016) and the change of the membrane tank 
mixture characteristics especially the SMP content and SMP molecular 
weight distribution (See details in Section 3.3 and 3.4). 
3.3. Impact of salinity on membrane tank mixture characteristics 
3.3.1. SMP variation and molecular weight distribution 
Membrane tank mixtures under different salinity conditions were 
characterized by SMP concentration and SMP molecular weight distri­
bution. The SMP concentration was between 93 and 179 mg/L under 
different NaCl concentrations (0–20 g/L), in which SMP protein (SMPp) 
concentration was 80–160 mg/L whilst SMP carbohydrates (SMPc) 
content was only 0–20 mg/L (Fig. 4a). In Phase I, the average SMP 
concentration in the membrane tank mixture was about 116 mg/L. After 
introducing salt stress of 5 g/L NaCl at the beginning of Phase II, the SMP 
concentration spiked abruptly to 162 mg/L and then gradually declined 
and stabilized at about 119 mg/L. The initial increase of SMP in Phase I 
and Phase II can be attributed to the introduction of organic shock 
loading and salinity, leading to SMP secretion to confront unfavorable 
conditions (Aquino and Stuckey, 2004). The following stabilization 
periods suggested that the microbial community acclimatized to the 
environment and slowed down the SMP secretion (Chen et al., 2019). 
Further increasing the NaCl concentration to 10 g/L in Phase III led to 
the SMP increase to a peak at 178 mg/L. This may be mainly attributed 
to cell lysis during a major shift of the microbial community structure 
under salt stress (Chen et al., 2019), since some microorganisms in the 
UASB reactor could only survive at the condition with <10 g/L NaCl (C. 
R.Woolard and R.L.Irvine, 1995; Lay et al., 2010). An subsequent 
gradual decline of SMP content at the end of Phase III was also observed, 
which may be explained by the re-establishment of a more tolerant 
microbial community to high salinity. As the salinity reached 20 g/L in 
Phase IV, SMP concentration decreased and was maintained at an 
average concentration of 133 mg/L, which is comparable to that in 
Phase I and II. The SMP fluctuation is in good agreement with the TMP 
profile results (Fig. 3) and suggested that a transition period of AnMBR 
occurred during Phase III. 
The SMP molecular weight distribution was also investigated 
(Fig. 4b). The macromolecules (>100 kDa) of SMP occupied the ma­
jority of the SMP content (60–80%), which is vital in foulants deposition 
(Deng et al., 2016). Similarly, Liu et al. (2018b) also reported that 
macromolecular foulants were the major components of the total or­
ganics. Additionally, the SMP molecular fraction (100 kDa–0.45 μm) 
including the physical size range of the membrane pore size (0.1 μm) 
showed the highest (10.0%) proportion in Phase III. This may be the key 
to more severe fouling in Phase III as this molecular fraction of SMP is a 
great membrane fouling contributor due to its high pore-clogging po­
tential (De Temmerman et al., 2014). 
3.3.2. Three-dimensional excitation–emission matrix (3D-EEM) 
To further investigate the SMP composition in the membrane tank 
mixture, the 3D-EEM analyses were conducted (Fig. 5). Peak A, Peak B 
and Peak C located in Region IV (Ex/Em = 250–380/380–540 nm), 
Region V (Ex/Em = 250–335/280–380 nm) and Region II (Ex/Em =
200–255/335–380 nm), belonged to dissolved microbial by-products, 
humic acid-like substances, and tryptophan protein-like substances 
respectively. It is clear to see that Peak A, representing microbial by- 
products, was dominant in all samples. With the salinity stepwise rose 
from 0 to 10 g/L (Phase I to Phase III), the fluorescence intensity of Peak 
A (dissolved microbial by-products) and Peak B (humic acid-like sub­
stances) continuously increased, but followed by a decreasing trend in 
Phase IV. This result is consistent with the SMP concentration result and 
might be due to the secretion from microbes under salt stress when 
salinity was below 10 g/L, and the re-establishment of a more tolerant 
microbial community to high salinity when salinity reached 20 g/L. The 
humic acid-like substances production under salt stress can be ascribed 
to the decomposition of dead cells and the breakdown of large molecular 
organic substances, e.g. proteins and polysaccharides (Qu et al., 2012). 
3.4. Microbial community structure analyses 
To better understand the microbial responses to the stepwise in­
crease salinity in AnMBR, the microbial consortium in the UASB reactor 
at the end of each phase were comprehensively examined (Table 3 and 
Fig. 6). Alpha diversity indices were used to determine the microbial 
community richness (observed species, ACE and Chao 1) and diversity 
(Shannon and Simpson). ACE, Chao1, Shannon and Simpson indices 
reduced dramatically to a minimum in Phase III and then slightly 
recovered in Phase IV. This result indicated that high salinity adversely 
affects the microbial community richness and diversity, but the further 
increase of salinity to 20 g/L can re-establish a more tolerant microbial 
community. Similarly, previous studies also reported that high salinity

decreased to below 0.1% in the following two phases. This observation 
suggested that low salinity can enrich some methanogens, but high 
salinity (>10 g/L) severely jeopardized the aforementioned metha­

trary, Longilinea, Ignavibacterium, Rhodovarius, Bosea and Flexilinea 
become preponderant under high salinity stress. Longilinea is a strict 
anaerobe that degrades carbohydrates to produce volatile fatty acid (Liu 
et al., 2017), which is also salinity tolerate increasing from 0.3% without 
salinity to 22.4% under 10 g/L salinity (Guo et al., 2018). Ignavibacte­
rium and Bosea was reported to be responsible for benzene ring opening 
and azo dye degradation, respectively (Song et al., 2021; Yuan et al., 
2023). Their higher relative abundance in Phase III and IV can be used to 
explain the relatively stable MO removal efficiency. 
To further assess the relationship between environmental factors 
such as salinity and microbial community, principal component analysis 
(PCA) was performed on microbial genera (Fig. 7). Clustered sample 
dots of Phase I and II were observed, indicating similar microbial com­
munity structures in these two phases. The projection of Phase III and IV 
suggests they are positively related to the salinity. It must be pointed out 
that the Phase III dot deviated more from the salinity vector than the 
other sample dots (Phase I, II and IV), which indicated that the microbes 
in Phase III are less sensitive to salinity change. This may suggest that 
Phase III is a transition period from salinity-sensitive microbes to a 
salinity-tolerant community. The relative abundance of bacteria (Ami­
niphilus, Caldatribacterium, Mesotoga) and archaea (Methanobrevibacter, 
Methanobacterium, Methanosaeta) at the genus level showed a negative 
correlation with the salinity. On the other hand, the Longlinea, Ignavi­
bacterium, Rhodovarius, Bosea and Flexilinea at the genus level indicated a 
positive correlation with the salinity. This observation is consistent with 
the specific genera variations under salinity in the previous discussion 
and further confirmed the microbial community transition from salinity- 
sensitive microbes to salinity-tolerant microbes. Interestingly, the vector 
of Ercella overlaps with the salinity vector, which suggests that it may be 
halophilic and highly sensitive to salinity (Li et al., 2021).

square error (RMSE) objective function. The trained model was regres­
sion simulated on the test set (30%) for the effluent COD analysis. The R2 
on the test set reached 0.9051, and the RMSE was 9.88 mg/L. Except for

4. Conclusion 
This study explored the impact of the stepwise increase of salinity on 
UASB-configured ceramic AnMBR treatment performance of textile 
wastewater. The following conclusions can be summarized:  
● Stepwise increase of salinity inhibited the COD removal from 92% to 
73%, but had a marginal effect on the MO removal (90–96%) in 
AnMBR.  
● Low salinity (5 g/L) boosted the biogas production whilst high 
salinity (>10 g/L) inhibited the process.  
● The increase of salinity resulted in SMP concentration soar and 
membrane fouling rate increase, peaking at a salinity of 10 g/L 
(Phase III) and recovering back to a lower level.  
● The microbial diversity analyses indicated a clear transition from 
salinity-sensitive microbes to a salinity-tolerant community, leading 
to the increased SMP concentration and more severe membrane 
fouling in Phase III and were mitigated after a new equilibrium was 
reached when the microbial community acclimatized to the high 
salinity  
● The Adaboost model was successfully established as the prediction 
model of effluent COD under different salinities, but more data is 
expected to improve the prediction accuracy.
