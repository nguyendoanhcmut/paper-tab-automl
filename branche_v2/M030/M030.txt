A B S T R A C T
Membrane bioreactors (MBR) have been used to treat oil refinery wastewater. However, to allow water reuse, 
ammonia concentration must be sufficiently low. Understanding and controlling its removal is thus essential and 
artificial intelligence modelling is an interesting alternative. Herein, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) and 
Principal Components Analysis (PCA) were applied to identify the variables that contribute the most for 
ammonia removal and to predict its behavior, whereas Multivariate Statistical Process Control (MSPC) was used 
to detect and diagnose low removal conditions. Monitoring data from a pilot-scale MBR treating real oil refinery 
effluent during four years was used. ANN and PCA models identified that membrane permeability and influent 
COD and oil and grease concentrations contribute to lower ammonia removals, while temperature and sludge 
retention time contribute to greater ones. ANN model also effectively predicted the ammonia removal from a set 
of operating conditions, with R2 equal to 0.868. The MSPC model's Q control chart detected more than 50 % of 
the operations with ammonia removal below 90 % and 100 % of the operations with removals lower than 85 %, 
facilitating more targeted interventions. Therefore, ANN and MSPC are valuable tools for supporting the 
decision-making regarding ammonia removal control, aiding in the understanding, management, prediction, and 
early detection of performance issues in MBR systems.

1. Introduction
Membrane bioreactors (MBR) are currently considered a highly 
efficient technology, as they combine biological treatment with mem­
brane separation and stand out for the high effluent quality achieved. 
Besides, other MBR advantages, like high removals of micro- and 
persistent organic pollutants, small industrial area requirement and low 
sludge production [1], are leading to its increasingly wider application. 
MBR technology has been developed for wastewater treatment for over 
three decades and through these years they have been applied for 
municipal [2] and different industrial wastewater treatment, such as 
textile [3], brewery [4], dairy [5] and oil refinery [6,7], and have been 
demonstrating great performances.
Furthermore, the better management of water resources and the 
reuse of treated water is one of the greatest challenges for nowadays 
industries, including oil refineries, which can be strongly benefited by 
the practice. However, in order to allow water reuse, especially at the 
cooling towers, the effluent ammonia concentration must be sufficiently 
low. In this sense,the application of MBR is a promising alternative, as it 
allows greater ammonia removals due to the longer sludge retention 
times (SRT) and the complete sludge retention achieved in this tech­
nology when compared to conventional activated sludge (CAS) systems 
[8].
Several works demonstrate the promise of MBR to remove ammonia 
from oil refinery wastewater, including many of our research group, 
which has been studying this matter for over ten years. Amaral et al. [9] 
and Alkmim et al. [10] assessed pilot-scale units and achieved average 
ammonia removals of 90 % and 92 %, respectively. Moser et al. [11] 
investigated different configurations on a lab-scale MBR and achieved 
removals greater than 95 %. Despite these great results though, the 
large-scale application of MBR focused on ammonia removal still faces 
several challenges, as highlighted by Mao et al. in their review paper 
[12]. Moreover, ammonia removal on MBR systems can be really sen­
sitive to operating conditions, which makes its control even harder. Sun 
et al. [13] observed that ammonia removal suddenly dropped from 95 % 
to approximately 54 % due to a sudden increase of influent chemical 
oxygen demand (COD) from 100–400 mgL−1 to 615 mgL−1.
Therefore, understanding and controlling the factors that impact the 
most on ammonia removal on MBR is essential, as well as being able to 
predict it and to early detect reductions in its values [14–16]. Several 
authors have empirically studied the variables that influence the bio­
logical removal of ammonia [17,18]. These are challenging studies, 
especially when dealing with industrial effluents that have a complex 
and variable composition. Artificial intelligence (AI) can highly 
contribute to this matter, since it can successfully realize feature 
extraction and correlation analysis of input and output data, achieving 
more efficient pattern classification or logical regression tasks than 
traditional methods [19]. The application AI tool offers significant ad­
vantages in optimizing ammonia removal in MBR. By reducing study 
complexity and minimizing human interference, ML can enhance 
operational efficiency, and streamline the study, ultimately saving time 
and reducing costs.
Machine learning (ML) is the AI technology based on mathematical 
and statistical algorithms and can be used to predict the output data by 
finding the relationship or rule of known data. Thus, ML can solve 
complex problems and achieve difficult modelling, for example 
nonlinear distribution processes and multidimensional space distribu­
tion processes [18]. There are several AI techniques that can be applied 
for monitoring MBR performance and the most common models include 
support vector machine (SVM), fuzzy logic (FL), random forest (RF) and 
extreme learning machine (ELM). Chang et al. [19] tested seven ML 
algorithms to model both water flux and salinity of a lab-scale osmotic 
membrane bioreactor (OMBR) and their results have demonstrated the 
promise of ML modelling for investigating OMBR systems. Among many 
ML techniques though, Artificial Neural Networks (ANN) [20], Principal 
Components Analysis (PCA) and Multivariate Statistical Process Control 
(MSPC) stand out for their high performances, particularly in applica­
tions such as wastewater treatment where large amounts of labeled data 
are no always available [21].
ANN are based on the functioning of biological neurons and they 
apply a learning process similar to the human brain's to solve different 
problems [22]. They consist of models formed by process units called, 
arranged in interconnected layers, whose main objective is to develop a 
relationship between input (independent) and output (dependent) var­
iables, which is established from the model learning process from a 
provided dataset [23]. Recently, ANN have become a popular alterna­
tive due to their decreased computational time, high accuracy, and 
ability to non-linearly map relationships between different variables. 
ANN modelling has been applied for classification, pattern recognition, 
clustering and forecasting [24]. Recent works regarding monitoring 
MBR performance through ANN modelling have showed good predic­
tion of real data, despite the complexity of MBR systems. Banerjee et al. 
[25] used ANN to analyze the influence of hydraulic retention time 
(HRT), mixed liquor suspended solid (MLSS) and influent COD on COD 
removal on a MBR treating real tannery wastewater and Kovacs et al. 
[26] applied ANN for membrane fouling prediction on a MBR treating 
municipal wastewater. The authors concluded that ANN models can be 
useful tools in providing decision support, leading to reduced opera­
tional costs.
PCA is extensively applied in many areas of research because it is an 
effective method that reduces a complex dataset to a lower dimension to 
reveal the hidden structures that often underlie it [27]. To extract the 
most relevant information from a dataset, PCA computes new variables 
called principal components (PC) that are obtained as linear combina­
tions of the original ones. This way, it provides an overview of complex 
databases, revealing relations between variables and observations, 
finding and quantifying patterns, and detecting extreme observations, 
among many other features [28]. PCA has been successfully applied as a 
data-driven approach for studying MBR systems. Guti´errez et al. [29] 
investigated the main factors influencing micropollutant removal in an 
MBR coupled with powdered activated carbon (PAC). Qin et al. [30] 
investigated the influence of HRT and organic loading rate (OLR) on 
fungal dynamics during food waste anaerobic digestion in immersed 
MBR. Miwa et al. [31] also used PCA to assess the microbial community 
of a MBR, but focusing on biofilm formation and consequently on 
membrane fouling. Viet and Jang [32] in turn investigated the feasibility 
of applying a novel protocol for constructing a fertilizer draw solution 
index used in OMBR for simultaneous wastewater treatment and sus­
tainable fertigation and PCA showed that fouling resistance played a 
pivotal role in the total variation of the system.
MSPC is a technique that monitors a process performance along time 
through control charts in order to check whether it behaves as it is ex­
pected to do and to detect any unusual event [33]. The multivariate 
control charts retain all the simplicity of presentation and interpretation 
of conventional univariate control charts, but they are much more 
powerful in detecting out-of-control observations since they apply the 
information contained in all measured variables simultaneously. Thus, 
by finding assignable causes for the atypical behavior, improvements in 
the process performance can be achieved by controlling it in a more 
accurate way [34]. The application of MSPC on MBR assessing is still 
scarce in the literature, but based on the results reported for controlling 
the quality of processes in other areas [35,36], its application on MBR 
intending to control ammonia removal is highly promising and should 
be investigated.
Therefore, in this work a pilot-scale MBR treating real oil refinery 
wastewater (so the matrix effects are considered) was assessed through 
the application of ANN and PCA to identify the variables that contribute 
the most for ammonia removal and to predict its behavior, and Multi­
variate Statistical Process Control (MSPC) to detect and diagnose low 
removal conditions. To the best of the author's knowledge, this study this 
research is the first to utilize these tools specifically for ammonia 
removal in an MBR treating refinery wastewater. The MBR was

2. Materials and methods
2.1. Pilot-scale MBR: configuration and performance
The pilot-scale MBR used as a case study in this research was 
installed in a Brazilian oil refinery and it was monitored during four 
years. The unit consists of a biological tank equipped with a submerged 
flat sheet PES (polyether sulfone) membrane module (Kubota) (Fig. 1). 
The oil refinery wastewater was fed to the MBR pilot unit after a series of 
pretreatment steps including water and oil separation, flotation, sand 
filtering and hydrogen peroxide dosing for sulphide concentration 
control. Table 1 shows the characteristics and the operating conditions 
of the unit.
The driving force for permeation was the hydrostatic pressure of the 
water column and the unit had an aeration system to ensure oxygen to 
the biological process requirements and to ensure fouling control by the 
shear stress caused by the ascending flow of air bubbles. The permeate 
flow was maintained by allowing 1 min of relaxation after every 9 min of 
permeation. Regarding chemical cleaning, the membrane was periodi­
cally submitted to a recovery cleaning with a 5000 mg L−1 sodium hy­
pochlorite (NaClO) solution. The chemical cleaning was carried out for 
24 h whenever maintaining the permeate flow was not possible with 
maximum hydrostatic pressure created by the water column within the 
membrane tank.
The MBR performance was assessed by periodically monitoring: i) 
operating conditions — transmembrane pressure (TMP), temperature, 
permeate flowrate, membrane permeability, SRT and pH; ii) sludge 
characteristics — mixed liquor volatile suspended solids (MLVSS), 
MLSS, sludge filterability, sedimentability, extracellular polymeric 
substances (EPS) and soluble microbial products (SMP); and iii) feed and 
effluent characteristics — biological oxygen demand (BOD), COD, total 
organic carbon (TOC), alkalinity, ammonia, chloride, sulphide, phos­
phorous, oil and grease (OG), turbidity, color and conductivity. Physi­
cochemical parameters were measured according to the Standard 
Methods for Examination of Water and Wastewater [37]; sludge filter­
ability was measured according to the Kubota recommended method 
(Filter Test — FT), in which 50 mL of sludge are filtered in a filter paper 
(Whatman 42 185 mm) folded with pleats with the aid of a simple funnel 
and the volume filtered during the initial five min is recorded as the 
sludge filterability (mL 5 min−1); and membrane permeability was 
calculated by dividing the measured permeate flow by the membrane 
area and by the pressure applied to the system.
Table 2 presents the MBR performance regarding pollutants removal. 
The removal efficiencies observed are in accordance with which is re­
ported for the treatment of oil refinery wastewater using pilot-scale MBR 
[38,39].
2.2. Database and preliminary statistical analysis
The monitoring data was provided by the oil refinery and consisted 
originally of 1131 samples, collected during the four years of moni­
toring. Each sample refers to a day and covers a set of 30 variables, 
specifically: TMP, temperature, permeate flowrate, membrane perme­
ability, SRT, sludge filterability, sedimentability, MLVSS, MLSS, EPS, 
SMP, pH, influent and effluent TOC, BOD, COD, ammonia, turbidity and 
alkalinity, effluent chloride, color and conductivity, and influent sul­
phide, phosphorous and OG. Since samples with missing data cannot be 
used to build multivariate models, some variables were selected aiming 
to obtain relevant information on both MBR treatment mechanisms and 
to keep the greatest possible number of samples. The variables selected 
were SRT, sludge filterability, MLVSS, pH, temperature, membrane 
permeability, influent concentrations of COD, phosphorous, ammonia, 
sulphides and OG, effluent concentration of COD, COD removal and 
ammonia removal. Alkalinity is an important variable for investigating 
ammonia removal, since it plays an important role in nitrification. 
However, it was not measured with a high enough frequency to feasible 
its use on the model.
Since even these variables were not measured with the same fre­
quency, the dataset still presented several missing data and, therefore, 
samples that did not contain measurements for all the selected variables 
were removed from the database. In addition, outliers were investigated 
using both interquartile range (IQR) [40] and Hampel identifier 
methods [41]. To avoid distortion in the results though, all occurrences 
were individually examined with the report of operation provided by the 
oil refinery along with the monitoring data to check if any operational 
problem could justify the extreme values. It was concluded that all ob­
servations classified as outliers should be kept in the dataset, especially 
because they referred to atypical operating conditions and thus added 
important information about the process control. Hence, the final 
database consisted of 479 samples covering 14 variables. Table 3 pre­
sents the descriptive statistics for the final database.

2.3.1. ANN model development
The final dataset was preprocessed to have standard deviation one 
and mean zero, i.e. standardized to Z score, according to Eq. (1). This 
standardization reduces the large difference between the orders of 
magnitude of different variables and thus make them more comparable, 
giving equal importance to each one in the multivariate models [44]. 
Zi = (xi −μ)/σ
(1) 
where Zi represents the standardized score, xi represents a specific var­
iable, μ represents the mean of the set of variables, and σ represents the 
standard deviation of the set of variables.
Once the data were organized and standardized, the ANN model was 
built in R using NeuralNet [45] and Keras [46] packages. The variables 
sludge filterability, MLVSS, pH, influent concentrations of ammonia, 
OG, sulphides, phosphorous and COD, effluent COD, COD removal, 
temperature, SRT and membrane permeability were passed to the model 
as input variables and the variable ammonia removal was defined as the 
output, since the objective is to predict its value and assess its behavior. 
A multilayer feedforward architecture was applied, and several tests 
were performed with different network configurations, combining 
different settings of activation function, number of hidden layers, 
number of neurons in each layer and number of epochs, to identify the 
configuration that presented the smallest mean square error (MSE). 
Based on these tests, it was determined that the network configuration to 
be used for modelling the MBR would apply the rectifier with dropout 
activation function; contain two hidden layers, containing 12 and seven 
neurons, in that order; and 5000 epochs.
The model was used for two purposes: sensitivity analysis of the MBR 
ammonia removal capacity to the input variables; and forecasting of the 
removal achieved from a set of input conditions. So, after importing the 
data, it was randomly divided into three sets: training, with 60 % of data; 
validation, with 30 % of data; and test, with 10 % of data. Zhong et al. 
[18] highlight at their review work the importance of proper model 
development and interpretation to deliver meaningful results when 
applying ML to Environmental Science and Engineering (ESE). The au­
thors stated that “more ‘representative’ data rather than ‘big’ data are 
more important for obtaining robust, powerful ML models”. Aiming to 
ensure that the training set was truly representative of the actual 
behavior of the system and that different ammonia removal conditions 
were addressed thus, the division of the sets was verified and repeated 
until the training set covered a wide range of ammonia removal per­
centages, so that the model could learn its behavior under different 
conditions. For the sensitivity analysis, the training and validation 
subsets were used to train and calibrate the network and for the 
ammonia removal prediction, the test subset was used as input 
configuration.
The model performance was evaluated through the statistical pa­
rameters coefficient of determination (R2), mean absolute error (MAE) 
and MSE.
2.3.2. PCA model development
PCA was performed to further explore the relations between the 
variables and the ammonia removal achieved, specially to analyze 
whether the correlations indicated by the ANN model were positive or 
negative. PCA achieves dimensionality reduction by orthogonal trans­
formation, i.e. the original vector group is transformed into a new vector 
group that is a linear combination of the original one. The imple­
mentation principle is shown in Eq. (2) [14–16]. 
Pi = Zi*cov
(2) 
where Zi represents the standardized numerical matrix before PCA, cov is 
the covariance matrix of Zi, and Pi is the numerical matrix after PCA.
PCA was computed using the PCA function found in the FactoMineR 
package [47]. The data was previously standardized to Z score (Eq. 1) 
and loading plots were built using the factoextra [48], ggplot2 [49] and 
ggpubr packages [50]. The number of components to keep was deter­
mined based on Kaiser criterion [51] and the scree test [52]. The quality 
of the model was also evaluated from its R2 value.
2.3.3. MSPC model development
Multivariate control charts were built based on a latent-variable 
based approach for MSPC, using PCA as statistical projection method 
(MSPC-PCA), as presented by Ferrer [53] and adopted by Taghezouit 
et al. [35]. The number of components to keep in the PCA model was 
again determined based on Kaiser criterion and the scree test. The out- 
of-control criteria applied was a minimum of 90 % of ammonia 
removal and therefore the dataset was split into two groups: Phase I 
contained only in-control (removal greater than 90 %) samples from the 
first three years of monitoring (n = 272); and Phase II contained all 
samples from the last year of monitoring, both in and out-of-control (n 
= 150). Phase I dataset was standardized to Z score (Eq. 1) and then used 
to develop the PCA model from which T2 and Q complementary statistics 
were derived for each observation. From the two statistics then, the 
respective multivariate control charts were built, assuming as upper 
control limits (UCL) the respective 95 % percentiles.
Next, phase II dataset was standardized to Z score (Eq. 1) and pro­
jected onto the PCA model developed so that T2 and Q statistics were 
calculated and plotted on the control charts to be checked against the 
UCL. For the observations detected to be beyond UCL on the Q-statistic 
control chart, a diagnostic approach based on contribution plots was 
performed. The contribution of each variable j for each abnormal 
observation i was calculated according to Eq. 3 [54]:

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Comprehending and predicting ammonia removal on MBR systems
3.1.1. Identification of the most influential variables on ammonia removal
Both ANN and PCA models developed were applied to identify the 
key factors affecting ammonia removal in the MBR. Fig. 3 ilustrates the 
ANN model architecture, presenting its layers and synaptic weights. The 
black lines show the connections between each layer and the weights on 
each connection while the blue lines show the bias term added in each 
step. The model performed well modelling the MBR behavior, with 
reasonably low values of MAE (order of magnitude 10−2) and MSE 
(order of magnitude 10−4), as shown in Fig. 4. The graphs show the 
learning process and evolution of the ANN model during the training 
and validation stages. It is possible to notice a considerable decline of 
both MAE and MSE, indicating that the model is adjusting to the data 
and learning its patterns.
Fig. 5a displays the scree plot for the PCA model. According to Kaiser 
criterion, PC with eigenvalues greater than one should be kept [51] and 
according to the scree test one should keep the PC before the elbow point 
in the scree plot [52]. The graph shows that the first three PC met both 
criteria. Besides, it is noticeable that adding more PC after the third one 
is not meaningful, since R2 values do not increase enough to justify the 
higher complexity of the model, as shown in Fig. 5b. For simplicity 
purposes, only the first ten PC are displayed on the graphs. Considering 
that PCA main goal is precisely to reduce data dimensionality, the fewer 
components kept, the better, as long as the loss of information is 
acceptable. Nevertheless, after three PC the model explained 55 % of the 
data variation, which is enough to explore the strongest relations be­
tween the different variables [57], and presented satisfactory perfor­
mance, with R2 equal to 0.772, which is regarded as good, according to 
Eriksson et al. [58].
Fig. 6 displays the sensitivity analysis accomplished with the ANN 
model, indicating the ranking of relative importance for ammonia 
removal. For simplicity purposes, only the ten most important variables 
are displayed since the relative importance of the others was too low. 
Additionally, Fig. 7 displays the loading plot obtained with PCA model, 
from which it is possible to understand how the variables are correlated 
to each other (indicated by the angles between the arrows: 90◦for un­
correlated, 0◦for complete positively correlated, and 180◦for complete 
negatively correlated variables).
According to both models, the variables with the greatest positive 
impact on ammonia removal are SRT, operating temperature and 
influent ammonia concentration. Conversely, the most substantial 
negative impacts are attributed to influent COD, oil and grease (OG) 
concentration, and membrane permeability. Zhang, Chen and Jiang 
[14–16] also applied ANN modelling to assess the removal efficiency of 
ammonia in wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) applying different 
biological processes. The removal efficiencies of WWTP were success­
fully predicted by the model, which considered five variables: inlet flow 
rate, pH, influent ammonia concentration, COD and total phosphorus 
concentration. According to their results, influent COD concentration 
was the main influential factor, similar to the results found here; how­
ever, the influent ammonia concentration did not greatly affect its 
removal, conversely to what was observed in our work.
The strong influence of influent ammonia concentration observed 
can be explained though by the fact that high ammonia concentrations 
accelerate the kinetics of nitrification reaction [59], besides increasing 
the nitrifying bacteria growth rate, according to Monod's relation. For 
these reasons, higher concentrations of ammonia in the MBR feed 
contribute to increased oxidized ammonia load [60]. Besides, the 
growth rate of nitrifying bacteria also increases exponentially with 
temperature [61], which can explain the great influence of this variable

correlation between ammonia removal and pH, since the reaction gen­

As for SRT, one of the main advantages of MBR over CAS technology 
is the independency of SRT from HRT, which allows to adopt higher SRT 
on MBR systems than usual in CAS. The higher SRT, in turns, allows a 
better sludge acclimatization, hence a greater capacity to remove pol­
lutants [8]. Additionally, the complete retention of the biomass by the 
membrane ensures a greater diversity of microorganisms in the biolog­
ical tank, which also contributes to the higher removal of different 
pollutants [63]. Furthermore, since the nitrifying bacteria's growth rate 
is considerably lower than the heterotrophic bacteria's one, higher SRT 
are needed to ensure the development of the nitrifying bacteria before 
they are washed out of the system [59]. All these factors explain the 
strong positive correlation between SRT and ammonia removal, as 
previously observed in other works [64].
The strong negative impact of influent COD is also related to the 
influence that this parameter has on the microbial community. High 
values of influent COD on biological treatment systems and, conse­
quently, high C/N ratio values, provokes a greater growth of hetero­
trophic bacteria and result in competition for substrate and DO, 
prejudicing the growth of nitrifying bacteria [60]. Sepehri and Sarraf­
zadeh [65] assessed the effect of nitrifying community on nitrification 
efficiency on MBR. The authors obtained a nitrifying-enriched activated 
sludge (NAS) through particular ammonia feeding of CAS. NAS and CAS 
were then compared and the results demonstrated the higher nitrifica­
tion efficiency of NAS, indicated by both ammonia removal percentage 
(100 % on NAS vs. 43 % on CAS) and nitrate concentration produced 
(6.6 mg L−1 on CAS vs. 37.5 mg L−1 on NAS). Brasil et al. [66] also 
demonstrated that lower C/N ratios (and higher temperatures) favor 
nitrifying bacteria and, consequently, improve ammonia removal. Thus, 
for the MBR assessed in this study, which operates under a high influent 
C/N ratio (20.0 ± 11.1), the ammonia removal is strongly affected by 
influent COD, as revealed by the models.
Higher influent OG concentrations also negatively influence on 
ammonia removal, due to its toxic effect on nitrifying bacteria meta­
bolism. Studies show that even at low concentrations, toxic compounds 
can inhibit the bacteria activity and compromise the ammonia removal 
[67]. Back in 2007, Qin et al. were evaluating the feasibility of MBR to 
treat oil refinery wastewater using a testing system that ran continuously 
over two months. The authors observed that, in the first two weeks of 
operation, the nitrification efficiency was low, as the ammonia removal 
was only 20–40 %, and the high OG levels (53–153 mg L−1) in the feed 
during this period were indicated as the principal reason. The authors 
also observed that nitrification was better when influent OG concen­
tration was below 20 mg L−1. OG concentrations on the assessed MBR 
were up to 48 mg L−1, its inhibitory effect can be related to lower 
ammonia removals.
Since high membrane permeability decreases HRT, its negative 
impact on ammonia removal can be explained by the shorter time 
available for its degradation, which leads to insufficient time for nitrifier 
oxidize the available ammonia. Song et al. [68] demonstrated that when 
HRT decreased below a certain level (6.5 days), insufficient nitrification 
caused a decrease in the overall nitrogen removal. Furthermore, recent 
works suggest that HRT is also an important factor for the development 
of the microbial community in biological treatment systems. Ni et al. 
[69] studied the effects of shortened HRT on the microbial community of 
MBR equipped with different membrane pore size (0.40 or 0.05 μm), 
operated at 25 ◦C and fed with domestic wastewater. The changes 
observed in the microbial community in each reactor were consistent 
with HRT changes, indicating that it could be an important factor for 
maintaining microbial community structures.
The less relevant impact of MLVSS also draws attention as it indicates 
that for greater ammonia removal, it is not enough to ensure a high 
concentration of sludge. Indeed, the most important factor is having 
enough ammonia-oxidizing microorganisms in the system. This result 
endorses studies that state that MLVSS is not an appropriate indicator of 
biomass physiological activity [70,71]. Hence, finding more suitable 
parameters to account for the concentration of nitrifying bacteria on 
MBR is of extreme interest. Sepehri and Sarrafzadeh [65] also concluded 
in their previously presented work that NAS was twice as filterable 
compared to CAS. This means that higher sludge filterability values can 
be related to higher concentrations of nitrifying bacteria and, therefore, 
to higher ammonia removals. This effect can be observed on PCA and 
ANN models from the impact that sludge filterability exerts on ammonia 
removal.

3.1.2. Ammonia removal prediction
ANN model was also applied to predict the ammonia removal ach­
ieved by the MBR from a set of input conditions. The correlation be­
tween the real (measured) and the predicted (by the model) values are 
shown in Fig. 8. As expected, due to the high performance of the ANN 
model (low values of MAE and MSE), the provisions were adequately 
assertive, with R2 value equals to 0.868, indicating that the error be­
tween the values predicted by the model and the actual measured values 
was low. This demonstrates that the ANN model was effective in pre­
dicting the behavior of the MBR regarding ammonia removal.
This result is of great interest because, despite being generated from 
offline monitoring data, it demonstrates the potential of ANN modelling 
to be used for online monitoring of MBR wastewater treatment systems. 
Due to its high predictive ability, new samples can be predicted by the 
model to evaluate if the system is performing as its expected to do or if 
there is some operating problem. By promoting a provision of data in 
real time, the ANN model could support more assertive decision-making, 
improving the monitoring and control of the process and contributing to 
more efficient operations.
3.2. Controlling ammonia removal on MBR systems
3.2.1. Detecting low ammonia removal conditions
In order to monitor the MBR performance regarding ammonia 
removal capacity along time and to detect any unusual event that lead to 
low percentages of removal, a MSPC-PCA model was applied to the 
database. The PCA model used as projection method kept three PC, 
according to both Kaiser criterion and the scree test. The three PC 
accounted for over 50 % of data variation. Fig. 9 presents the T2 and Q 
multivariate control charts, as well as the values of ammonia removal 
percentage of each Phase II sample.
T2 control chart did not performed well in detecting low ammonia 
removal operation and presented a detection rate of samples with 
removal percentages below 90 % of only 11 %. T2 control chart checks if 
a new observation projects on the PC hyperplane within the limits 
determined by the reference data. Thereby, a value of this statistic 
exceeding the control limits indicate that the corresponding observation 
presents abnormal values in some of its original variables, even though it 
maintains the correlation structure between the variables in the model 
(this observation is tagged as an outlier inside the PCA model) [53,72]. 
This way, the low detection rate can have been caused by some breakage 
of the model correlation structure in the out-of-control samples. Q 
control chart, in turn, checks the occurrence of any new events that 
cause the process to move away from the hyperplane defined by the 
reference model. This way, it was able to detect 54 % of the operation 
with ammonia removal percentages below 90 % (outliers outside the 
model).
Besides detecting over 50 % of the operation with less than 90 % of 
ammonia removal, Q control chart detected all operating points with 
removals below 85 %. This is very meaningful for the control of oper­
ation point of view, since this early detection can prevent the level of 
ammonia removal from reaching extremely low values, guiding the most 
appropriate times to act on the system. Furthermore, this result indicates 
that with better adjustments in the model or more representative input 
data, the detection rate of operation with ammonia removal percentages 
lower than 90 % could be improved.
Nevertheless, both statistics had a low percentage of false alarms (3 
% in T2 chart and 1 % in Q chart), considering that these errors are 
inevitable due to the very probability of the statistics (5 %). Therefore, Q 
control chart proved to be an interesting tool for the monitoring and 
control of ammonia removal on MBR, as it can detect its reductions. 
MSPC application can thus enable preventive and more assertive acting 
on the system, avoiding unnecessary costs, increasing treated water 
quality and, consequently, improving MBR efficiency.
3.2.2. Diagnosing low ammonia removal conditions
Since Q-statistic performed better on the detection of low ammonia 
removal operation, it was used to build a contribution plot aiming to 
investigate what caused the low ammonia removals observed during the 
MBR operation monitoring (Fig. 10). Similar to sensitivity analysis re­
sults, only the ten most important variables are displayed for simplicity 
purposes.
From the plot it can be noticed that the variables with greater con­
tributions to the exceeding values of Q-statistics were influent concen­
tration of COD, SRT and temperature, which indicates that these three 
variables influenced the most on ammonia removal reduction, endorsing 
the results obtained with PCA and ANN models. This also confirms the 
critical role that C/N ratio plays on ammonia removal control, as well as 
demonstrates the importance of keeping the system operating under 
proper values of temperature and SRT, since high values of influent COD 
and low SRT and temperature values were the most common causes of 
low percentages of ammonia removal by the MBR. Studying the asso­
ciation of these different factors in order to find a balance between them 
is also highly interesting, since, in practice, it is not always possible to 
keep all of them within the desirable range. Stewart et al. [73], for 
example, assessed the performance of two pilot-scale biological nutrient 
removal (BNR) treatment trains and observed that increasing the SRT 
during the period of cold water temperatures helped maintain ammonia 
removal performance.

4. Conclusion
For a pilot-scale MBR applied for the treatment of a real oil refinery 
wastewater, ANN, PCA and MSPC have proven to be suitable for 
monitoring the wastewater treatment system aiming ammonia removal 
better understanding and control. PCA and ANN modelling were effec­
tive in mapping the MBR behavior regarding ammonia removal and to 
detect the most important factors that impact on the pollutant removal, 
both positive and negatively. The models identified SRT, temperature 
and influent concentration of ammonia as the variables that improve the 
most ammonia removal, whereas influent concentration of COD and OG 
and membrane permeability are the variables that decrease it the most. 
ANN model was also successfully applied to predict the ammonia 
removal, with R2 equals to 0.868. Therefore, the error between the 
values predicted by the model and the actual measured values was low, 
demonstrating that ANN was effective in predicting the MBR behavior 
and thus could be used to forecast ammonia removals from a set of
